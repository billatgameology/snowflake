# E07 — A crystal remembers—but not perfectly

English production authority, 2026-09-20. The maker authorized the complete E07/E09 batch and explicitly waived a draft or maker-review gate. Technical, source and performance checks remain required; their scope is recorded in the episode review.

**Question:** What can a finished crystal tell us about how it grew?

**Takeaway:** New growth can preserve earlier material and respond to shared changing conditions, but a final shape neither gives its own clock nor uniquely reconstructs the weather. Local variation, internal growth behaviour, later alteration and selection matter.

**Established prerequisites:** E06 taught face-normal addition, conditional habit maps and seed/history limits; E05 taught branching feedback; E02 established related crystal directions. No factorial, twin geometry or instrumental measurement is assumed.

No source photograph is served. The opening library choice follows the named-crystal catalog and the existing served-recording provenance; it is not the published sandwich-model run.

## E07-01 — A shape with a past

### Narration

Look at this little column with a plate spreading from each end. It looks like a tiny cotton reel. This is a computer-grown example from our library, not a natural specimen. How did one crystal end up with both a long middle and wide, flat ends? A map of constant growing conditions gives us a starting point, but a cloud can change around a growing crystal.

Now switch to a simplified drawing and follow one possible growth story. Start with a small prism, then let it grow into a column. The darker ice marks material already there. As new ice joins, that older material stays inside our example.

A cloud carries a crystal through changing temperature and water supply. The next ice can grow differently from the ice that came before it. That gives a crystal a kind of memory. But how much of the journey can we really recover from the finished shape?

## E07-03 — Six arms in shared air

### Narration

Now consider a different crystal: a six-armed star. All six arms travel together, separated by only a small distance. When the surrounding air changes across the whole crystal, the arms can experience nearly the same change at nearly the same time.

Watch one shared change, then follow the fresh growth on one arm. The new part broadens. Now look around the other arms: a similar change can appear on each. One arm hasn't sent the others a drawing to copy. The ice structure gives related directions, and their shared surroundings give related growing conditions.

This is a way to make a complicated pattern with an overall sixfold resemblance. It doesn't require the arms to be isolated from one another. They draw water from the surrounding air and can affect that shared supply. Shared conditions explain a family resemblance, not a guarantee of perfect copies.

## E07-04 — Look closer than the outline

### Narration

Keep the same star and look closer at two neighbouring arms. Their broad changes resemble one another. But this little sidebranch is longer on one arm, and that small branch is missing from the other. Even the two sides of one arm needn't match.

A small local disturbance can be amplified by the branching feedback we followed earlier. The air needn't be perfectly uniform, and a nearby crystal can draw down the supply on one side. So a shared journey can coexist with different details.

A laboratory can deliberately make large, abrupt changes around a crystal. Those common changes can produce particularly well-matched features. Under less strongly imposed changes, smaller differences can stand out more. The resemblance and the mismatch are both part of what we need to explain.

Retain same star; select two adjacent arm sectors with leaders to side-by-side magnified copies, preserving their actual chosen unequal branch pattern. Show one localized disturbance and growth, not a new global condition change. Finish with separate common input pulses and matched major bands. Sources: Libbrecht pp. 83–84, 358. Schematic, no counted probability.

## E07-05 — Distance is not a clock

### Narration

Suppose we follow one arm from the centre towards its tip. In this simple outward-growth story, we meet earlier growth before later growth. But equal distances don't have to represent equal amounts of time.

Here's a chosen example with two equal time intervals. In the first interval the tip advances a short distance. In the second it advances farther. The same time has made different lengths because the growth rates were different. Without those rates, the finished arm doesn't tell us how long either part took.

Nor is every visible mark a separate weather event. Some surface ridges can develop during steady growth. Later sublimation, melting, collisions or frozen droplets can also alter the record. The useful idea of memory needs both a growth mechanism and a limit.

## E07-06 — A change without changed controls

### Narration

There is a sharper warning from a published computer model. This is a drawing of that reported result, not a recording of a real crystal. The model lets vapour spread and adds ice using direction-dependent rules. Its adjustable rules are not a direct measurement of the molecular surface.

Keep the model's outside controls fixed. A plate grows for a while, then its rim develops into two spreading layers with an open space between them. The old centre remains while the new growth takes a different form. If you only saw the final change in shape, would that prove the outside controls had changed?

No: they stayed fixed in this model run. The change arose as the model crystal developed. That doesn't prove the same mechanism made a particular natural specimen. It does show why a convincing-looking boundary is not, by itself, proof of a weather event.

## E07-07 — What does alike mean?

### Narration

So what about the saying that no two snowflakes are alike? First decide how closely we are looking. Two simple hexagonal plates can look alike under an optical microscope. A complicated branching crystal has many more visible details that could differ.

Now consider two separate laboratory crystals growing side by side. Give both a common schedule of strong changes in their surroundings. Experiments have produced remarkably similar shapes that way. Look closely, though, and differences remain. Sharing the schedule doesn't make every local detail identical.

Matching all the details of two elaborate natural crystals would be extraordinarily unlikely. That is a useful statement about complexity, rather than a rule forbidding simple lookalikes. And looking alike is different from having every molecule, isotope and defect in an identical position. A photograph cannot check that stronger kind of sameness.

### Visual and sources

Two plain plates first, equal optical silhouettes; introduce two new elaborate laboratory schematic stars under a shared schedule. Add visibly related coarse features and retain selected differences. Do not label identical or imply photographic evidence. Final zoom lens icon indicates observational scale; no factorial number promoted to exact probability. Sources Libbrecht pp. 42–44, figures 1.28–1.29, pp. 358, 383.

## E07-08 — The crystals a portrait leaves out

### Narration

One more trap comes from which crystals we choose to show. A gallery of beautiful six-armed stars is a selection, not a sample of everything falling from a cloud. Natural snow also includes simple forms, uneven crystals, clusters and ice coated with frozen droplets.

Here is a cluster with several crystal orientations. It needn't present one tidy six-armed outline. Here is a plate with a triangular outline. It can still be a single crystal of ordinary hexagonal ice. How such unusual outlines develop is a test for later explanations.

The mix you collect depends on the cloud, where you sample it, and what survives the journey. Our examples are a selection too, not a measured census. A good explanation has to face the ordinary and awkward crystals as well as the photogenic ones.

## E07-09 — From a plausible story to a test

### Narration

We can now read a shape more carefully. A retained column and later spreading caps suggest an order of growth. Related patterns on several arms can reflect shared conditions. Unequal details remind us that the same broad history needn't make exact copies.

But a final shape doesn't provide its own clock, or one unique set of weather readings. Some changes can come from the growing crystal itself, and later events can remove or cover earlier features. The crystal is evidence to interpret, not a complete written log.

To go further, we need to watch growth while keeping track of the conditions. How much new ice appeared, and how long did it take? How do we measure something so small without changing the way it grows? That takes us from reading a finished crystal to measuring one as it grows.

## Reader depth retained, not discarded

### Reading a capped column without inventing a thermometer

A capped column has a columnar middle and plate-like growth at its ends. Libbrecht’s figure 1.4 describes a familiar interpretation: initial column growth near −5 °C followed by plate growth after movement to a colder location. Our drawing constructs that route explicitly, preserving the original column and adding the caps beyond its ends. It is a qualitative growth construction, not a numerical simulation or a measured trajectory.

That construction does not license an exact inversion for an unknown specimen. The empirical habit map depends on supply, pressure, seed, preparation, size and observation method. Even a plausible ordering does not provide exact temperature values, times, vertical distances or the unique path through a cloud. A crystal can also lose material by sublimation or melting, collect droplets, join an aggregate, or break. The preserved-core example deliberately excludes those later changes; it does not assert that all old ice survives.

Sources: `docs/education/chapters/08-a-snowflake-is-a-record.html#reading-a-capped-column`, `docs/education/chapters/08-a-snowflake-is-a-record.html#what-the-record-does-not-say`, `docs/education/chapters/01-not-a-frozen-raindrop.html#two-words`, [Primary source](https://arxiv.org/abs/1910.06389v2).

### Shared conditions, coupled supply and imperfect symmetry

The six main arms of a small stellar crystal occupy nearby air and share crystallographically related directions. Changes affecting the whole crystal can therefore generate related features on several arms. This does not mean that every arm receives identical local vapour concentration, or that arms are independent in a transport calculation: each consumes vapour from a shared surrounding field. A local gradient, neighbouring ice, uneven growth or a small disturbance can make the arms differ.

Libbrecht contrasts the coarse resemblance of a stellar dendrite with its poorly correlated fine sidebranch details (printed pp. 83–84). Large imposed changes can also stimulate more closely matched sidebranches. The common-schedule laboratory twins demonstrate substantial reproducibility under their preparation and growth conditions; their remaining differences prevent an exact-copy claim. We use explanatory drawings to separate the shared input from local differences, not to measure the probability or size of either effect.

Sources: `docs/education/chapters/08-a-snowflake-is-a-record.html#why-the-six-arms-match`, `docs/education/chapters/08-a-snowflake-is-a-record.html#where-the-symmetry-runs-out`, [Primary source](https://arxiv.org/abs/1910.06389v2).

### Why a length cannot supply an elapsed time

For a selected tip following a defined direction, the distance advanced equals the growth speed integrated over the time interval. If the speed is constant, the simple relation is distance = speed × elapsed time. If the speed changes, the same distance can take different amounts of time. The two equal time bars in the animation are invented teaching intervals, not measured seconds or equal segments extracted from a photograph.

Even establishing which feature formed first needs a growth model and a view of the relevant geometry. Ice can be added on broad faces as well as at branch tips; a surface ridge is not automatically the boundary of a time layer. Our centre-to-tip reading applies to the simplified outward-growing branch. It should not be extended into a universal age map for every point inside an arbitrary crystal.

### Ridges, ribs and a counterexample inside a model

A radial ridge and a ring-like rib need not encode the same process. The chapter describes radial surface ridges arising during steady plate growth, while a change in supply can help write a transverse feature. The observation of a line alone does not select its cause. A photograph may also leave uncertain whether the marking is on an outer face or between two plates.

Gravner and Griffeath’s published three-dimensional cellular model uses vapour diffusion and adjustable, direction-dependent mesoscopic attachment rules. In reported fixed-parameter runs, a plate can develop a sandwich structure: two thin spreading layers with space and markings between them. A delayed structural change could be mistaken for an external change if only the final shape were available. The episode draws that reported possibility; it does not replay the published run, validate the model, or claim its chosen parameters are measured temperature or humidity.

The model also reports seed sensitivity, needle-bearing forms and appearances resembling rime without a droplet process. Those results are warnings about interpretation within that model, not demonstrations that natural rime is vapour growth or that every capped column arose at fixed conditions. Its capped-column examples explicitly change parameters. Controlled observations would still be needed to decide which mechanism made a particular natural specimen.

Sources: `docs/education/chapters/08-a-snowflake-is-a-record.html#the-writing-on-the-surface`, `docs/education/chapters/08-a-snowflake-is-a-record.html#the-record-can-lie`, [Primary source](https://www.math.ucdavis.edu/static/research/infovault/gravner/Phy_Rev_E-79-2009.pdf).

### The useful version of no two alike

Sameness depends on the observation. Plain hexagonal plates can have essentially the same visible outline at an optical microscope’s resolution. Complex natural crystals have many more potentially different features, so a detailed match is extraordinarily unlikely. Neither claim is a census of all crystals ever formed. Laboratory crystals grown side by side under strongly imposed common changes can be strikingly similar while retaining differences.

Molecule-by-molecule identity asks a different question, including isotope positions and defects invisible in an ordinary photograph. Libbrecht’s book-ordering analogy illustrates the rapid growth of the number of arrangements as complexity increases. It is not an exact statistical model of snowflakes: features are correlated, not freely shuffled objects, and neither a precise feature count nor an exact match probability follows from a factorial. The narration therefore gives the qualitative complexity argument without presenting that analogy as measured odds.

Sources: `docs/education/chapters/08-a-snowflake-is-a-record.html#no-two-alike-precisely`, [Primary source](https://arxiv.org/abs/1910.06389v2).

### A population is more than a portrait collection

A snow crystal is a connected crystalline body, while a snowflake in everyday speech can be an aggregate of several crystals. Riming adds frozen cloud droplets, and sufficiently extensive riming can produce rounded graupel. A classification must state which objects it includes and how they were collected. Photographers, collectors and instruments impose different selection effects; a chosen gallery cannot establish the frequency of a habit in a cloud.

Chapter 9 retains the extended morphology lists, population studies, riming timescales and cold-cloud census. Their counts belong to their named conditions and collection methods, not a universal proportion of snow. Several orientations in one particle can produce bullet rosettes or other non-sixfold outlines. Crystal twins require a specific relationship between orientations; an arbitrary cluster is not automatically a twin. Detailed twin angles and uncommon pyramidal faces remain useful classification depth, but are not prerequisites for this history argument.

A triangular plate is another warning against confusing visible outline with molecular substance. We recognize that observed class here without deriving its mechanism or equating three visible sides with a new lattice. Episode 11 uses unusual forms as a test of explanations; Episode 8 first establishes how growth can actually be measured.
