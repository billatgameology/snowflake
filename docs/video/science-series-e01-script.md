## E01-01 — A hundred thousand droplets

### Narration

A large, star-shaped snow crystal can be built from the water in about a hundred thousand cloud droplets. But those droplets do not have to hit the crystal. They can disappear around it while the crystal grows.

Watch one droplet. It shrinks, releasing water into the air. That water travels as an invisible gas, then joins the ice. Liquid, vapour, solid. A cloud is being taken apart and assembled into something else.

The hundred thousand is an approximate example from snow-crystal physicist Kenneth Libbrecht, for one large stellar crystal. It is not a fixed price for every snowflake. The point is the surprising route: a great many tiny liquid drops can supply one much larger piece of ice without being collected as drops.

How can a droplet lose water in the very same cold air where a crystal gains it? Why has the liquid not simply frozen already? Follow those two questions, and the cloud becomes less like a white backdrop and more like the machinery that builds the snow.

## E01-02 — Four things that can fall

### Narration

First, separate a snow crystal from a frozen raindrop. Freeze a raindrop and you get an ice pellet. The water was already together as liquid before it became solid. That is not how a delicate snow crystal builds its arms.

The growth we are following adds water from the gas straight onto ice. That change has a name: deposition. Arriving molecules can become part of the solid. The elaborate structure grows by addition; it is not carved out of a larger frozen bead.

There are other routes, and you can sometimes see their results on the same piece of snow. A liquid cloud droplet can collide with a crystal and freeze onto it. That makes rime: a coating of little frozen beads. Add enough and the original shape can be buried in the soft, lumpy ice called graupel. That is genuinely collecting droplets, unlike our vapour relay.

Crystals can also collide with other crystals and stick together. A large fluffy snowflake may be a cluster of many separate crystals, not one enormous six-armed crystal.

So our question is narrower, and more interesting, than how water gets cold. How does one crystal acquire its structure from water arriving through the air? To answer it, we need to see what is hidden inside the cloud.

## E01-03 — What a cloud is hiding

### Narration

The white part of this cloud is not water vapour. Vapour is water in its gaseous state, invisible and mixed into the air. The visible mist contains liquid droplets. In the winter cloud we are following, liquid droplets and ice can be present together.

One way to make those droplets is to lift moist air. As the air rises, it expands and cools. The amount of vapour needed to balance liquid water becomes smaller. Eventually, water begins condensing onto tiny particles in the air, producing a cloud of drops.

Libbrecht describes typical cloud droplets only ten to twenty micrometres across: one or two hundredths of a millimetre. They are not little raindrops falling at the speed of rain. Their small size and air resistance let them remain suspended for long periods.

Between the drops is mostly air, with water vapour mixed among the other gases. The visible droplets are a reservoir. The invisible vapour is the form in which water can travel from that reservoir to a growing crystal.

Those two forms of water are easy to confuse because we cannot ordinarily see the journey between them. Magnify the cloud, keep both in view, and the first mystery becomes very precise. We have liquid droplets. We have cooled them below zero. Why are so many still liquid?

## E01-04 — The first piece of ice

### Narration

Below zero does not mean instant ice. A small droplet can remain liquid below its usual freezing point. It is then supercooled. The liquid has not become some intermediate kind of ice. It is still liquid water.

Freezing has to begin with a small ordered patch of ice. Starting that patch is called nucleation. A suitable surface can help the molecules get organised, but different particles help by different amounts. A particle that collected a droplet is not a guarantee that the droplet freezes as soon as it reaches zero.

That is why a cooling cloud does not switch from liquid to solid all at once. Some droplets freeze while their neighbours remain liquid. In the examples Libbrecht describes, very clean water droplets can remain liquid until nearly minus forty degrees Celsius. Certain bacterial proteins can help initiate freezing as warm as about minus two. Those examples show how strongly the starting conditions matter; they are not a recipe for assigning a freezing temperature to every atmospheric droplet.

Follow one droplet that does freeze. It is now a small piece of ice surrounded by liquid neighbours. This is the seed route we are using, not a claim that every ice particle begins in exactly this way.

The speck helped start the ice. It did not contain a drawing of the finished snow crystal. Most of the material, and all the growth we are about to follow, is still to come.

## E01-05 — Balance does not mean stillness

### Narration

Put that piece of ice in a sealed box with air around it, and hold the temperature steady. Even when it looks completely still, its surface is busy. Some water molecules leave the ice and enter the gas. Others arrive from the gas and stay on the ice.

Imagine beginning with very little water vapour in the box. More water leaves the ice than returns, so the ice shrinks and the vapour increases. As more molecules occupy the gas, more can arrive back at the surface. With enough ice to keep some solid present, the two rates eventually balance.

The ice then has no net growth, but the traffic has not stopped. Molecules still arrive. Molecules still leave. The balance value of the vapour at this temperature is called saturation with respect to ice.

Add more vapour without changing the temperature. Arrivals can now exceed departures, and the ice grows. Remove vapour, and departures can exceed arrivals: the ice shrinks. We have changed the outcome without changing how cold the box is.

This is why “the air is full” is the wrong picture. Saturation is not a maximum carrying capacity. It is a balance against a particular surface, at a particular temperature. More vapour than that balance value is supersaturation. For our growing ice, the useful question is not simply whether the air is humid. It is whether there is a surplus relative to ice.

## E01-06 — One cloud, two balance points

### Narration

Now place supercooled liquid water beside the ice, at the same temperature. The liquid also exchanges molecules with the vapour. But the two surfaces do not have the same balance point.

Below zero, the equilibrium vapour pressure over liquid water is higher than it is over ice. Water molecules escape more readily from the liquid. It therefore takes more vapour around the liquid to balance that outward traffic.

Take the minus fifteen degree example in Libbrecht's table. The balance pressure over ice is about one point six five millibar. Over supercooled liquid water it is about one point nine one. These are the pressures due to water vapour, not the pressure of all the air in the cloud.

Air balanced with the liquid therefore contains more vapour than the ice needs for balance. Compared with the ice value, the surplus is about sixteen percent. The same air can be at one hundred percent relative humidity with respect to water and still be supersaturated with respect to ice.

Nothing changed in the air when we changed the reference. We asked two different questions of it.

That small difference is enough to give our two neighbours opposite tendencies. In air between their balance values, the liquid loses water while the ice gains it. They do not need different temperatures. They need different surfaces.

## E01-07 — The relay, explained

### Narration

Return to the cloud. The liquid droplets exchange water with the air around them. When there are plenty of droplets, that exchange tends to keep the vapour close to the liquid-water balance value.

Our ice seed sees a surplus. Water joins it, and the ice grows. That removes vapour from its surroundings. Where the vapour falls below the liquid-water balance value, nearby droplets can lose more water than they receive. They evaporate, helping replenish the vapour being used by the ice.

Follow the handover slowly. A molecule leaves a liquid droplet. It joins the gas and moves through the air. If it reaches the crystal and becomes incorporated, it is now solid ice. The neighbouring droplets shrink while the crystal gains material. The transfer is net liquid to vapour to solid; the individual molecular traffic still goes both ways.

Now the opening number has a mechanism. About a hundred thousand small cloud droplets can supply the material for one large stellar crystal in Libbrecht's example. It is a material budget, not a count of impacts. The donor drops need not be glued together, and the crystal need not sweep through them like a net.

There is also an important limit to the picture. Water removed near the crystal has to be replaced through the surrounding air. It cannot teleport from reservoir to surface. How that journey affects different parts of the growing crystal will become a question in its own right.

## E01-08 — A percentage is not a supply

### Narration

There is a tempting prediction hiding in that comparison. As the temperature falls, the liquid-water balance value gets further above the ice value in percentage terms. Does colder therefore mean more material for a crystal to grow?

Not necessarily. The percentage is measured against a balance value that is itself getting smaller. Cooling lowers the equilibrium amount of water vapour over ice, and over liquid water too. A larger fraction of a smaller amount can still leave less water to use.

The table makes the contrast concrete. Air at the liquid-water balance value is about five percent above the ice value at minus five degrees. At minus fifteen it is about sixteen percent above. At minus forty the tabulated comparison is about forty-seven percent. But that last comparison describes equilibrium properties; it does not promise a real cloud full of liquid droplets at minus forty.

To ask about the supply, subtract the ice balance density from the water balance density and count the excess water per volume of air. That is an amount, not a percentage. Calculated from the saturation values, this excess rises, reaches a broad maximum near minus twelve degrees, then falls as it gets colder.

This does not predict a finished snowflake, or even its growth rate by itself. It tells us something simpler and essential: a bigger percentage on a humidity scale is not automatically a bigger supply of water.

## E01-09 — What runs out

### Narration

The reservoir can also change as the cloud develops. Some liquid droplets freeze. Others evaporate to feed the ice already present. So the population of liquid donors is not guaranteed to last.

In the cooling-cloud account Libbrecht describes, much of that liquid has disappeared by around minus twenty degrees. The crystals may already have used it, or it may have become ice. The particular droplet-to-vapour relay we have followed is then losing its supply.

That gives a physical meaning to the saying “too cold to snow,” but not a universal temperature at which snow becomes impossible. A threshold on a thermometer is not a complete description of a cloud. The source's story follows a reservoir being spent; it is not a rule that every cloud has the same history or that ice cannot grow at lower temperatures.

We have already seen why cold alone is not fuel. At lower temperatures the equilibrium vapour supply is smaller, and in this cloud the liquid supply can also become depleted. To understand what happens next, we need to know what water remains and whether it can reach the ice.

That is a different picture from rain turning solid on the way down. The snowfall has a history before anything reaches the ground. Its crystals have grown by drawing on a changing supply, and the supply leaves its own limits on what can happen.

## E01-10 — Water accounted for, shape still unexplained

### Narration

Return to the crystal we began with. Its material is no longer mysterious. A small piece of ice has grown by deposition. The surrounding droplets supplied water through vapour because liquid water and ice have different balance points. A large structure could be built without collecting all its donors as liquid drops.

But look at the result. We have explained where the water came from, not why it arranged itself into flat faces, six directions and branching arms.

The seed did not carry a picture of those branches. Nor was a larger lump carved down until only the pattern remained. The structure developed as water was added, with the growing surface and its surroundings determining what happened next.

There is a way to turn that idea into an experiment. In Libbrecht's laboratory, changing temperature and vapour supply during a crystal's growth changed the features that developed. The experimenter supplied a history of conditions, not a tiny stencil for each branch. We will return to those experiments when we can understand what the controls are changing.

For now, separate the two questions. Supplying enough water is one problem. Making a particular form from that water is another. A cloud can explain the delivery without telling us why ice chooses six directions in the first place.

That is where we go next: inside the solid. Before we can explain the branches, we need to find out what is repeating in the ice.
