# E10 — Can the edge change the rules?

English production source for the authorized final batch. Question: can the width and history of a growing facet change its effective surface response? Takeaway: narrow-facet growth challenges a description based only on broad faces; the proposed molecular feedback is useful but remains incomplete. Prerequisites are the face orientations, delivery problem and growth-to-response inference demonstrated in Episodes 6, 8 and 9. All moving diagrams are labelled explanatory or proposed; none is a digitization, molecular recording or validated forward simulation. The specific sources below retain their own experimental conditions and uncertainty. Two review/update rounds are required by the current plan.

## E10-01 — Keep the rim in view

### Narration

Keep the hollow column from our last question. Its opening is surrounded by a narrow, flat rim. Compare that strip with the broad end of a solid prism. Both are end faces of ice, but one gives a growing layer much less room. Should their surface responses be the same?

Now take a separate example: a very thin plate near minus fifteen degrees Celsius. Its broad top grows slowly while its narrow side rim advances outwards. The old centre stays thick; the new outer growth is thin. To explain that contrast, we need to know what happens at the rim.

Measurements on broad faces are our starting point. Applying their fitted responses to these extreme shapes leaves a serious discrepancy in the source model. A narrow facet may behave differently from a broad facet of the same orientation. That is a hypothesis we can investigate, not something the shape alone proves.

## E10-03 — What the experiment actually sees

### Narration

First separate the evidence from the proposed mechanism. In one set of experiments, thin plates grew from needle seeds in ordinary-pressure air. The camera followed the crystal dimensions over time. It did not see individual molecules moving around the edge.

Keep those observed changes in view. To estimate the surface response, the analysis used a broad face as a witness: it assumed that face's response was known, then worked backwards from its growth to estimate the local vapour surplus. With that estimate, the rapid rim growth implied a different effective response on the narrow side face.

Across temperatures, these model-based estimates showed a strong narrow-prism reduction near minus fourteen degrees. Separate hollow-column experiments inferred a narrow-end-face reduction near minus four. The measured growth supports a challenge to the broad-face description, but the barrier values depend on the model and its local-supply estimate. They are not readings from a barrier meter.

## E10-04 — More molecules on the same strip

### Narration

Here is a proposed microscopic explanation. A growing edge can remain sharper than the rounded shape favoured by surface energy. Molecules moving along that surface can be driven from the curved corners towards the flat strip. In our schematic, give the broad and narrow strips the same added corner supply. Which strip becomes more crowded?

The narrow strip. The same number spread over less area gives a larger surface density. As these arrivals collect, a new island can survive and spread. A more crowded terrace can start layers more readily. This drawing explains the proposal; it is not a molecular movie from the experiment.

Notice what did not change: the cost of making a step edge on that crystal plane. More surface molecules made successful layer starts more likely. A growth fit can represent that increase by lowering an effective barrier. The fitted barrier falls, even though this mechanism did not make each step edge cheaper.

These dots represent an added supply, not all equilibrium surface molecules or source-measured numbers. Show concentration before nucleation; one surviving island spreads more readily on the narrow lane. Retain identical short ledge markers as unchanged step-cost reference while the fitted response changes. Label the mechanism proposed throughout.

## E10-05 — A window, not a permanent switch

### Narration

The proposal also has to explain why the effect is strongest over a limited temperature range. Begin with a cold, ordered surface. A molecule can wander along a terrace, yet have difficulty crossing the lip of a step. In the proposed picture, that obstacle prevents the extra corner traffic from reaching the narrow strip.

Warm the same schematic surface. Disorder first softens the steps, so molecules can cross the lip and reach the strip. Now the added traffic can raise its surface density. Warm it further and a thicker disordered layer covers the surface. The simple picture of loose molecules crowding a rigid terrace no longer works in the same way, and the proposed enhancement weakens.

Blocked, enhanced, then weakened: that gives a reason to draw a dip in an effective barrier. It does not calculate where the dip belongs. The different temperature windows for the two face types were proposed using growth observations. Neither the exact onset nor the complete molecular mechanism has been established from first principles.

### Visual and sources

Use one fixed stepped cross-section across three successive states. Cold: a highlighted molecule travels to the step lip then returns. Middle: retain the same lip location, add localized disorder marks, move molecules across onto the narrow terrace, retain added crowd. Warm: extend an explicitly schematic disordered outer band while ordered bulk remains; replace distinct migrating dots with local disordered motions before reducing the enhancement marker. Build a small three-state trail only after each event. No invented temperatures or “melting entire ice” animation.

## E10-06 — A feedback that builds a thin edge

### Narration

Return to the growing plate, with its original thick centre still visible. Suppose its narrow side terrace starts layers more readily. The side advances faster. Under suitable diffusion-limited conditions, faster growth can sharpen the edge and bring the terrace steps closer together.

Follow one more turn of that proposed feedback: a narrower terrace, an easier layer start, faster outward growth, and a still narrower growing terrace. Each turn adds new ice at the outside. It does not flatten the old centre or remove material that was already there.

This is the proposed edge-sharpening instability. On a side rim it can build a thin plate; on an end rim, a related feedback can extend a hollow column. These are different face orientations and different temperature regimes. Ordinary branching also involves a delivery feedback, but a branch splitting does not by itself explain why a plate or column wall becomes so thin.

## E10-07 — The start and the surrounding gas matter

### Narration

That loop needs more than a surface rule. The proposal depends on growth being sufficiently limited by delivery through a background gas. Lowering the pressure makes transport easier. In near vacuum, the same sharpening feedback need not operate, and broad facets can hide a small edge contribution within a much larger interior.

History matters too. Consider two separate schematic growth histories near minus five degrees. One crystal starts gently and keeps broad end faces. The other first grows quickly and develops a narrow end feature. Now give them similar later conditions. Their different geometries have not disappeared.

The source interprets such histories using broad and narrow surface-response branches. It does not supply a universal pulse strength or a measured width at which every crystal must switch. Temperature and today's vapour supply are not a complete record of how a crystal got here. Keep the earlier growth with the later shape.

## E10-08 — Two crossing lines are not a crystal

### Narration

There is a tempting shortcut. Plot the two fitted barriers against temperature and mark where the lines cross. That crossing says the barrier inputs are equal. If we also assign equal overall scales and one shared positive local vapour surplus, the calculated surface responses are equal there. Can that crossing tell us where a whole crystal changes from a plate to a column?

No. First change the overall scale for just one face. Equal barriers now give unequal responses. Even with the full response formula, changing the chosen shared local supply can change where the response curves cross. The result belongs to that restricted calculation.

A real crystal has different local supplies around its top, sides and corners, and those supplies change as the shape grows. Follow incoming water to those different places, then let the boundary advance. To test a proposed habit transition, a model must solve that coupled growth problem and compare its resulting shape with observations. Counting crossings cannot replace that test.

## Reader depth retained, not discarded

### What is narrow, and how narrow is the evidence?

A prism facet is one of the six side orientations of a hexagonal ice prism; a basal facet is an end orientation. Width dependence compares broad and narrow patches of the same orientation. It is different from comparing basal with prism. A plate has broad basal faces and narrow prism rims; a hollow column can have narrow basal rims around its opening. The last molecular terrace, the edge's radius of curvature and the whole crystal's thickness are different lengths.

The monograph estimates the last-terrace width as w ≈ sqrt(8 R a), with molecular size a about 0.3 nm. With R around 1–2 micrometres, this gives about 49–69 nm. The often-quoted fifty-nanometre, fewer-than-two-hundred-molecule example is therefore a scale estimate, not a universally measured switch width. Likewise the roughly 1–2 micrometre edge scale discussed in the feedback proposal is not the terrace width. The episode's geometry and moving distances are schematic.

The surface-diffusion length near 100 nm and surface diffusion coefficient near 10^-7 square metres per second in the mechanism paper are rough estimates. The latter may be wrong by an order of magnitude or more. There is no measured universal threshold width that turns this proposed mechanism on. Narrowness, temperature, pressure, supply and growth history must remain attached to a claimed response.

### The two experiments and their witness assumptions

The narrow-prism study used needle-seeded crystals in filtered air at one bar and mapped the temperature dependence at a far-field ice-relative supersaturation of 32%. Strong diffusion prevented a meaningful direct measurement of the local surface supersaturation. The analysis treated broad basal kinetics as known, used that witness to infer the local supply, and principally constrained the ratio of the two responses. The paper allows possible factor-of-two systematic errors in inferred local supply for some morphologies and says the central witness assumption cannot be independently verified within the same experiment.

The resulting narrow-prism barrier reduction is centred approximately near −14 °C. Near the deepest region, the measurements do not fit the simple exponential form; the connecting dotted line is an eye guide. Neither a fabricated smooth dip nor a dot drawn by this website is an experimental point. The experiment's temperature map at one surrounding supply does not determine how the effect depends on supply generally. A separate run at 22% far-field supersaturation localized the minimum near −14.15 ± 0.15 °C; it did not supply a complete map of dependence on surrounding supply.

The basal study inferred a reduction near −4 °C from needle and hollow-column growth at surrounding supersaturation near 8%. It used a prism witness relation to estimate the local supply; corrections for slanted specimens and other model assumptions enter the result. The basal and prism studies support width-dependent effective response within their models, while not supplying a direct barrier measurement or unique microscopic explanation.

### Effective barrier, real step cost and the proposed temperature window

A useful broad-facet fit writes alphaHK = A exp(−sigma_0 / sigma_surf), where alphaHK is the inferred attachment response, A its overall scale, sigma_0 the fitted barrier and sigma_surf the positive local excess vapour relative to equilibrium over ice. These are not direct collision counts. Nucleation theory relates layer-starting difficulty to the free-energy cost of a molecular step. In the proposed narrow-facet mechanism, increased surface molecule density changes the rate of successful nucleation without requiring the intrinsic cost of a step on that plane to decrease. A fitted effective sigma_0 can consequently fall.

The proposed cold-side restriction is a barrier to molecules crossing a terrace-step edge, commonly called the Ehrlich–Schwoebel barrier. Partial surface disorder may soften this obstacle before the whole surface develops a thicker quasi-liquid layer. At the warmer end, the simple crowding mechanism weakens or loses applicability. This gives a qualitative reason for a limited window; it does not derive the onset temperatures, exact width dependence or absolute enhancement from first principles. Ordered bulk remains solid throughout these surface changes.

The mechanism paper's illustrative estimate at local supersaturation around 1% and curvature radius near 100 nm behaves as though the local supersaturation doubled, equivalent within the exponential to halving an effective barrier. The narrow-prism inversion near −14 °C is closer to one tenth of the broad value. The half-versus-tenth comparison concerns remaining effective barrier, not two directly measured barrier readings, and does not establish that the proposed molecular route is unique.

Sources: `docs/education/chapters/12-why-the-shape-flips.html#narrow-facets`; `docs/education/chapters/12-why-the-shape-flips.html#how-sure`; [Mechanism proposal, pages 4, 7–11](https://arxiv.org/abs/2012.12916)

### Why the same later conditions need not erase history

The −5 °C study represents broad basal faces with A = 1 and sigma_0 about 0.7%, and narrow basal features with A = 1 and sigma_0 about 0.1%. It calls the narrow branch a rough approximation and allows intermediate behaviour depending on width. Early rapid growth can create a narrow feature that persists under similar later conditions. This is a proposed model interpretation of growth histories, not a measured universal pulse threshold or a rule that a single photograph identifies a branch.

The free-fall examples were selected for well-formed columnar growth, while blockier forms were rejected and the full population distribution was not retained. Substrate experiments had different possible interactions. Aspect ratio integrates past growth rather than directly measuring the current ratio of attachment responses. At high growth rates, heating near extremities can matter; in normal air, diffusion makes attachment inference difficult. These conditions limit a quantitative comparison even when the qualitative history effect is compelling.

The proposed sharpening feedback needs diffusion-limited growth to couple faster incorporation with changing edge geometry. Near vacuum therefore differs both in transport and in the broad geometry of specimens used to obtain clean surface measurements. A tiny edge contribution may be overwhelmed by the larger area of a broad face. This explains why absence of a dramatic broad-facet effect is not a direct rejection of every narrow-facet hypothesis, but also makes a controlled external test essential.

### What crossing calculations actually establish

Equality of the two fitted barriers implies equality of attachment coefficients only when the two prefactors are also equal and one shared positive local supersaturation is assigned. In general the equality condition is A_basal exp(−sigma_0,basal / sigma_surf) = A_prism exp(−sigma_0,prism / sigma_surf). Both A and the selected local supply affect the comparison. The barrier-only count therefore cannot limit the number of habit changes a three-dimensional model might produce.

Two published broad-facet parameterizations give one barrier-equality root near −8.4 °C or −10.9 °C, respectively. Restoring the prefactor in one parameterization gives three coefficient-order swaps over a narrow chosen shared-supply range, without adding narrow-facet dips. Those are properties of the formulas under restricted inputs, not simulated shape transitions. A real crystal requires different local fields around its changing surface, transport and heat assumptions, complete growth history, and an independently specified shape comparison.

A later analytic dip parameterization can also produce three barrier-equality roots. Its exact dip functions were chosen by hand; the logarithm convention requires interpretation, and the centres are not the equality roots. Choosing dip locations partly from the morphology diagram makes comparison with that same diagram in-sample. The episode deliberately does not convert root counts into plate/column labels or treat agreement in a count as a prediction. Testing the mechanism requires a forward result and evidence not used to choose its answer.
