# E05 — The moment a corner becomes a branch

English editorial/prototype draft, 2026-09-20. This follows the released E04 ending. The question is
why a small lead can grow into a branch rather than being corrected. The takeaway is a reinforcing
loop between shape, delivery through air and further growth, opposed and directed by surface
physics. Branching is distinct from the question of why the overall crystal is flat or long.

All prototype geometry is an explanatory drawing. It is neither measured growth nor a numerical
solution of the vapor field. Selected primary passages were checked in Libbrecht's *Snow Crystals*,
arXiv:1910.06389v2, printed pp. 80–85 and 102–107, with the primary 2002 dendrite paper checked online.
Chapter 6's stronger statements about universal instability, exact symmetry and invariant tip size
are qualified here. Exact performance timing, complete viewing, listening and audience comprehension
remain review steps. Three paragraphs per scene organize the prototype; they are not prescribed
runtime or equal-duration production cues.

## E05-01 — Can a small lead disappear?

### Narration

We left this corner just beginning to get ahead. The flat-face correction was no longer keeping up.
Before we follow that corner outward, let's make the difference easier to see. Here is an enlarged
drawing of a growing surface with a small raised patch. The marked ice is already there; new ice
will be added around it.

First, consider growth where the surface can still correct this difference. The neighboring regions
have enough opportunity to catch up. Hold the temperature and the supply farther away steady. As
more ice joins, will the raised patch become more prominent, or less?

Less prominent. The surrounding surface gains enough ice to reduce the difference in height. The
original raised patch hasn't been shaved off or melted away. It is now inside the larger crystal.
A little unevenness does not automatically become a branch. What matters is whether the growth
around it reduces that difference or increases it.

## E05-02 — A lead that builds on itself

### Narration

Now start again with the same raised patch, the same temperature and the same supply farther away.
This time, consider a surface whose correction is too weak to keep that patch in check. Growing ice
depletes the vapor beside it. The projection has better access to replacement water diffusing in
from the surrounding air. What happens if that advantage lets it add ice faster than its neighbors?

The projection grows farther out. That changes the shape of the growing boundary, which can give
it an even greater share of the arriving water. It gains more ice, reaches farther again, and the
difference grows. Follow the loop twice: shape changes delivery; delivery changes growth; growth
changes shape. The supply farther away has stayed steady throughout.

That is positive feedback: a change reinforces itself. Here it produces the branching instability.
Compare our two cases now. Both added ice, but one reduced the unevenness and the other amplified
it. On a real crystal, the competition depends on its size, growth conditions and surface response.
A branch can emerge quite suddenly; there isn't one humidity or crystal size that triggers every
snow crystal.

## E05-03 — Why the familiar six arms?

### Narration

Return to the whole hexagonal crystal. We followed one corner, but this simple plate has six. In
an evenly supplied setting, each corner begins in a similar position. Each can gain the same sort
of advantage. Let them grow, and the familiar six-arm arrangement starts to appear.

The branching feedback didn't invent the number six. The crystal already had a sixfold structure,
and its flat faces gave it six projecting corners. The branches inherit that arrangement. This is
a common route to a stellar snow crystal, not a requirement that every piece of growing ice produce
six identical arms at exactly the same moment.

We can now explain how the broad arrangement begins. But look at the end of one arm. If projections
keep gaining an advantage, why doesn't its point simply grow sharper and sharper, or keep splitting
into more points? Let's follow that one tip before adding the rest of the decoration.

## E05-04 — A growing tip can keep its shape

### Narration

Here is a drawing of a single branch growing into open space, under steady conditions. The tip moves
forward while the old ice stays put. In laboratory examples called free dendrites, the region near
the tip can keep roughly the same rounded shape as it advances. Free means it is growing clear of
nearby walls and competing crystals. Dendrite means a tree-like crystal.

Keep the first outline as a reference. Now compare it with the new tip. The branch is longer, but
the small rounded end looks much the same. A camera traveling alongside the tip would make that
end look almost stationary. The pattern persists because fresh ice is being added; a rigid needle
isn't sliding through the air.

This behavior needs more than the runaway loop. At very small scales, surface energy makes sharply
curved features harder to maintain. The rate at which water joins the surface matters too. For
snow dendrites growing from vapor, that surface response is especially important in selecting the
tip's scale and speed. Diffusion alone leaves several possible tips; it doesn't choose the one we
observe.

## E05-05 — Direction helps a tip persist

### Narration

The surface response also depends on direction within the crystal. We met that difference when
some faces grew slowly and remained flat. At a branch tip, a preference for certain directions
can help one advancing point persist. In a flat stellar dendrite, the favored branch directions
carry the sixfold arrangement of the ice.

To see why that matters, consider a model comparison. First, a tip with a strong directional
preference advances along one direction. Now consider a growth rule with no preferred direction.
The branching feedback still acts, but a protrusion on the tip can compete with the tip itself.
In simulations of this kind, repeated tip splitting produces tangled, seaweed-like patterns.

The point is that branching and directional order are different parts of the explanation. Removing
the preference doesn't remove the instability. It removes an influence that helps organize its
results. Our drawings illustrate that comparison; they haven't calculated it. Real snow-crystal
tips can split too, so directional preference is not a promise that splitting can never happen.

## E05-06 — A branch can grow branches

### Narration

Return to our single advancing arm. A little farther behind the tip, its sides are growing surfaces
too. Small unevenness there can be amplified by the same feedback. Follow one raised patch as it
extends into a sidebranch. Then follow another nearby. They don't need separate dust particles to
start them; a small fluctuation in growth can be enough.

These neighbors share a vapor supply. Suppose one gains a small lead. As it projects farther, it
can receive water from a wider region while its shorter neighbors remain more sheltered. The lead
can grow larger. Watch the short neighbor: it still adds ice, but the longer one pulls ahead.
Competition for delivery helps leave large branches beside stunted ones.

The two sides of an arm needn't begin with matching bumps. Growth can amplify different small
fluctuations on each side. There can be a characteristic spacing without every gap being equal.
That gives a fernlike arm its mixture of regular directions and uneven decoration.

## E05-08 — The resemblance has a limit

### Narration

On a sufficiently large fernlike crystal, a sidebranch can carry smaller sidebranches of its own.
The pattern partly repeats at smaller scales. That resemblance is why snow crystals are often
compared with fractals. Keep those few levels in view while we try a different kind of construction.

This is the mathematical Koch snowflake. Replace the middle of each straight segment with a small
triangular bump, then apply the same instruction again. In the ideal mathematical object, you can
repeat forever. The rule tells us exactly where every new corner belongs. It is a construction
rule, not a story of water diffusing through air.

A physical crystal has a finite size and finite growth features. Its branches don't repeat the same
shape down through endlessly smaller scales. Calling it roughly fractal describes a resemblance;
it doesn't explain the vapor supply, surface response or preferred directions. Those are the
processes that make the branching pattern grow.

## Reader depth retained, not discarded

### What a branching instability does—and does not—say

The important quantity in the first two scenes is the difference between a projection and its
surroundings, not merely the overall crystal size. A stable response reduces a small disturbance;
an unstable response amplifies it. Our first example reduces the height difference by adding more
ice around a projection, without removing the original ice. The second increases the difference
because the projection advances faster. Both are growth-only drawings. Their outlines and speeds
were chosen to expose the distinction; they do not solve the moving-boundary problem.

In the idealized diffusion-limited example in figure 3.6, the surface incorporates material readily
and lacks the directional attachment contrast that supports faceting. A small geometric
perturbation then has a reinforcing delivery advantage. Applying that statement to every feature
on real ice would omit stabilizing physics. Surface energy affects the equilibrium vapor condition
near a sharply curved interface; attachment kinetics and facet regulation also change how a
perturbation develops. The outcome depends on scale and conditions. The term Mullins–Sekerka
instability names the broader diffusion-driven instability; branching is one visible
consequence of that instability.

The companion surface model writes local normal speed as `v_n = alphaHK × v_kin × sigma_surf`.
`alphaHK` is the effective Hertz–Knudsen attachment coefficient, not a count of accepted cartoon
collisions and not the G–G boundary-mass threshold. `v_kin` is a temperature-dependent reference
speed. `sigma_surf` is the fractional vapor surplus immediately beside the surface. More delivery
can alter that surplus, while a changed surface response alters incorporation; the two must be
solved together. A dark or light patch of air alone does not measure the net flux into ice.

### Why the transition can be abrupt without a universal trigger

Episode 4 established the source's qualitative regulation mechanism: steps become more closely spaced
near the face center, compensating for its lower local vapor supply. In that formulation, the
center cannot increase its effective response without limit. Once the correction is insufficient,
the remaining geometric difference can feed further differential growth, so a small change in
conditions can lead to a conspicuous change in form. The monograph's figure 3.5 shows a crystal
sprouting corner branches as the applied supersaturation was increased.

This is not a transferable onset value. Size, surface response, external supply and the resulting
local field are coupled; the source explicitly calls calculation of exact onset nontrivial. The
rod-in-honey analogy in Chapter 6 illustrates loss of stability, but its mechanical tipping
threshold is not a snow-crystal threshold. In the growing crystal, the reinforcing change
is between geometry, water delivery and the addition of ice.

### A persistent tip and the selection problem

The ideal Ivantsov construction describes a parabolic tip advancing with constant shape and speed
under its specified diffusion problem. In three dimensions the ideal shape is a paraboloid of
revolution. A flat snow dendrite is not exactly that object. Its actual tip can be partly faceted,
and its sidebranches complicate the rest of the outline. The ideal solution is useful because it
relates forward speed and tip radius, but that relation alone admits a family of pairs. Choosing a
particular pair requires further surface physics: the selection problem.

For fernlike free dendrites near −15 °C, the monograph reports a roughly linear relation between
far-away supersaturation and measured forward speed. Its fit, using an assumed geometric factor,
yields a tip radius of about one micrometer, or one thousandth of a millimeter. The source says
low-resolution tip images are consistent with weak dependence of radius on supersaturation. That
is weaker than an independently resolved measurement of an unchanging one-micrometer tip. The
2002 primary paper develops the attachment-controlled interpretation and gives its approximations;
the monograph's printed p. 106 stresses that poorly known attachment kinetics leave the theory
under-constrained. A drawn circle is not a measurement, and weak dependence under those
conditions does not imply that humidity can never change a real tip.

Pressure, temperature, crystal form and nearby boundaries matter. The monograph describes finer
structures at higher pressure and a predicted inverse pressure dependence of the selected radius,
while noting that a direct test of that prediction had not been made to the author's knowledge
at the time of writing. That prediction remains distinct from an established measurement.
A characteristic dendrite radius is also not a universal minimum size for every edge, step or
surface feature on ice.

### What directions select, and what they leave undecided

Direction-dependent surface properties are called anisotropy. For vapor-grown snow crystals,
attachment kinetics are strongly anisotropic. They help preserve facets and organize stable
advancing dendrites. The monograph compares numerical examples with different anisotropy in
figure 3.26: weakening it produces more tip splitting, ending in seaweed-like structures when the
chosen model is isotropic. This establishes a model comparison, not an experiment in which the
molecular structure of real ice has been switched off. Real ice can show tip splitting in some
conditions, as the accompanying discussion records.

Branch orientations can follow crystallographic directions while the detailed sidebranch pattern
varies. Small fluctuations can be amplified and neighboring branches alter one another's access to
vapor. There may be a characteristic spacing, so random does not mean every spacing is equally
likely or that no scale exists. Common external perturbations can also stimulate more coordinated
branches. Neither precise sixfold copying nor compulsory mismatch at every location follows.
The symmetry of a mature crystal supports an inference about its earlier growth; it is not a unique
reconstruction of that history. Episode 7 will make that distinction explicit.

### Other dendrites, and the limited fractal comparison

Similar branching appears when other materials solidify, including the transparent organic
compound succinonitrile. In solidification from a melt, diffusion of the released heat can limit
growth, whereas this episode primarily follows delivery of water vapor through air. Similar-looking
branches do not establish identical growth rates or identical stabilization mechanisms. Ice
forming from liquid water also deserves a separate treatment from atmospheric vapor growth.

The Koch construction replaces each straight segment with four segments of one-third the original
length. After `n` rounds, a starting perimeter `P_0` becomes `P_n = P_0 × (4/3)^n`, which increases
without bound as the ideal mathematical repetition continues. The corresponding snowflake's area
approaches a finite limit. These are properties of a prescribed geometric object, not a model of
water supply or incorporation. Physical fernlike crystals can show a few nested levels and limited
statistical resemblance across scales. Finite size and physical growth scales stop the analogy
well before the mathematical infinite limit.

A crystal's habit means its overall form or proportions, such as plate-like or columnar. The
complexity of its edge is another description. A plate can be plain or strongly branched. Slow
basal growth helps confine a fernlike star to a thin plane, but explaining extreme thinness and its
variation with conditions needs additional surface physics. Episode 6 establishes those controlled
observations; Episode 10 examines the proposed role of narrow facets. Branching feedback alone
does not settle the plate-versus-column question.

## Coverage and outgoing contract

E05 adds the reinforcing feedback, the meaning of unstable versus corrected growth, inherited
branch directions, persistent but selected tips, sidebranch competition, imperfect detail and the
limits of fractal language. It does not assume percentage-supersaturation arithmetic, detailed
stacking or diffraction, fitted surface-response curves, or knowledge of an experimental apparatus.

E06 may assume that a plain plate and a branched star share a broadly flat habit, that branch
complexity is a separate observation from overall proportions, and that the audience wants a
controlled comparison of temperature and specified vapor supply. E06 must still teach the meaning
of its map axes and experimental conditions. E07 may build on the possibility of uneven details,
but must explain how shared changing conditions coordinate arms. Later scripts should cite these
performed concepts only after the corresponding visual/voice review, not merely because this draft
has been written.
