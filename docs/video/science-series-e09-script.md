## E09-01 — Start with the moving face

### Narration

We can now measure how quickly ice grows. But a slow face leaves us with two suspects: water might
be slow to reach it, or the surface might be slow to build that water into ice. How can an
experiment separate them?

Let us follow a specific example reported by Kenneth Libbrecht: a side face of a small ice prism,
at minus fifteen degrees Celsius, in air at twenty millibars. That pressure is about one fiftieth
of ordinary atmospheric pressure. The experiment records how far the face moves in a known time,
while the water supply is varied.

Distance and time give the observed growth speed. The chamber settings help describe the
surroundings, but do not directly measure the spare vapour beside the face. That missing link
matters because growing ice draws water out of its own neighbourhood.

### Visual and sources

Introduce one supported prism from the side, fixed old outline, observed face advance and a clock.
Only then reveal the -15 °C / 20 mbar condition strip. New ice extends outward while old material
and support stay fixed. The drawn specimen/advance are explanatory, not a reconstruction of the
source photograph or digitized data. End by placing the surrounding supply away from the face; no
live local-humidity meter.

## E09-02 — Find the surface part

### Narration

Keep that right-hand face and the line where it started. Water travels in from the surrounding gas, and growing ice leaves less vapour close to the face. A transport calculation estimates the extra vapour that remains above the ice balance level there. Low pressure makes this delivery problem smaller, but does not remove it.

Now make a calculated copy beside the experiment. Give it the same temperature, the same estimated local vapour surplus, and the same elapsed time. In this calculation, set the surface response to one: the reference case with no reduction from that factor. Its face advances farther than the measured face in our teaching comparison.

Divide the measured advance by the calculated reference advance over that same interval. That fraction is the inferred surface response, called the attachment coefficient. We inferred it from motion and a model; we did not count successful molecular collisions. Change the local vapour estimate and the reference changes, so the inferred fraction changes too.

End by shifting the calculated reference only when the local estimate changes; the measured displacement stays fixed.

## E09-03 — A step changes the question

### Narration

Select a patch on that face and tip it towards us. A small new island appears, then disappears before it can spread into a lasting layer. Without an existing step, this patch must wait for a new island that survives.

Now look at a separate patch with a ledge already winding around a fixed point. The ledge is the edge between two layer heights, not a line painted on flat ice. At the same low vapour surplus, which patch could keep adding ice without first starting a new island?

The surface with the continuing step. Follow the edge as it winds outwards: new layer area stays behind it, while an edge remains connected to the source. A screw dislocation, a defect through the crystal, can supply this kind of continuing step. That is a possible growth route, not a defect we have seen inside every crystal whose growth curve looks similar.

## E09-04 — Two jobs for a fitted curve

### Narration

Return to patches that have to start new layers. Keep the local water supply the same in this schematic comparison. On one patch, a surviving island spreads; on the other, more attempts disappear and less ice is added in the same time. The nucleation barrier describes this difficulty in getting a new layer started.

Now put that comparison on a graph: local extra vapour along the bottom, inferred response up the side. With a larger barrier, the model gives a smaller response at the same supply. As the extra vapour shrinks towards the ice balance level, the response falls smoothly rather than switching off at one sharp threshold.

There is also an overall scale: even where starting layers becomes easier, the response may stay below the reference value of one. Watch the whole curve move down while its barrier stays the same. The factor setting that scale is the prefactor. Researchers fit the growth observations with these parts together, after accounting for delivery; they do not read either part directly from a surface meter.

### Visual and sources

Start with two tilted physical patches under the same local supply and synchronized clocks. One surviving island spreads; more failed starts leave the second patch with less added area. Name the layer-starting barrier only after this. Keep small physical references while the graph appears; transfer the easy/hard comparison to two explicitly model curves. Hold local supply fixed for the vertical response comparison. After explaining an overall scale, move the whole response curve downward without changing its barrier. No invented measured point set and no absolute threshold.

## E09-05 — Change the supply, keep the fit

### Narration

Let us use the published fit for that prism face at minus fifteen degrees. Its barrier is three percent and its prefactor is one; what follows is a calculation, not a new measurement. Begin with a local vapour surplus of one percent. The pale group represents a hundred parts at ice balance; the extra bright part makes the one percent. The calculated response is about five hundredths, so the face advances only a short distance during our chosen interval.

Start the next calculation from the same original face. Add a second extra part: the local surplus is now two percent, with the temperature and fitted surface parameters unchanged. Would the calculated growth speed merely double?

It rises by more than twice. The response itself rises to about twenty-two hundredths, because starting layers becomes easier. Run the same clock again: twice the local surplus and a larger response together give about nine times the advance. This is one chosen comparison from one fitted relation, not a ninefold rule for all snow crystals.

## E09-06 — A fitted value has conditions

### Narration

Repeat this work for different temperatures and for the top and side faces, and you obtain a map of
fitted responses. Much of the broad-facet evidence comes from small crystals at low pressure, where
transport is easier to control. A smooth curve through those estimates is a useful summary, but it
cannot remove their uncertainties or extend their tested conditions by itself.

Near the melting point, fitted side-face prefactors can lie well below one. One possible influence
is surface premelting: the outermost region becomes less ordered while the bulk remains ice.
Changes in molecular motion there could affect incorporation, but this does not establish one
complete microscopic explanation.

Air and trace impurities can also change surface behaviour in some experiments. Separating those
effects from transport, heating and surface structure requires additional comparisons. A fitted
coefficient needs its temperature, pressure, geometry and preparation history attached.

## Reader depth retained, not discarded

### What the growth law does and does not count

The source writes the face-normal growth speed as v_n = alphaHK × v_kin(T) × sigma_surf. Here
sigma_surf = [c_surface − c_sat(T)] / c_sat(T) is a dimensionless ice-relative supersaturation at
the surface, not ambient relative humidity. v_kin is a temperature-dependent speed conversion based
on equilibrium vapour density, ice number density and molecular thermal motion. At fixed
temperature and local surplus, v_kin × sigma_surf is the kinetic reference speed and alphaHK = v_n
/ [v_kin × sigma_surf] is the effective response inferred under this description.

The measured observable is displacement over elapsed time, with optical calibration and geometric
interpretation. The near-surface vapour level generally requires a transport calculation or a
justified approximation. Its correction depends on crystal size and shape, pressure, surrounding
boundaries and uptake. A low-pressure experiment reduces gas-transport resistance; it does not
directly observe every near-surface molecule or make corrections unnecessary. Heat transfer and
substrate interactions can matter too.

The monograph sometimes motivates alphaHK as a sticking probability. In this episode the
measured-growth interpretation remains explicit: the coefficient summarizes net incorporation
relative to a specified kinetic relation, rather than a literal tally of accepted incident
molecules. Reciprocal exchange is already part of the net-growth description. At zero local
supersaturation the reference growth vanishes; dividing a zero growth speed by a zero reference
cannot determine the coefficient.

### Reproduce the calculated comparison

Libbrecht’s Snow Crystals, printed p. 144, Figure 4.4, describes a prism-facet growth example at
−15 °C in 20 mbar background air, fitted with alphaHK = exp(−sigma_0 / sigma_surf) and sigma_0 =
3%. The plotted measurements are growth speeds. The exponent uses the ratio of supersaturations in
the same units; 3% / 1% is 3, not 0.03. The unit prefactor is implicit in that printed relation.
The episode does not digitize the plotted speed points or call its schematic face displacement a
measured trace.

For chosen local sigma_surf = 1%, the fit gives alphaHK = exp(−3) = 0.049787. At 2%, it gives
exp(−1.5) = 0.223130. With temperature fixed, the speed ratio is [0.02 × exp(−1.5)] / [0.01 ×
exp(−3)] = 2 × exp(1.5) ≈ 8.963. The local supply doubles and the response rises by about 4.48, so
the two effects multiply. These are calculated values from one empirical relation; no absolute
speed or exact measured growth increment is invented.

### Barrier, prefactor and the limits of a compact formula

For nucleation-limited broad facets, a common fitted form is alphaHK = A × exp(−sigma_0 /
sigma_surf). At fixed positive sigma_surf, increasing the nucleation parameter sigma_0 reduces the
response; decreasing A scales the entire curve downward. As sigma_surf becomes large relative to
sigma_0, this mathematical form approaches A. That limit does not guarantee that one mechanism or
one parameter set remains valid to arbitrarily large vapour supplies. The prefactor and barrier can
be coupled in a fit, and a reported analysis may constrain the prefactor, for example to one, where
justified; they are not always two independently measured quantities.

Sigma_0 is expressed in the same supersaturation units as sigma_surf. It is a parameter controlling
the exponential suppression, not a measured step height or an absolute humidity threshold. In
classical terrace-nucleation theory it relates to the free energy of a molecular step, temperature
and molecular-scale geometric quantities. Inferring that step free energy from growth introduces
the assumptions of the theory; it is not a direct microscopic energy measurement.

Broad-facet parameter estimates are often summarized by smooth temperature functions. In the cited
CAK literature, the closed forms were chosen to represent available evidence conveniently.
Broad-facet inversions, interpolation curves, narrow-facet extensions and proposed molecular
explanations are different evidential layers. An acronym containing “comprehensive” does not
certify completeness. Coefficient crossings at chosen local conditions do not by themselves
determine a three-dimensional habit or prove that a model cannot form one.

### Steps already present: defects and slightly tilted faces

A screw dislocation can supply a continuing step as the edge winds around its source. Growth may
then proceed at local supersaturations too low for frequent new terrace nucleation. This is a
mechanism illustration, not a statement that every rapidly growing face contains such a defect.
Source selection and preparation matter when comparing crystals described as well formed or defect
free.

A slightly tilted, or vicinal, surface provides another source of existing steps: its terraces form
a staircase rather than one uninterrupted plane. The competition is between reaching a step by
surface diffusion and leaving the surface again. Step spacing compared with the typical distance
travelled before desorption can therefore change incorporation. Step-edge barriers and asymmetric
access from different terraces complicate the simple sketch. The tilted-surface pathway stays in the reader. The continuing screw-dislocation step is demonstrated in the main story before its name is introduced.

### A possible surface influence is not a finished explanation

The warm-temperature prism prefactors below one are empirical constraints on a description of
growth. Surface premelting is a possible contributor: the outer region can lose some crystalline
order while the interior stays solid. A less ordered interface is not necessarily bulk liquid
water, and neither its transport properties nor its effect on basal and prism growth can be read
directly from a stylized disordered band. The proposed connection between this layer and inhibited
prism incorporation remains mechanistic interpretation.

The broad-facet CAK source assumes its parameters are not changed by background pressure, although
many constraining measurements were made at low pressure. Extrapolating to air therefore carries a
condition to test. Libbrecht’s 2016 study reported pressure-dependent inferred prism kinetics,
revising an earlier pressure-invariance interpretation; an air-versus-low-pressure difference
cannot simply be assigned to vapour diffusion or to narrow width alone. Gas transport, latent heat,
adsorbed material and surface structure can act together. The present episode does not assign a
universal direction or magnitude to impurity or air effects, nor claim that one cause explains
every discrepancy.

### What the levitated particles establish

Pokrifka and colleagues’ 2020 analysis uses mass ratios from electrodynamically levitated particles
at approximately −44 to −30 °C and chamber-calibrated ambient ice supersaturations of roughly
3–29%. These are different conditions from the −15 °C, 20 mbar facet example. The primary
observable is normalized levitation voltage interpreted as mass ratio; initial size, temperature
and chamber calibration enter further inference. Growth-time shape, grain count and individual face
speeds were not directly measured.

Ten of seventeen homogeneously frozen cases were represented by constant-mechanism faceted fits;
seven were assigned a kinetic transition under the authors’ model. The printed 0.025 to 0.0016
coefficient change is from an empirical, hypothetical transition fit, not an observed time series
of the attachment coefficient. The authors explored possible changes in exposed surface structure.
A 2024 morphology analysis of a newer, colder 2023 dataset from this apparatus suggests that some
frozen-droplet products may be polycrystalline. It does not establish grain counts in the older
particles, but strengthens the caution against animating these records as a proven single-crystal
faceting movie. The Penn State analysis uses a Nelson–Baker composite growth description; its
characteristic supersaturation is not automatically the same parameter as the nucleation barrier in
the exponential broad-facet relation.

In the 2020 analysis, the inferred response for heterogeneously frozen droplets decreased across increasing chamber ice supersaturation, while coefficients inferred for homogeneously frozen droplets increased (accepted manuscript pp. 21 and 24; Figure 8b). These are trends across distinct runs, with differing conditions and uncertainties, not one particle subjected to a humidity sweep. The chamber value is not the local surface surplus in the broad-facet fit. A decreasing inferred coefficient also does not mean the raw mass-growth rate decreases; the normalized heterogeneous growth rates rose with chamber supply (p. 24, Figure 14). The episode draws only qualitative trend directions, not source data or fitted slope values.

The 2016 heterogeneous records and their 2020 reanalysis belong to one observational lineage.
Revised chamber calibration and interpretation do not produce independent replication. The 2020
analysis also introduced transformations of mass-growth histories that do not explicitly use
ambient supersaturation, including dm/dt proportional to m^(P/3). The reference limits P=1 and P=2
assume particular constant-condition, size/shape and mechanism descriptions. Derivatives and
smoothing introduce additional choices; the exponent is not a shape-independent microscope or a
direct measurement of a molecular mechanism.

### The next question belongs to the narrow facet

The −5 °C example in the monograph, Figure 4.18, concerns small simple prisms in low-pressure
growth experiments, for which plate-like habits were common, especially at low supersaturation.
Slender and hollow columns grown in air near the same temperature involve different transport,
geometry and surface conditions. This is a reason to test the transfer of broad-facet response
functions, not proof from two curves alone that one microscopic explanation is uniquely required.

Harrington and Pokrifka’s peer-reviewed 2026 paper, Revisiting Theories for the Growth of Solid and
Hollow Single Crystals: The Importance of Step-Source Location, considers where surface steps
originate and compares model descriptions with growth observations, including sensitivity to narrow
rims. It is separate from the earlier levitated mass-history study and must not be presented as
another measured coefficient time series. Episode 10 owns the detailed broad-versus-narrow evidence
and proposed feedback; Episode 11 owns how competing growth models are tested. No narrow-facet
parameter or three-dimensional morphology is derived in this episode.
