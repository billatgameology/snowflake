# E08 — How to measure a growing crystal

English editorial draft and substantive reader, 2026-09-20. First parallel batch, initial
script/prototype milestone only. The incoming E07 contract is that a finished shape admits
plausible histories without uniquely measuring its past. The outgoing E09 contract is a growth
rate with stated observation, calibration and apparatus limits, ready for an inference about
delivery and effective surface response. E08 does not fit an attachment coefficient or barrier.

Takeaway: a measurement links something observed to a physical quantity through a checked
relationship, and the apparatus can change the quantity being investigated. The main story uses
one chamber, then one optical measurement, then an explicitly different levitation experiment.
All prototype apparatus, particles, light signals and rate comparisons are original explanatory
schematics. No plotted teaching line is experimental data or an independently solved vapour field.
The source observations remain available through the precise primary links and reader below.

## E08-01 — Give the question an experiment

### Narration

A crystal can suggest where it has been. But its finished shape cannot tell us every temperature,
every change in water supply, or how fast each face grew. To find out what changes growth, we need
to watch it happen while keeping track of the surroundings.

Imagine following one small crystal on a transparent support. We keep its temperature steady,
control the supply of water vapour, and record it as it grows. This is real ice gaining water from
vapour, just as a snow crystal does. Growing it indoors gives us a chance to ask a more controlled
question.

But the support, the walls and even a sensor can change the experiment. Before we trust a growth
rate, we have to ask two things: what did the instrument actually observe, and what did the
instrument itself change? Let's begin with the water supply.

  support and a camera framing its old outline. Keep the crystal in place through E08-04.
- Reveal a temperature control and then the supply region. The complete apparatus must not

## E08-02 — Build a supply you can check

### Narration

Here is a frost-covered ceiling above our crystal. We can hold the frost at one temperature and
the support below at another. The frost exchanges water with the nearby air. Its temperature
sets the amount of vapour in balance with that ice.

Now make the frost a little warmer while holding the support steady. The warmer frost supplies
vapour at a level above the balance level for ice on the colder support. That difference gives
our test crystal a supply for growth. We have changed the source, while keeping the test
temperature fixed.

The two temperature readings help us calculate the surrounding supply in this chamber. They
are not a humidity measurement at the crystal's surface. That calculation depends on the
chamber's geometry and on what else is taking water from the air. A floor that looks almost
empty can be enough to upset it.

### Visual and sources

- Build the source above the existing support. Identify frost as ice, not a cloud of droplets.
  Show reversible exchange before any change. Only the reservoir control changes in paragraph 2.
- Increase the qualitative supply region after the control moves; show net addition against the
  fixed test-crystal outline. Tracers show schematic random transport, not a directed water jet.
- No live `surface humidity` meter. The two temperatures become an explicitly calculated
  surrounding-supply estimate only in paragraph 3. Do not conflate that estimate with local
  surface supersaturation. The reader gives the boundary-condition approximation.

## E08-03 — The floor that looked empty

### Narration

Keep those two temperature settings unchanged. First, consider our test crystal by itself. Water
diffuses from the source, through the air, to the growing ice. We mark the starting outline so
we can see the material added during an interval.

Now replay that comparison from the same starting outline, with extra ice specks on the floor.
They occupy very little space, but they also grow. Our test crystal is sharing the supply with
all of them. With the controls unchanged, would it receive the same supply as before?

No. In this chamber comparison, the neighbours draw down the vapour near the floor, leaving
less for the test crystal. It grows less over the matched interval even though the temperature
readings have not changed. If we ignored the neighbours, we could blame the slower growth on
the surface when the hidden change was in its surroundings. The small amount of visible ice
can be a poor guide to how much it affects the experiment.

### Visual and sources

- Hardest demonstration. Finish the isolated case before resetting. Retain the original
  outline, source/support settings and equal-interval bracket as comparison references. Label
  the reset `replay · same starting crystal`, never shrink a physically growing specimen silently.
- Add the neighbours before the prediction. They must receive visible schematic traffic and
  gain material after release. Keep the test crystal stationary and visibly smaller in added
  extent than the retained first result. The rate contrast is qualitative, not a measured ratio.
- Hold result motion until paragraph 3. Do not show a depleted field, shorter-growth result or
  explanatory conclusion during the question. At paragraph 3 show the vapour change before
  growth, and only then compare the completed cases. Still preserves that disclosure sequence.
- The source's factor-of-twenty estimate is derived under particular geometry and uptake
  assumptions; retain its operands in the reader rather than attaching it to arbitrary dots.

## E08-04 — Repair, then check

### Narration

So the experimenter isolates one test crystal and brings the frost source closer. Removing
other ice reduces competition. Shortening the gap reduces the large-scale supply difference
across the chamber. Neither change makes every uncertainty disappear, and a short gap alone
does not fix a floor crowded with ice.

Next comes a check on the zero point. Adjust the source until the test crystal is between
growing and shrinking. Watch its boundary against the old outline. This helps locate the
balance setting for that run. It has an uncertainty; a dial marked zero is not enough.

There is a separate check using liquid droplets, with the test crystals removed. Adjust the
source until the droplets stay the same size. Their balance condition is independently known,
so it tests the chamber calculation. Remember our first episode: a liquid drop and ice do not
balance against the same vapour level. These two checks use different references. Now we have
a better-controlled setting. How do we measure a change too small to read easily from the outline?

### Visual and sources

- Retain the chamber; remove neighbouring crystals as an explicit setup change and then shorten
  the gap. Do not depict bringing the ceiling closer as reducing supersaturation at fixed
  boundary settings. It improves supply control; quantitative surface corrections remain.
- Sequential zero search: shrinking boundary → larger source setting → boundary stable against
  the before-line. A selected Still pose shows only the current step, not the final answer.
- Explicitly reset to `separate droplet check · no test ice`. Draw liquid as round droplets,
  not faceted solid. The large frost source remains. Label the reference `liquid balance` only
  after the stable-droplet observation, distinguish it from the prior `ice balance`.

## E08-05 — Let light travel both ways

### Narration

Return to our ice crystal on its transparent support. Look at it from the side. A weak laser
shines onto the top. Some light reflects from that upper surface. Some travels through the
ice and reflects from the lower boundary where the ice meets the support.

The second reflection has made an extra round trip through the ice. The two returning light
waves combine. Depending on that extra journey, they can strengthen or weaken each other.
The camera sees a brighter or dimmer spot. That combining of waves is called interference.

Now let the upper surface move outward as ice is added. The lower boundary stays put, so the
extra journey changes. The spot passes from dim to bright and back to dim. One brightness alone
does not tell us how thick the crystal is. The sequence of changes is what makes this a ruler.

### Visual and sources

- Introduce a side-on cross-section with selected upper/lower reflecting interfaces, old ice
  and fixed support. Mark the angle/height as schematic. Trace the first optical route before
  introducing the second; offset their drawn tracks slightly for legibility, not physical scale.
- Introduce the detector only after both returns exist. Show two simple wave traces and their
  combined amplitude in a separate explanatory inset; a wave is not a water molecule trajectory.
- Grow new ice above the initial top line with a stationary base. The optical spot follows the
  relative phase; it does not pulse as an unrelated decorative animation. No cycle count yet.
- `docs/education/chapters/10-how-we-know.html#counting-fringes`
- [Libbrecht, *Snow Crystals*, printed pp. 228–230 and 259; figures 6.14–6.15, 7.10](https://arxiv.org/abs/1910.06389)

## E08-06 — A signal becomes a rate

### Narration

Keep watching that spot. For a known wavelength and this simple optical geometry, each complete
brightness cycle corresponds to the same increase in thickness. How much depends on the
light and on how it travels through ice. We can count complete cycles without knowing the
crystal's starting thickness.

Now add the clock. Mark a beginning and an end, count the cycles between them, and convert them
to an added thickness. Divide that change by the elapsed time, and we have an average growth
speed. The light signal became a distance; the distance and clock became a rate. More growth
in the same interval would give more complete cycles.

A second view can track the side faces directly against a calibrated length scale. That tells
us about widening while the light tells us about thickening. But this laser method does not
give the absolute starting thickness, and complicated reflections can make its cycles hard to
read. Another optical method, using white light, can provide absolute thickness for suitable
thin crystals. Different rulers suit different specimens.

### Visual and sources

- Keep the same cross-section, old top line and detector. Introduce the time trace only after
  an observed cycle; label it `illustrative signal`. First bracket one full dim–bright–dim cycle,
  then reveal an equal-thickness increment, then introduce elapsed time.
- Show a schematic equal-interval comparison only after the conversion is established. No
  fabricated seconds, micrometres or data points. Reader arithmetic is a named optical
  calculation, not an experiment. Partial cycles must not increment a completed-cycle counter.
- In paragraph 3 identify a pair of opposing side faces and their separation. State in the
  reader that separation change is not automatically one face's normal speed. Do not draw
  actual experimental scatter or imply the source's white-light plot used this laser signal.

## E08-07 — The support is part of the experiment

### Narration

Our transparent support makes these reflections possible, but it also touches the ice. That
contact can affect growth, and the support carries heat away. A coating chosen to reduce one
kind of interference can change heat transfer too. A precise signal does not remove those
physical effects.

The small chamber can also operate at low air pressure, which reduces the resistance to vapour
transport. That is useful when investigating the surface, but it is a different setting from
ordinary air. Crystal size, pressure and geometry belong with the result, not just the equipment
list.

For large, branching crystals, researchers use other arrangements. A thin plate can grow out
from a little pedestal, clear of the window beneath it, or a crystal can grow from an ice needle.
These make different shapes accessible, with different limits. Could we go further and measure
growth with no supporting surface touching the particle at all?

### Visual and sources

- Keep the optical specimen while highlighting the contact area and a separately labeled heat
  path. Do not animate coating as a universal improvement or heating as necessarily dominant.
- Introduce reduced air pressure as a separate apparatus condition. No implication that it
  removes surface diffusion, latent heat or all gas transport.
- Sequential schematic pedestal then needle. These are different specimens/apparatus choices;
  do not morph the original optical specimen into a millimetre star or present them as observed
  recordings. End on the no-contact question before revealing levitation hardware.

## E08-08 — Weigh it while it floats

### Narration

A different research group at Penn State uses an electric field to hold a charged ice particle
in the air. Gravity pulls down. The electrical force balances that pull, while other electrodes
keep the particle from drifting sideways. There is no slide touching it.

As the particle gains water, its weight increases. To hold it at the same position, the instrument
adjusts the voltage. With the charge unchanged and the electrical geometry fixed, the required
voltage changes in proportion to mass. The recorded voltage is therefore a way to follow how
the mass changes.

Compare the voltage with its starting value and you get a mass ratio: how many times the
starting mass the particle has now. Combine that record with an initial mass and a clock, and
you can calculate mass gained per unit time. This measurement follows the whole particle.
It does not directly tell us which face advanced or what shape the particle became.

That difference matters. The optical experiment watches dimensions; this experiment follows
mass without a support touching the ice. They have different strengths and different uncertainties.
The second method is an independent route into the problem, not a promise that either apparatus
is free of disturbance. We can now ask more of a proposed explanation than whether it resembles
a photograph.

### Visual and sources

- Begin a new specimen explicitly. Use a neutral irregular ice silhouette marked `particle
  symbol · shape not measured`, not a growing perfect prism. Establish gravity, then opposing
  electric force, then lateral confinement. Keep the levitated position fixed during balance.
- Show aggregate new mass without pretending to observe its shape: brighten/add a halo marked
  `mass indicated schematically` and lengthen both force arrows equally as voltage changes.
  Do not draw a falling trajectory that the source did not measure.
- Delay the voltage record until the feedback action is understood. Display `charge unchanged`
  and `same position` with the relationship. Illustrative ratios, if used, must be marked as
  chosen teaching values; the prototype uses qualitative scales only.
- Only after the mass chain is complete show a sequential recall of the optical chain. This is
  a comparison of observables, not matched-rate data from identical specimens or conditions.

## E08-09 — What the rate leaves unanswered

### Narration

Follow our first chain once more. Two controlled temperatures, a checked chamber and an isolated
crystal give us an estimate of its surroundings. A changing optical signal gives a thickness
change. With elapsed time, that becomes a growth rate. Each link has a reason behind it and a
limit to check.

Then the other chain: a particle held in place, a changing voltage, a mass record and its change
over time. We gain independence from the supporting slide, but we do not gain a picture of
every face. Measuring something well includes being clear about what was not measured.

Now we know how fast ice is being added under specified conditions. But remember the two waits
we met in the air: water has to reach the surface, and the surface has to incorporate it. A
growth rate mixes those effects together. In the next episode, how can we use a measured rate
to find out what the surface itself is doing?

## Reader depth retained, not discarded

### Two temperatures give a boundary estimate, not a local surface measurement

Let `c_sat(T)` denote the equilibrium water-vapour number density over ice at temperature `T`.
It is a property of the ice–vapour balance, not an amount that the background air can hold.
The simple source/support arrangement gives the reference estimate
`sigma_1 = [c_sat(T_reservoir) - c_sat(T_support)] / c_sat(T_support)`. Both number densities
have the same units, so `sigma_1` is dimensionless; `0.01` means a one-percent ice-relative
surplus. The crystal's local surface value is a different quantity because growth draws vapour
down near that surface.

The monograph first states this estimate **in the absence of the test crystal**. A sufficiently
small isolated crystal approximately experiences it as a surrounding or far-field boundary
condition. A large crystal, additional ice, imperfect boundary temperatures and the full chamber
geometry require transport consideration. E08-02 therefore deliberately avoids the chapter's
stronger suggestion that two thermometers directly determine what the growing surface feels.

Sources: `docs/education/chapters/10-how-we-know.html#two-thermometers`;
[Libbrecht, *Snow Crystals*, equation 6.1, printed p. 222](https://arxiv.org/abs/1910.06389).

### Why one percent floor coverage can matter so much

The source works an approximate array of equal hemispherical crystals. Write `f_cover` for
their fractional floor coverage, `L` for source-to-floor separation, `R` for crystal radius,
and `epsilon` for a response factor between zero and one. Its surrounding-floor estimate is
`sigma_bottom / sigma_1 ≈ 1 / (1 + 2 × epsilon × f_cover × L/R)`.
This is a chamber approximation with well-separated small crystals, not a universal formula
for any arrangement, and `sigma_bottom` is not every crystal's local surface value.

For the source's chosen diffusion-limited example, `epsilon = 1`, `f_cover = 0.01`,
`L = 1 cm` and **radius** `R = 10 micrometres`. The denominator is 21, so the calculated
ratio is about 0.048, conventionally summarized as one twentieth. Those are example operands,
not directly observed before/after rates, and the factor does not transfer to the prototype's
drawn specks. Reducing only `L` to 1 mm gives a ratio of one third under the same approximation;
the short gap is helpful but is not a complete remedy for a populated floor. The source's
precision apparatus combines a small gap with selecting one isolated test crystal.

Sources: `docs/education/chapters/10-how-we-know.html#empty-substrate`;
[Libbrecht, *Snow Crystals*, equations 7.6–7.7, printed p. 251, and apparatus on p. 254](https://arxiv.org/abs/1910.06389).

### Calibration is an experimental task

The mathematical reference is zero when reservoir and support temperatures are equal.
The practical growth experiment locates an offset for each crystal using the onset of growth
or sublimation. The monograph describes an absolute supersaturation uncertainty around
0.001 in its low-pressure procedure; this is an uncertainty in a dimensionless fraction,
not a guarantee of one part in a thousand relative accuracy at every small nonzero setting.
The later apparatus paper describes the zero offset as an adjustable part of the analysis
and explores changing that assumption. A precisely displayed controller does not erase this.

The droplet check is a separate experiment with the test ice removed. When liquid droplets
are neither growing nor shrinking, their vapour balance is compared with the independently
known liquid/ice equilibrium relation. It checks the chamber calculation at that condition;
it does not directly measure the local vapour concentration beside a different growing crystal.
E08's crystal-balance and droplet-balance checks must not be animated at one identical setting.

### What the optical cycle measures

For near-normal incidence through a simple plane-parallel ice layer, the round-trip optical
path changes by `2 × n × delta_h`, where `n` is the refractive index and `delta_h` is the
change in physical thickness. One full interference cycle corresponds to one wavelength
`lambda` of additional optical path, giving `delta_h = lambda / (2n)`.
As a rounded teaching calculation, a 633 nm wavelength and `n = 1.31` give about 242 nm per
cycle. These chosen optical values demonstrate the conversion; they are not a transcription
of a particular crystal's measured growth. Accurate work uses the appropriate wavelength,
orientation-dependent optical properties, angle and optical calibration.

Cycle counting alone yields thickness **change**, not the unknown starting thickness. It also
needs the direction of change established by the controlled sequence or other observations:
brightness oscillations alone can occur during either increasing or decreasing thickness.
Multiple reflections, small interfaces, poor contrast and lost cycles can spoil the simple
reading. The monograph describes direct laser interferometry as useful for suitable thicker
crystals but less precise than its white-light method. In white-light interferometry, fringe
spacing across a calibrated spectrum carries absolute thickness information. Its quoted
nanometres-per-second performance concerns speed sensitivity in ideal cases, not a universal
nanometre thickness accuracy. E08 does not mislabel a white-light measurement as laser data.

In the supported geometry with the lower interface fixed, the upper interface's displacement
is the thickness increase. For freely growing opposite faces, the total thickness change is
the sum of both outward displacements. Likewise, changing distance between two opposite side
faces gives one face's normal speed only after the geometry and any symmetry assumption are
specified. A measured silhouette is not a complete three-dimensional reconstruction.

Sources: `docs/education/chapters/10-how-we-know.html#counting-fringes`;
[Libbrecht, *Snow Crystals*, printed pp. 228–230 and 259–260](https://arxiv.org/abs/1910.06389).

### Different instruments buy different access

Substrate contact can affect terrace formation and thermal transport. Hydrophobic treatment may
reduce unwanted contact effects while making heat removal worse. Reducing gas pressure weakens
particle-diffusion resistance but does not remove the latent heat released when vapour becomes
ice. The 2019 apparatus analysis uses a simplified geometric model that is more suitable for
nearly isometric prisms than very thin plates or long columns. E09 must carry these conditions
when it turns a measured rate into fitted surface parameters.

For larger specimens, plate-on-pedestal growth gives an accessible thin crystal above a window,
while an ice needle offers a small support for growth away from a broad substrate. A pedestal
is not contact-free, and neither method samples every environment. In the plate-on-pedestal
arrangement, liquid droplets can appear at high supply and constrain nearby vapour near liquid
saturation. Seeing beautiful, controllable laboratory growth does not mean the instrument can
visit every region of a cloud's temperature, pressure, supply and seed-history space.

### The levitated mass measurement and its limits

The Penn State apparatus balances a charged particle vertically with a DC electric field and
provides lateral confinement with additional electrodes. At fixed position and electrical
geometry, the vertical field is proportional to its controlling voltage. With stable charge,
balancing electrical force against weight means the voltage ratio follows the mass ratio.
Charge changes, position changes and other forces would upset that simple interpretation and
must be controlled. The archive records voltage, position, elapsed time and normalized mass.

The initial liquid-droplet size supplies a separate initial-mass estimate; combining it with
the normalized record gives mass growth. This does not measure facet-specific speeds, grain
structure or the growing particle's actual outline. The local source index records that later
work in this apparatus lineage qualifies the frozen-droplet products as likely polycrystalline.
The schematic particle therefore deliberately has no asserted crystal habit.

The 2016 experiment and the 2020 reanalysis of its heterogeneous traces are not independent
replications. Later analysis corrected conditions and some initial sizes. E08 uses the apparatus
principle and its directly recorded mass-ratio observable, not the superseded fitted coefficient
range or historical conditions assigned to individual archived runs. E09 owns the kinetic
interpretation and changing-response evidence; E11 owns the competing theoretical accounts and
the subsequently published 2026 comparison. No part of this episode treats a dataset as proof
of one narrow-facet mechanism.

### Start by observing, and keep the distinction

A cold dark surface and a small magnifier are enough to inspect freshly fallen snow before it
clumps or changes. Record when and where it was collected, and distinguish a selected striking
specimen from a representative sample. Such observations are useful evidence. They do not by
themselves supply the controlled growth history needed to infer a surface response. The
laboratory and outdoor observation answer related questions with different information.

Source: `docs/education/chapters/10-how-we-know.html#go-and-look`;
[Libbrecht, *Snow Crystals*, printed p. 445](https://arxiv.org/abs/1910.06389).
