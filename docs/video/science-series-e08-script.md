critique. Rebuilt causal demonstrations are under renewed performance review; earlier production

Takeaway: a measurement links something observed to a physical quantity through a checked

## E08-01 — Give the question an experiment

### Narration

A crystal can suggest where it has been. But its finished shape cannot tell us every temperature,
every change in water supply, or how fast each face grew. To find out what changes growth, we need
to watch it happen while keeping track of the surroundings.

Imagine following one small crystal on a transparent support. We keep its temperature steady,
control the supply of water vapour, and record it as it grows. This is real ice gaining water from
vapour, just as a snow crystal does. Growing it indoors gives us a chance to ask a more controlled
question.

But the support, the walls and even a sensor can change the experiment. Before we trust a growth
rate, we have to ask two things: what did the instrument actually observe, and what did the
instrument itself change? Let's begin with the water supply.

## E08-02 — Build a supply you can check

### Narration

Here is a frost-covered ceiling above our crystal. We can hold the frost at one temperature and
the support below at another. The frost exchanges water with the nearby air. Its temperature
sets the amount of vapour in balance with that ice.

Now make the frost a little warmer while holding the support steady. The warmer frost supplies
vapour at a level above the balance level for ice on the colder support. That difference gives
our test crystal a supply for growth. We have changed the source, while keeping the test
temperature fixed.

The two temperature readings help us calculate the surrounding supply in this chamber. They
are not a humidity measurement at the crystal's surface. That calculation depends on the
chamber's geometry and on what else is taking water from the air. A floor that looks almost
empty can be enough to upset it.

### Visual and sources

- Build the source above the existing support. Identify frost as ice, not a cloud of droplets.
  Show reversible exchange before any change. Only the reservoir control changes in paragraph 2.
- Increase the qualitative supply region after the control moves; show net addition against the
  fixed test-crystal outline. Tracers show schematic random transport, not a directed water jet.
- No live `surface humidity` meter. The two temperatures become an explicitly calculated
  surrounding-supply estimate only in paragraph 3. Do not conflate that estimate with local
  surface supersaturation. The reader gives the boundary-condition approximation.

## E08-03 — The floor that looked empty

### Narration

Keep those two temperature settings unchanged. First, consider our test crystal by itself. Water
diffuses from the source, through the air, to the growing ice. We mark the starting outline so
we can see the material added during an interval.

Now replay that comparison from the same starting outline, with extra ice specks on the floor.
They occupy very little space, but they also grow. Our test crystal is sharing the supply with
all of them. With the controls unchanged, would it receive the same supply as before?

No. In this chamber comparison, the neighbours draw down the vapour near the floor, leaving
less for the test crystal. It grows less over the matched interval even though the temperature
readings have not changed. If we ignored the neighbours, we could blame the slower growth on
the surface when the hidden change was in its surroundings. The small amount of visible ice
can be a poor guide to how much it affects the experiment.

- Hold result motion until paragraph 3. Do not show a depleted field, shorter-growth result or

## E08-04 — Repair, then check

### Narration

So the experimenter isolates one test crystal and brings the frost source closer. Removing other ice reduces competition. Shortening the gap reduces the large-scale supply difference across the chamber. Neither change makes every uncertainty disappear, and a short gap alone does not fix a floor crowded with ice.

Next comes a check on the zero point. Here the test crystal is slowly shrinking; keep its starting outline in view. Raise the source setting a little, then watch the boundary again. Now it stays in place, although water continues to enter and leave the ice. Finding this boundary between growth and loss helps locate the ice-balance setting for the run. The setting still has an uncertainty; a dial marked zero is not enough.

Keep that ice-balance setting as a reference. Then begin a separate check with liquid droplets and no test ice. Watch a droplet shrink at first. Adjust the source again until the droplet boundary holds steady. That liquid-balance condition is independently known, so it checks the chamber calculation. Liquid and ice need different vapour levels to balance; we have not found the same zero twice. With the supply better checked, how can we measure a very small thickness change?

## E08-05 — Let light travel both ways

### Narration

Return to our supported ice and look at it from the side. Keep the lower boundary fixed against the transparent support. Follow a weak laser beam down to the upper surface. One part reflects there. Another part travels through the ice, reflects at the lower boundary, and returns through the ice again.

Bring both returns into the same spot at the camera. The second has travelled an extra round trip through the ice. This enlarged wave drawing shows what happens when the returns combine. Crests lining up reinforce each other; the combined wave is stronger. Crests meeting troughs weaken each other instead. The camera measures that combined brightness. This is interference.

Now watch actual addition in our teaching drawing: new ice builds above the old top line. The bottom stays fixed, so the extra light journey gets longer. As thickness increases, the combined spot goes from dim to bright and back to dim. That is one complete cycle. The added ice stays. A single brightness would not tell us the starting thickness; the changing signal is our ruler.

- Introduce a side-on cross-section with selected upper/lower reflecting interfaces, old ice
  and fixed support. Mark the angle/height as schematic. Trace the first optical route before
  introducing the second; offset their drawn tracks slightly for legibility, not physical scale.
- Follow the two return paths into one shared camera spot. The enlarged explanatory inset overlays
  both waves and draws their sum; first reinforce, then cancel. Clearly distinguish that
  wave-addition comparison from the subsequent physical thickness change. A wave is not a molecule path.
- Grow one retained thickness band above the initial top line with a stationary base. The optical
  spot follows the relative phase and completes dim–bright–dim. Preserve that exact added band

## E08-06 — A signal becomes a rate

### Narration

Keep that same crystal, its added ice and the same camera spot. Watch two more complete brightness cycles as the top moves outward. Each cycle adds the same thickness in this simple geometry, for a known wavelength and optical properties. The equal bands mark thickness increments, not molecular layers. We can count these changes without knowing how thick the original ice was.

Now start a clock beside the same growing crystal. During this marked interval, watch the clock sweep while two more cycles add two more equal bands. Stop the clock and keep the beginning and ending lines. Convert the counted cycles to a thickness change, then divide by the elapsed time. That gives an average thickening speed over this interval. The light measured a change; the clock made it a rate.

A separate calibrated view can follow the two side faces as they move apart. That measures widening; our light signal measures thickening. The laser cycles do not give the unknown starting thickness, and complicated reflections can spoil the reading. Other optical methods can measure absolute thickness for suitable specimens. The reader explains the differences.

### Visual and sources

- Keep the same cross-section, old top line and detector. Introduce the time trace only after
  an observed cycle; label it `illustrative signal`. First bracket one full dim–bright–dim cycle,
  then reveal an equal-thickness increment, then introduce elapsed time.
- Keep every earlier band. First add two equal increments with two complete cycles. Then start
  a visibly sweeping clock for a new interval as two further cycles add two further bands.
  Retain the interval’s beginning and ending boundary lines. No fabricated seconds, micrometres
  or data points. Partial cycles must not increment a completed-cycle counter.
- In paragraph 3 identify a pair of opposing side faces and their separation. State in the

## E08-07 — The support is part of the experiment

### Narration

Look beneath the same crystal: its lower surface touches the support. When vapour becomes ice, heat is released. Some heat can leave through that contact. The contact can also affect how the ice grows. A precise optical signal does not remove those effects.

Here is a separate arrangement for a larger plate. A small ice pedestal raises it above the window. Watch new ice spread outward while the window stays below, clear of the growing rim. The pedestal still touches the crystal. This changes the contact; it does not abolish it.

## E08-08 — Weigh it while it floats

### Narration

A different research group at Penn State holds a charged ice particle in an electric field. Gravity pulls down. An upward electrical force balances the weight; other electrodes prevent sideways drift. Mark its position. No slide touches it.

Keep the electrode arrangement and the particle position unchanged, with the same electrical charge. Now water joins the particle. Our added colour stands for extra mass, not an observed shape. As the weight grows, the instrument raises the balancing voltage to keep the particle at that marked position. Under those conditions, the required voltage increases in proportion to mass.

Keep the starting voltage beside the new reading. Dividing the new reading by the starting one gives the mass ratio: how many times the starting mass we now have. We also need to know how much ice we started with, from a separate estimate, and how much time passed. Then we can calculate mass gained per unit time.

This measures the whole particle. It does not show which face advanced or how its shape changed. The optical experiment followed a dimension; this one follows mass without a touching slide. These are different specimens and measurements, with different uncertainties. Neither instrument is free of disturbance.

## Reader depth retained, not discarded

### Two temperatures give a boundary estimate, not a local surface measurement

Let `c_sat(T)` denote the equilibrium water-vapour number density over ice at temperature `T`.
It is a property of the ice–vapour balance, not an amount that the background air can hold.
The simple source/support arrangement gives the reference estimate
`sigma_1 = [c_sat(T_reservoir) - c_sat(T_support)] / c_sat(T_support)`. Both number densities
have the same units, so `sigma_1` is dimensionless; `0.01` means a one-percent ice-relative
surplus. The crystal's local surface value is a different quantity because growth draws vapour
down near that surface.

The monograph first states this estimate **in the absence of the test crystal**. A sufficiently
small isolated crystal approximately experiences it as a surrounding or far-field boundary
condition. A large crystal, additional ice, imperfect boundary temperatures and the full chamber
geometry require transport consideration. Two thermometers therefore establish a reference for the chamber; they do not directly measure the vapour at the growing surface.

Sources: `docs/education/chapters/10-how-we-know.html#two-thermometers`;
[Libbrecht, *Snow Crystals*, equation 6.1, printed p. 222](https://arxiv.org/abs/1910.06389).

### Why one percent floor coverage can matter so much

The source works an approximate array of equal hemispherical crystals. Write `f_cover` for
their fractional floor coverage, `L` for source-to-floor separation, `R` for crystal radius,
and `epsilon` for a response factor between zero and one. Its surrounding-floor estimate is
`sigma_bottom / sigma_1 ≈ 1 / (1 + 2 × epsilon × f_cover × L/R)`.
This is a chamber approximation with well-separated small crystals, not a universal formula
for any arrangement, and `sigma_bottom` is not every crystal's local surface value.

For the source's chosen diffusion-limited example, `epsilon = 1`, `f_cover = 0.01`,
`L = 1 cm` and **radius** `R = 10 micrometres`. The denominator is 21, so the calculated
ratio is about 0.048, conventionally summarized as one twentieth. Those are example operands,
not directly observed before/after rates, and the factor does not transfer to the schematic specks shown here. Reducing only `L` to 1 mm gives a ratio of one third under the same approximation;
the short gap is helpful but is not a complete remedy for a populated floor. The source's
precision apparatus combines a small gap with selecting one isolated test crystal.

Sources: `docs/education/chapters/10-how-we-know.html#empty-substrate`;
[Libbrecht, *Snow Crystals*, equations 7.6–7.7, printed p. 251, and apparatus on p. 254](https://arxiv.org/abs/1910.06389).

### Calibration is an experimental task

The mathematical reference is zero when reservoir and support temperatures are equal.
The practical growth experiment locates an offset for each crystal using the onset of growth
or sublimation. The monograph describes an absolute supersaturation uncertainty around
0.001 in its low-pressure procedure; this is an uncertainty in a dimensionless fraction,
not a guarantee of one part in a thousand relative accuracy at every small nonzero setting.
The later apparatus paper describes the zero offset as an adjustable part of the analysis
and explores changing that assumption. A precisely displayed controller does not erase this.

The droplet check is a separate experiment with the test ice removed. When liquid droplets
are neither growing nor shrinking, their vapour balance is compared with the independently
known liquid/ice equilibrium relation. It checks the chamber calculation at that condition;
it does not directly measure the local vapour concentration beside a different growing crystal.
The crystal-balance and droplet-balance checks use different settings.

[Libbrecht, *Snow Crystals*, printed p. 255](https://arxiv.org/abs/1910.06389);

### What the optical cycle measures

For near-normal incidence through a simple plane-parallel ice layer, the round-trip optical
path changes by `2 × n × delta_h`, where `n` is the refractive index and `delta_h` is the
change in physical thickness. One full interference cycle corresponds to one wavelength
`lambda` of additional optical path, giving `delta_h = lambda / (2n)`.
As a rounded teaching calculation, a 633 nm wavelength and `n = 1.31` give about 242 nm per
cycle. These chosen optical values demonstrate the conversion; they are not a transcription
of a particular crystal's measured growth. Accurate work uses the appropriate wavelength,
orientation-dependent optical properties, angle and optical calibration.

Cycle counting alone yields thickness **change**, not the unknown starting thickness. It also
needs the direction of change established by the controlled sequence or other observations:
brightness oscillations alone can occur during either increasing or decreasing thickness.
Multiple reflections, small interfaces, poor contrast and lost cycles can spoil the simple
reading. The monograph describes direct laser interferometry as useful for suitable thicker
crystals but less precise than its white-light method. In white-light interferometry, fringe
spacing across a calibrated spectrum carries absolute thickness information. Its quoted
nanometres-per-second performance concerns speed sensitivity in ideal cases, not a universal
nanometre thickness accuracy. The two optical methods supply different information.

In the supported geometry with the lower interface fixed, the upper interface's displacement
is the thickness increase. For freely growing opposite faces, the total thickness change is
the sum of both outward displacements. Likewise, changing distance between two opposite side
faces gives one face's normal speed only after the geometry and any symmetry assumption are
specified. A measured silhouette is not a complete three-dimensional reconstruction.

Sources: `docs/education/chapters/10-how-we-know.html#counting-fringes`;
[Libbrecht, *Snow Crystals*, printed pp. 228–230 and 259–260](https://arxiv.org/abs/1910.06389).

### Different instruments buy different access

Substrate contact can affect terrace formation and thermal transport. Hydrophobic treatment may
reduce unwanted contact effects while making heat removal worse. Reducing gas pressure weakens
particle-diffusion resistance but does not remove the latent heat released when vapour becomes
ice. The 2019 apparatus analysis uses a simplified geometric model that is more suitable for
nearly isometric prisms than very thin plates or long columns. Those conditions matter when a measured rate is used to infer a surface response.

For larger specimens, plate-on-pedestal growth gives an accessible thin crystal above a window,
while an ice needle offers a small support for growth away from a broad substrate. A pedestal
is not contact-free, and neither method samples every environment. In the plate-on-pedestal
arrangement, liquid droplets can appear at high supply and constrain nearby vapour near liquid
saturation. Seeing beautiful, controllable laboratory growth does not mean the instrument can
visit every region of a cloud's temperature, pressure, supply and seed-history space.

### The levitated mass measurement and its limits

The Penn State apparatus balances a charged particle vertically with a DC electric field and
provides lateral confinement with additional electrodes. At fixed position and electrical
geometry, the vertical field is proportional to its controlling voltage. With stable charge,
balancing electrical force against weight means the voltage ratio follows the mass ratio.
Charge changes, position changes and other forces would upset that simple interpretation and
must be controlled. The archive records voltage, position, elapsed time and normalized mass.

The initial liquid-droplet size supplies a separate initial-mass estimate; combining it with
the normalized record gives mass growth. This does not measure facet-specific speeds, grain
structure or the growing particle's actual outline. Later work using this apparatus qualifies the frozen-droplet products as likely polycrystalline: one particle may contain several differently oriented grains. A mass trace alone cannot settle that question or establish a crystal habit.

The 2016 experiment and the 2020 reanalysis of its heterogeneous traces are not independent
replications. Later analysis corrected conditions and some initial sizes. The instrument's directly recorded voltage and inferred mass ratio must be distinguished from fitted surface coefficients and the conditions assigned to individual runs. Interpreting the changing growth response requires further analysis and comparison between explanations. A mass-growth dataset by itself does not prove one particular mechanism on a narrow crystal face.

### Start by observing, and keep the distinction

A cold dark surface and a small magnifier are enough to inspect freshly fallen snow before it
clumps or changes. Record when and where it was collected, and distinguish a selected striking
specimen from a representative sample. Such observations are useful evidence. They do not by
themselves supply the controlled growth history needed to infer a surface response. The
laboratory and outdoor observation answer related questions with different information.
