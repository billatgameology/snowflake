# E06 — One substance, many shapes

English production authority, 2026-09-20. Full first-batch production authorized; source freeze and performed-review status are recorded separately. Viewing order remains E05 → E06 → E07. Runtime follows a spoken rehearsal, not a scene quota.

**Question:** How do controlled growing conditions organize the shapes of snow crystals?

**Takeaway:** Growth perpendicular to the end faces or side faces changes a crystal's proportions; branching changes its elaboration. Temperature and spare vapour organize useful experimental patterns, but a two-axis map needs the pressure, seed, growth history, size and method that gave it meaning.

**Established prerequisites:** E02 demonstrated basal ends, prism sides and their outward growth directions; E03 demonstrated air delivery and surface addition as separate, coupled processes; E04 demonstrated steps and new layers; E05's agreed boundary supplies branching feedback. Percent supersaturation is not assumed. E06 restates the feedback in one sentence where it is used, so production need not depend on the final E05 wording.

**Image status:** The opening uses two registered project model recordings, identified as such, followed by qualitative teaching drawings. Ch7 supplies source-bound observations; the experimental illustrations do not replay a laboratory crystal or solve its growth. Recorded models remain separate from experimental comparisons.

## E06-01 — Two questions hiding in one shape

### Narration

A branch can explain why a crystal becomes more elaborate. But why is one crystal almost flat, while another grows long like a pencil? Here are two computer-grown examples from our library. First, look at this thin plate. Its width is much greater than its thickness. Now look at a separate long column. Its length stands out instead. These are model recordings, not natural specimens. The same ordinary ice structure can have very different outside proportions.

Now switch to simplified shape drawings: a broad plate, then a slender column. Now give the plate branches. Its outline becomes much busier, but it can still be thin. How flat or long it is, and how much it branches, are two different things to notice.

We call those overall proportions the crystal's habit. Let's follow the faces that change those proportions. Then we'll see what happens when experimenters change the air in which real crystals grow.

## E06-02 — Which face makes a plate wider?

### Narration

Take this small, blocky prism. The flat ends are its basal faces. The six walls around the sides are its prism faces. Those are the directions we met when we looked inside ice. Keep the original little crystal visible while new ice grows around it.

In this teaching example, let the side walls advance outward faster than the ends. The walls don't travel through the ice. New material is being added in front of them. Before we let that happen, which dimension will gain more: the width, or the length between the ends?

The width gains more. Watch the sides move away from the old core while the ends advance only a little. The crystal becomes broader compared with its thickness. A broad face can therefore be a slowly advancing face: its area grows because the surrounding edge moves outward.

P1 introduces ends, then sides. P2 shows outward side arrows, identifies them as chosen rates, and holds before growth. Release only in P3; side-normal addition expands radius while c extent grows slightly. A width bracket and end-to-end bracket arrive after the growth, with no numbers. The large basal face must not carry the fast arrow. An orthographic side section may be used on a phone, with parent hexagon retained.

## E06-03 — Change the advance, change the proportions

### Narration

Start again from an identical little core. This time the ends advance faster than the side walls. The temperature isn't being simulated here; we're choosing the face movements so we can see what each movement does.

Now new ice builds beyond the ends, and the distance between them grows much more than the width. The result is a column. Put the two completed examples together: faster side advance made the broad shape; faster end advance made the long shape.

That tells us how different growth directions change the proportions. It doesn't yet tell us why the faces grow at different rates. Water still has to arrive through the air and become part of a surface. A branching feedback helps explain intricate outlines; it isn't, by itself, an explanation for keeping an entire crystal extremely thin.

### Visual and sources

Explicit reset to identical core and same view/scale. P1 end arrows, no answer geometry until P2. P2 grows column; only after the completed result introduce the prior plate beside it, using the same dimensional scale. P3 focus the slow end normal of plate and slow side normal of column in sequence. Final stage closes to one neutral specimen, not a plate morphing into a column.

## E06-04 — Let the experiment choose the shape

### Narration

How can we find out what real ice does? Grow separate crystals while controlling their surroundings. Nakaya made that approach famous by growing snow in a refrigerated chamber. Later laboratories used other methods, including a cloud tunnel that kept a crystal suspended in an upward flow of air.

In that tunnel study, the air was kept near balance with liquid water, as in a cloud containing droplets. Near five degrees below freezing, long forms developed. In a separate run near fifteen below, growth spread into flat, branched forms. The experiments changed temperature while keeping the same kind of cloud setting.

That last condition matters. Balance with liquid water doesn't mean the same spare vapour above the ice balance at every temperature. So these are observations along a stated experimental path, not proof that temperature acts alone. They give us a useful contrast: real ice can favour different growth directions under different controlled conditions.

### Visual and sources

Teaching reconstruction: establish chamber and its held-condition card before any result. Show one specimen labelled `near −5 °C`, then a new specimen labelled `near −15 °C`. Establish each alone; show completed contrast in P3. Label `schematic of reported habits` and `cloud tunnel · near liquid-water saturation`. No raw rate, equal-size claim, or simulated knob-to-crystal kinetics. Do not use library recordings under these temperature labels. The source's −5.3 °C series includes hollow columns and needles; the −14.4 °C series includes dendrites. Shapes are category summaries, not tracings or replayed specimen histories.

## E06-05 — More spare vapour, at one temperature

### Narration

Now keep the temperature fixed, near fifteen below, and change the water supply. Remember the ice balance: water can leave as well as join. Air with a little extra vapour above that balance can support growth. Air with more extra vapour gives a stronger supply. That extra, measured relative to the ice balance, is called supersaturation.

First consider the lower-supply case. A plate can grow with a simple outline. Now consider a higher-supply case at the same temperature. A projecting corner can get ahead, gain better access to vapour, and grow farther ahead. Branching can become more pronounced. Laboratory series show this progression, though the exact outcome also depends on the crystal and how long it grows.

Compare the two after each has grown: simple plate, branching plate. Both are broadly flat. We've changed elaboration without making the crystal a column. More supply doesn't assign one universal shape, and colder doesn't automatically mean more branches. Temperature and supply influence both parts of the story; these comparisons help us tell the parts apart.

### Visual and sources

Introduce the qualitative supply reference before any plot axis. At fixed temperature, equal drawn air regions carry an ice-balance reference and then extra white vapour markers; counts are schematic and are not collision probabilities. P2 presents the simple case first, then a new higher-supply case with visibly increased outer supply. Only after that input appears does the branching construction start. P3 combines them with a matched edge-on thinness cue. No percent values or universal branching threshold appear.

## E06-06 — A hollow grows without digging

### Narration

A long crystal has another way to become more elaborate. Look at one end of this solid column. Suppose the rim advances faster than the middle. Keep the old ice marked. What happens if that difference continues?

The rim extends around an open space. The middle also may grow, but it falls behind the rim. A hollow develops at the end without anyone digging out the old ice. That distinction is easy to miss in a finished photograph: a deep opening can be the result of unequal addition.

Hollow columns occur in the column-growing region as the supply increases. Access to vapour can reinforce the growing rim's lead, much as it reinforces a branch. But this outline alone doesn't settle what happens on the rim's very narrow surface. We'll return to that question later. For now, a hollow is another feature to record alongside the crystal's overall proportions.

### Visual and sources

Select top end of same column, then labelled longitudinal cutaway. The solid initial core and original cap line remain fixed. New rim and slower centre extend only outside the original body: never subtract old core to animate a cavity. One selected end suffices; note the other end may hollow too. P1 question holds original geometry; P2 releases added material. P3 briefly returns to oblique hollow silhouette; narrow-rim model is explicitly unperformed.

## E06-07 — Build the map from the comparisons

### Narration

We can collect comparisons like these on a map. But put the experiment's conditions beside it from the start: air pressure, the kind of seed, how it was grown, and the size or age at which we looked. Those conditions matter. Even the traditional cold end of this map has been revised by later experiments.

Across the page goes temperature. Up the page goes more spare vapour above the ice balance. Put the simple plate in its broad neighbourhood first, then the branching forms above it. Add the long forms around the other temperature we explored. Now the map summarizes relationships we have already seen.

This is the snow-crystal morphology diagram, often called the Nakaya diagram. It helps us organize a striking pattern: familiar growth conditions favour plates in some temperature regions and columns in others, while increasing supply often brings branching or hollows. The regions are approximate, and the two controls are not separate switches with one job each.

## E06-08 — Two axes leave things out

### Narration

Try using the map backwards. If I hand you a small plate, can you read off one exact temperature? No. Different conditions can produce similar forms, and similar reported temperature and humidity can produce different forms.

One laboratory compared ice that began as frozen droplets with ice grown from prepared frost seeds. Their growing shapes differed. They also found compact plates and short columns under similar reported temperature and humidity. How a crystal starts can leave a mark on what follows.

Pressure changes delivery through the air. Size and time change how far growth has developed. And colder-cloud experiments found many forms made from several crystal orientations, beyond the tidy single prisms of the familiar drawing. The map stays useful when we keep its conditions attached; it becomes misleading when we turn it into a guaranteed weather report.

## E06-09 — A point cannot tell a whole journey

### Narration

We can now separate three observations. Sideways advance and end advance change a crystal's proportions. Branching and hollowing change how its growth is distributed. Controlled experiments show how these behaviours vary with the surroundings.

That's enough to make a useful map. It isn't yet a complete explanation of why the surface changes its behaviour with temperature. To test that, we'll eventually need measurements of growing faces and a model that survives comparison with them.

## Reader depth retained, not discarded

### Proportions, face speeds and the limit of a photograph

For a simple hexagonal prism, describe the end-to-end size as c and the across-crystal size as a. Their ratio c/a summarizes whether the prism is relatively flat or long. This convention uses total dimensions; an author using half-lengths consistently obtains the same ratio. The definition of the across-crystal direction must still be checked: across corners and across flats differ by a geometric factor. Complex, hollow, branched or multiple-orientation crystals require more than one number to describe them.

The animation prescribes motions of faces; it does not predict them. For the illustrative prism, increasing lateral extent rapidly while increasing c slowly makes c/a smaller. Reversing the relative advances makes it larger. Neither animation removes ice from the starting core. A growing broad basal face can advance slowly in its normal direction while its area increases because prism faces move outward. That distinction separates surface area from face-normal speed.

A real vapour-grown crystal couples geometry, local supply and effective surface response. Libbrecht's extreme-habit argument requires a strong difference between basal and prism responses to sustain extremely thin plates or slender columns in that setting. It does not make the aspect ratio a direct numerical measurement of those responses: a single final photograph supplies neither the full time-dependent growth nor the local vapour fields. Later, we will examine how growth rates are measured and what they reveal about surface response, then ask whether that response changes when a facet becomes narrow.

Sources: `docs/education/chapters/07-plates-columns-plates-columns.html#habit`, `docs/education/chapters/07-plates-columns-plates-columns.html#the-rule`, `docs/education/chapters/07-plates-columns-plates-columns.html#not-diffusion`; [Libbrecht, Snow Crystals, printed pp. 111–112](https://arxiv.org/abs/1910.06389v2).

### What the upward direction measures

At a stated temperature, let the ice-balance vapour concentration be c_sat and the concentration far enough from the growing crystal be c_inf. Ice-relative supersaturation is sigma_inf = (c_inf − c_sat) / c_sat. It is dimensionless. Multiply by 100 to express a percentage. As a teaching example, an equal-volume sample containing 110 arbitrary units of vapour against an ice-balance reference of 100 has 10 extra units, or 10% supersaturation. Those chosen counts are arithmetic, not a measurement of a cloud.

An increase along that axis means a larger fractional excess at the specified temperature. The reference itself changes with temperature, so equal percentages at two temperatures need not mean equal absolute amounts of water. A vapour amount near the surface also need not equal the far-away amount: the depletion demonstrated in our investigation of air delivery remains part of the problem. Do not substitute a chart's remote supply into a surface growth law as if both locations were interchangeable.

Liquid-water saturation is a different reference. At the subfreezing temperatures used for the warm cloud-tunnel comparison, vapour in balance with liquid droplets is above the ice balance, but its ice-relative excess depends on temperature. Thus a series grown near liquid saturation follows a temperature-dependent path through an ice-relative morphology diagram. Where liquid droplets persist, their condensation and evaporation can buffer vapour near liquid-water balance; this is not an inviolable humidity ceiling for every natural cloud, particularly in much colder regimes where long-lived liquid is absent.

Sources: `docs/education/chapters/07-plates-columns-plates-columns.html#second-axis`; E01 reader on the two reference balances; [Takahashi et al. (1991), experimental conditions](https://www.jstage.jst.go.jp/article/jmsj1965/69/1/69_1_15/_article). This qualification replaces the chapter's overly broad suggestion that every point above water saturation belongs only to laboratories.

### What the controlled temperature comparison actually controls

The cloud-tunnel experiment maintained individual selected ice crystals in an updraft, allowing growth in conditions corresponding to free fall. The published study covered −3 to −23 °C and periods up to 30 minutes. Its detailed photo series includes a columnar/hollow/needle sequence near −5.3 °C and branched planar forms near −14.4 °C. Those are separate specimens grown under the study's stated protocol, not one crystal changing shape as a temperature knob moves.

The reported cloud setting was nominal liquid-water saturation and atmospheric pressure around 1010 mb. It therefore did not hold the ice-relative supersaturation constant across temperatures. Seed preparation, selected single-crystal population, elapsed growth and relative airflow also belong to the account. The paper's images at different times can represent different crystals grown for those durations. A production animation must not silently turn that sampling into one continuously observed specimen.

The experiment supports a warm-region alternation of favoured forms under that protocol; it does not license universal temperature boundaries. Its coldest column classification conflicts with later cold-region work and is not used here as a universal band. The official corrigendum corrects the continued Figure 3 photographic panel and publication-month information; production should use the corrected orientation when inspecting the source.

### A photograph matrix is not a census

Later morphology surveys provide individual laboratory observations arranged by temperature and far-away supply. Libbrecht's 2023 survey contains 206 observations on tips of c-axis ice needles, with a size and elapsed time for each panel. The reported temperature uncertainty is typically ±0.2 °C; reported far-away supersaturation is estimated between 0.8 and 1.2 times the stated value. That vapour estimate comes from the apparatus model, not a tiny direct probe at each growing face.

A needle tip offers a reproducible starting geometry and one exposed basal end, but it also influences the surrounding field. The selected photographs favour legible, isolated examples; they are not frequencies of forms in an unbiased cloud sample. Different panels have different times and sizes, and a two-dimensional image can hide thickness or hollows. Comparing two pictures requires carrying their individual labels, rather than assuming every tile is a matched exposure. The episode's qualitative map does not digitize this matrix or turn its icons into new observations.

### Why seed history and the cold region deserve their own warning

Bacon, Baker and Swanson compared small levitated particles grown from frozen droplets with particles grown from prepared frost seeds. Their compact droplet-grown forms clustered closer to equal dimensions; the prepared-seed forms spanned thicker plates and short columns. They also reported plates and columns at similar reported temperature and humidity. This supplies a counterexample to the idea that those two environmental numbers uniquely determine every shape.

The histories were not identical apart from an abstract label: freezing, sublimation, holding and total subsequent growth differed. Humidity was inferred from particle growth, and the paper's prose and figures do not give one completely consistent supersaturation range. This episode therefore makes the qualitative non-uniqueness claim without inventing a precisely matched pair or promoting a proposed defect mechanism into a direct observation. The instrument, the origin of surface steps and the inferred response need their own experimental examination.

Bailey and Hallett explored much colder growth with pressure and nucleation method specified. Their laboratory account found predominantly plate-like, often multiple-orientation forms between approximately −20 and −40 °C under its conditions, with columnar growth becoming predominant colder than that. They attributed part of the mismatch with classic cold-column charts to initiation method and classification. This revises the old diagram's authority, rather than supplying a new universal line at −40 °C. Their growing population also includes many polycrystals, so a cartoon of one perfect hexagonal prism cannot represent its census.

### Hollows, narrow rims and what is still unmeasured

A hollow end need not be formed by melting or sublimating the interior. If the rim advances farther than the centre while both accumulate ice, the advancing boundary surrounds a recess. The old solid below the initial end remains intact. Diffusion can favour projecting regions, reinforcing a rim's advantage in an appropriate growing geometry. That is the connection to branching, not a claim that every hollow has been quantitatively predicted.

A deep hollow brings a narrow rim into the problem. How steps originate and move on that narrow surface, how its effective response differs from a broad facet, and what roles defects and history play require measurements and competing models. Those questions remain part of the investigation ahead. The present cutaway is a construction with chosen displacements: it teaches how addition can make a cavity and gives no rate, rim thickness or onset threshold.

Sources: `docs/education/chapters/07-plates-columns-plates-columns.html#hollow`; `docs/education/chapters/09-the-menagerie.html#names`; [Libbrecht, Snow Crystals, printed p. 114](https://arxiv.org/abs/1910.06389v2).

### Description is useful before a complete explanation

A morphology diagram organizes repeated observations. It lets us compare growth protocols, look for disagreements and pose questions to a model. It does not, on its own, explain why basal and prism surfaces change their relative behaviour with temperature, or uniquely reconstruct a crystal's journey. It also cannot stand in for the population of aggregated, rimed, damaged or multiple-orientation particles falling from a cloud.

The often-used warm progression from plate-favouring to column-favouring and back to plate-favouring conditions is a useful pattern. Treating it as sharp, exhaustive temperature bins loses the dependence on supply, protocol and history. Treating every increase in supply as guaranteed branching also loses size, temperature and surface dependence. Next, we follow one crystal through changing conditions. Later, measurements of growth will let us compare physical explanations.
