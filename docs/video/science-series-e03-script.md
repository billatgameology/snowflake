## E03-01 — Follow one molecule

### Narration

Here is the stellar dendrite we left at the end of the last episode. It is one computer-grown
recording from our library, not footage of a natural crystal. Last time we looked inside the solid.
Now leave the crystal where it is and look at the air around it.

Imagine marking one water molecule in that air. It does not see the nearest branch and steer toward
it. Nitrogen and oxygen molecules keep knocking it onto a new path. Our marker will exaggerate that
zigzag so we can follow it; the real collisions happen on a scale far too small and fast to watch
this way.

The molecule has two different waits ahead. It must first reach the ice through the air. Then it has
to become part of the ice. Those are the two parts we called arriving and joining last time. Here is
the puzzle for this episode: why can even a willing surface grow slowly?

## E03-02 — A random walk can have a direction

### Narration

Keep just our marked molecule and the schematic crystal. One collision sends the molecule left, the
next sends it right, and another may send it almost back where it came from. No single step knows
where the crystal is. This wandering motion continues even in air that looks perfectly still.

Now release many markers. Molecules wander both toward and away from the ice. But if the air farther
out contains more water vapor than the air beside the growing surface, more random paths begin on
the crowded side. Add all those two-way journeys together and there is a net delivery from richer
air toward the depleted region.

That statistical spreading is diffusion. The direction belongs to the crowd, not to an individual
molecule. In still air, net vapor transport can therefore feed a crystal without suction, attraction
or a wind aimed at it. Random motion and directed net flow are not opposites; the second emerges
from many examples of the first.

- Replace the recording with one explicitly schematic hexagonal crystal. Animate one reversible,
  collision-broken path, then many independent two-way paths. Only after both directions are visible
  should an aggregate arrow appear from the richer side toward the depleted side.
- Keep `random path` visually distinct from `net diffusion`; never aim every particle inward.

## E03-03 — The crystal changes its surroundings

### Narration

Keep watching the same crystal. As it grows, more water vapor becomes ice than returns to the air.
The solid edge creeps outward while the nearby dots thin out. Water from farther away wanders back
in, but not quickly enough to keep the two regions equal. The air close to the ice is left with less
spare vapor than the air farther away.

A crowd of moving dots helps with the journey, but it hides the larger pattern. Let the dots fade and
look straight down at the same crystal. Now color the surrounding air by how much spare vapor it holds.
Lighter means more; darker means less. The dark area near the ice is its depleted halo—a smooth change
in supply, not a shadow or a hard shell.

After a short settling time, that pattern can persist while the crystal keeps growing. The supply
changes as you move away from the surface. That change with distance is a gradient. Net diffusion
carries vapor inward through it until delivery balances the crystal's net uptake. Individual molecules
still travel both ways. The map shows the average result of all those paths, not the route of any one.

## E03-04 — Seeing an invisible halo indirectly

### Narration

How could anyone check a halo made of invisible vapor? Look down through a microscope at a laboratory
crystal. The faceted plate hangs just above the glass. Farther out, the glass is fogged with tiny round
liquid droplets. A clear band surrounds the ice, with no droplets in it.

Follow the edge of that clear band. Outside it, conditions allowed liquid droplets to condense. Inside,
the vapor density was lower. So the edge marks where droplet formation turned on. It gives us one
visible boundary, not a photograph of the whole vapor field. And it does not show droplets forming
near the ice and later evaporating.

Here is a calculation of the water vapor around a crystal. Its colors show less nearby and more farther
away. The clear band and the calculated low-supply region both bend around the ice. They come from
different crystals, so they are not an exact match. But the droplets give us a visible clue: growing
ice changes the air around it.

## E03-05 — Two waits in series

### Narration

Let's make the shape simple: a ball of ice. This is a teaching example, not the shape of our dendrite.
Keep the temperature and the water supply farther away the same. Water first has to reach the ice.
Then it has to become part of it. Those are two different steps. Which one can hold growth back?

First make it easy for water reaching the ice to become part of it. Replacements still have to wander
through the air. In this first case, which step holds everything up? The air-delivery step limits the
growth. Now the nearby supply thins, and a thin rim advances beyond the starting outline. A willing
surface can use vapor faster than diffusion replaces it, so growth goes only as fast as new vapor
arrives.

Start again from the same outline. This time make it harder for delivered water to become ice. Over
the same interval, the air near the surface stays closer to the far-away supply, but the added rim is
smaller. This time the surface is the slower step.

Together these two cases show the contrast. The easy surface leaves less vapor nearby and grows more;
the reluctant surface leaves more vapor nearby but grows less. When water becomes ice, it also releases
heat, and that heat has to escape. We leave heat out here because, in the source's usual cases, slow
vapor delivery matters more—not because heat is absent. But real crystals are not usually round. What
changes when we give this one corners?

## E03-06 — Shape redirects the supply

### Narration

Keep the same ice ball and the same supply of water in the air. In our diagram, give the ball six
corners. Look first at one projecting corner. It reaches farther toward less-depleted air. Then look at
the middle of one flat face. It sits farther back inside the field shaped by the whole crystal.

Now add a few lines to map the surrounding supply. Every point on one line marks the same amount of
spare vapor, and the amount changes by the same step from one line to the next. Scientists call each
line a contour. Near the corner, the contours crowd into a shorter distance; near the face center,
they spread farther apart. Close contours mean the supply changes more over the same distance—a
steeper gradient.

Now watch delivery along those two routes. More water is delivered to the corner than to the face
center in this schematic comparison. The color and lines show how much spare vapor is present; the
moving markers show its net flow. Those readings are related, but they are not the same. This geometry
can favor exposed corners and protruding regions over sheltered ones. It is not by itself an
explanation of branching; the surface response and local conditions still matter too.

## E03-07 — When the traffic reverses

### Narration

So far the far-away air has carried extra vapor. Make it slightly too dry for ice instead. Molecules
now leave the solid and the net diffusive traffic points outward. That direct change from ice to
water vapor is sublimation. It uses the same transport physics with the sign reversed.

But the shape history does not simply run backward. The physicist Jon Nelson tested that idea by
growing and then sublimating individual ice crystals under controlled conditions. That makes the
experiment a useful comparison of addition and loss on the same kind of object.

During sublimation, the crystals rounded and their measured loss rates followed a diffusion-limited
calculation. Nearly spheroidal shapes retained the aspect ratio reached at the end of growth: a plate
remained broadly plate-like and a column broadly column-like while edges disappeared. A literal
rewind would retrace the earlier growing outlines; these shrinking crystals rounded instead. Same
transport equation, different surface process.

- Keep the E03-06 silhouette and invert only the far-field condition and net arrows. Then show a
  labeled explanatory reconstruction of Nelson's measured rounding sequence. Do not present the
  reconstruction as archival footage or visualize it as a reversed growth animation.
- Keep the experimental ranges in the reader rather than loading the main visual with them. The

## E03-08 — Moving through the supply

### Narration

Still air was our clean starting case. A falling crystal also moves relative to the surrounding air.
That flow can carry less-depleted air toward the crystal and sweep depleted air away. This refresh
of the supply is called ventilation; it changes delivery through the air, not the ice lattice.

Compare the same schematic crystal in the same far-away conditions: still relative air on the left,
moving relative air on the right. Which side should show the steeper near-surface gradient and the
greater delivery? Hold the surface response fixed before you choose. The moving-air case is fed
faster. That answer is a tendency, not a universal percentage or a new rule for crystal habit. In
many cases diffusion and surface kinetics still dominate.

Free-fall cloud-tunnel experiments show why we should not erase the effect. Takahashi and colleagues
grew crystals while they fell through nominally water-saturated cloud air. Some branching plates
showed ventilation effects; compact forms showed no enhancement, and needles gave no clear effect.
Relative flow can matter, but how much depends on size and shape.

- Split one persistent hex silhouette into matched still/moving cases. Hold far-field supply, size
  and surface response constant. In the moving case, deform streamlines and contours before revealing
  a larger delivery gauge. Do not animate the lattice changing under the wind.

## E03-09 — Arrival is not joining

### Narration

Let's replay the route in order. One molecule zigzags randomly through still air. Add many two-way
paths, and their imbalance produces net delivery toward the ice. Let the crystal grow: the nearby
supply thins and a gradient forms. Give the crystal corners and delivery favors exposed places; move
air past it and fresh supply reaches closer. None of this steers one molecule. It changes what the
crowd does.

Stay with that same crystal and zoom to one flat face. Water can reach this neighborhood without
becoming part of the ice. Some arriving molecules join the solid, while others leave again. What
matters is the net result of many encounters, not the fate of one drawn dot.

That gives us the answer: growth has two waits. The air controls how much water reaches the face. The
surface controls how much becomes net added ice. Either one can slow growth. Yet molecules arrive from
a jumble of directions and the face stays flat. How can a growing face stay flat?

## Reader depth retained, not discarded

### Random motion, a field and a net flux

Diffusion does not give a water molecule a preferred microscopic direction. Collisions with the
much more abundant nitrogen and oxygen molecules continually scramble its velocity. A concentration
or supersaturation gradient changes the statistics of many such paths: more walkers start on the
crowded side than on the sparse side, so the crossing counts do not cancel. `Net inward` therefore
describes a population average while individual molecules continue to cross both ways. In a moving
air mass, advection joins diffusion; the episode begins with still relative air so those ideas are
not silently mixed.

Supersaturation is a field, written as a value at each position, rather than one label for an entire
cloud. At fixed temperature it can be expressed as `(c - c_sat) / c_sat`, where `c` is local vapor
number density and `c_sat` is the equilibrium value over flat ice. A growing surface is a sink for
vapor in the net bookkeeping sense. The surrounding field can settle much faster than the crystal's
shape changes—the source gives a characteristic diffusion time proportional to `R²/D`, with
`D ≈ 10⁻⁵ m²/s` for water vapor in typical atmospheric air—so a quasi-steady depleted halo is often
a useful approximation. Latent heat must also diffuse away; Chapter 4 argues that particle diffusion
is usually the larger correction in its default cases, but explicitly treats that omission as an
approximation.

Sources: `docs/education/chapters/04-the-fuel-supply.html#the-last-millimetre` and
[Libbrecht, *Snow Crystals*, sections 3.1 and 3.5](https://arxiv.org/abs/1910.06389).

### The ideal-sphere calculation behind the two doors

For a sphere of radius `R` in an effectively infinite reservoir with fixed far-field supersaturation
`sigmaInfinity`, the steady solution is
`sigma(r) = sigmaInfinity - (R/r)(sigmaInfinity - sigmaSurface)`. Matching diffusion to the
Hertz–Knudsen surface law gives
`sigmaSurface / sigmaInfinity = (X0/R) / (alphaHK + X0/R)` and a normalized growth factor
`alphaHK(X0/R) / (alphaHK + X0/R)`. Here `alphaHK` is an effective attachment coefficient for net
surface growth, not a direct count of single molecular collisions, and `X0/R` is the dimensionless
diffusive conductance for this geometry.

The reader's numerical version uses the source's `X0 ≈ 0.145 μm` at −15 °C and chooses `R = 10 μm`, so
`X0/R = 0.0145`. With the illustrative willing-surface limit `alphaHK = 1`, the surface ratio and
combined factor are both `0.0145 / 1.0145 ≈ 0.0143`: diffusion is the narrow door. With the equally
illustrative `alphaHK = 0.001`, the surface ratio is `0.0145 / 0.0155 ≈ 0.935`, while the combined
factor is about `0.000935`: the surface is the narrow door. These coefficients are teaching choices,
not measurements of a dendrite. The exact solution assumes a sphere, a fixed radius for the steady
field, fixed far-away conditions and no geometric competition between tips and recesses. A growing
non-spherical crystal requires geometry factors or a three-dimensional field solution.

Sources: `docs/education/chapters/04-the-fuel-supply.html#two-bottlenecks-in-series`,
`docs/education/chapters/05-the-restless-surface.html#the-attachment-coefficient` and
[Libbrecht, *Snow Crystals*, equations 3.16–3.24 and table 2.1](https://arxiv.org/abs/1910.06389).

### Sublimation as an independent transport check

Nelson's 1998 experiment formed and sublimated roughly one hundred individual ice crystals on a fine
glass capillary. The reported range was −18 to −0.1 °C and 0.05–5% undersaturation, with temperature
controlled to ±0.03 °C and some crystals cycled repeatedly between growth and sublimation. The
crystals became rounded, approximately spheroidal shapes; their aspect ratios remained approximately
the values reached at the end of growth, and diffusion calculations reproduced the measured size
loss. Nelson concluded that sublimation in these conditions was diffusion limited and consistent
with an attachment coefficient near one.

This is evidence against treating sublimation footage as a growth movie played backward. It is not
evidence that every round atmospheric particle has sublimated; frozen droplets can also be round.
Nor does outward net transport mean each molecule only moves outward. The individual motion remains
random while the population flux changes sign.

Sources: `docs/education/chapters/04-the-fuel-supply.html#the-mirror-experiment` and
[Nelson, “Sublimation of Ice Crystals,” *Journal of the Atmospheric Sciences* 55 (1998)](https://doi.org/10.1175/1520-0469%281998%29055%3C0910:SOIC%3E2.0.CO;2).

### Ventilation is conditional, not a universal multiplier

Only air motion relative to the crystal refreshes its boundary layer. The source's ideal-sphere
ventilation factors and one representative fern estimate make the default claim modest: near that
fern's first branching the estimated correction was about one percent, while mature sharp tips were
estimated to grow roughly 25% faster at terminal fall. Neither value is a general multiplier for a
dendrite, and neither predicts habit by itself.

Takahashi, Endoh, Wakahama and Fukuta supplied a distinct experimental regime in 1991. Their vertical
supercooled cloud tunnel kept individual crystals in free fall at water saturation for up to thirty
minutes over −3 to −23 °C. They reported ventilation effects for morphologically changing plate-like
crystals grown near −12, −14.5 and −16.5 °C, no enhancement for compact forms, and no clear effect for
needles near −5.5 °C. The result shows that relative flow can materially affect some freely falling
crystals; it does not erase the coupled roles of diffusion, geometry and surface kinetics.

Sources: `docs/education/chapters/04-the-fuel-supply.html#falling-through-the-supply` and
[Takahashi et al., *Journal of the Meteorological Society of Japan* 69 (1991)](https://doi.org/10.2151/jmsj1965.69.1_15).
