# E04 — How a growing face stays flat

English authority draft for a nine-scene episode, 2026-09-20. It answers E03's closing question and
remains held from public release. This file separates spoken narration, visual instructions and
optional reader depth. The opening uses the retained `named-solid-columns-baseline` computer-grown
recording as an example of a blocky shape, not a measured crystal or molecular simulation. The later
surfaces, steps, islands, growth envelopes and supply profiles are explanatory diagrams; their sizes,
rates, curvature and particle counts are not measurements.

The central takeaway is that slowly advancing surface directions remain visible as facets, and that
moving molecular steps can compensate for unequal local vapor supply so a microscopically concave
face advances nearly evenly. This is the qualitative mechanism described by Kenneth Libbrecht in
*Snow Crystals*, printed pp. 79–80, not an independently solved surface-transport model. Primary source
passages checked for this draft include printed pp. 53–54, 79–81 and 139–142 of arXiv:1910.06389v2,
alongside education Chapters 5, 6 and 11. English narration production, Chinese semantic review,
performed timing, complete viewing, listening and audience understanding are not established by this
draft.

## E04-02 — A ledge changes the odds

### Narration

Look along the surface in our drawing. It has a flat stretch, then a tiny ledge, then another flat
stretch. Each flat stretch belongs to a molecular layer. It is called a terrace. The ledge where a
layer ends is a step. We have made its height enormous so we can see it.

An arriving molecule can linger on a terrace and leave again. Beside a step, it can find more nearby
molecules to bind to, making incorporation into the ice easier. These are different opportunities,
not a promise that every molecule at a ledge stays. Real surfaces keep exchanging molecules with
the air, and their outermost molecules are much less rigid than this tidy drawing.

Now watch the ledge move sideways as new material is added at its edge. The old ice stays where it
was. Behind the moving ledge, the surface is one layer thicker. Repeated steps can therefore move a
face outward without making the whole solid slide. From far away, all those tiny additions look
like a smooth face advancing.

## E04-03 — The slow faces remain

### Narration

That difference between surfaces changes the shape of the whole crystal. To make the geometry easy
to follow, use this simplified cross-section with six main sides and small cut-off corners. It is
a teaching shape, not a growth simulation. Keep the starting material marked while we give the six
main surface directions a slow outward speed and the directions between them a faster one.

Both kinds add material. For this comparison, their chosen speeds stay fixed. Look at a short,
fast-moving surface between two slower neighbors. As every boundary moves outward, which surfaces
will remain on the outside?

The slower faces remain. The faster surface advances until it meets its neighbors and no longer
forms part of the outer boundary. Its added ice has not vanished; that material is still inside the
larger shape. Slow does not mean stopped. In ice, the favored directions come from its own lattice,
and this difference in growth rates helps leave the familiar flat faces. A growing crystal's shape
is not explained simply by saying that it chooses the lowest surface energy.

## E04-04 — Starting the next layer

### Narration

Return to one terrace on our ice face. An existing step gives new material an edge to join. But
suppose this patch has no step nearby. How does the next layer get started? A few loosely attached
molecules can meet and form a tiny island of new ice on top of the old layer.

Most very small islands break apart again. Making an island also makes a new edge, and that edge
costs energy. For a small island, the cost is large compared with the amount of new ice. Under the
same growing conditions, a sufficiently large island is more likely to keep growing. Its edge can
then spread across the terrace, leaving another layer behind.

The start of a new stable island is called terrace nucleation. More spare vapor at the surface can
make it easier, so new layers begin more readily. We are following one island to see the process;
several can be growing at once. This is also not the only source of steps: some crystal defects can
keep supplying them. The important point is that a face's growth depends on both starting steps
and moving the steps already there.

- Make an explicit magnification back to the same selected face. First identify a terrace patch
  without nearby steps, then change to a top-down island view that carries the established
  new-material color and old-layer texture. This viewpoint change restates known layer addition
  before introducing island stability.
- Show a small island appearing and losing members, then a separate larger island expanding under
  the same conditions. These are qualitative representative histories, not one deterministic
  molecular-size threshold. Introduce the name only on `terrace nucleation`.
- Show the additional spare-vapor condition before increasing the frequency of new stable islands.

## E04-05 — Unequal supply, one face

### Narration

Zoom back out to our blocky hexagonal crystal and select the same side face. Hold the temperature
and the vapor supply farther away steady. Growing ice depletes the air beside it. In this example,
the projecting corners have slightly more spare vapor beside them than the middle of the face.

Compare those locations before adding any new mechanism. More vapor is available near a corner;
less is available near the middle. If the surface responded equally everywhere, that difference
would let the corners advance faster. The middle would be left behind, and our straight-looking
face would begin to dish inward.

So why does the difference not keep getting larger? The surface response does not have to stay
equal. The steps we just met can change it. Keep this particular supply pattern in view: it is a
blocky-crystal example, not a rule that every crystal has its richest air at the corners.

## E04-06 — Steps move inward

### Narration

Stay with that same face, but enlarge its tiny ledges again. In the mechanism described by snow
crystal physicist Kenneth Libbrecht, new layers tend to start near the corners, where the local
spare vapor is higher. Follow one of their step edges as it spreads inward toward the face's middle.

Near the corner, the step has more vapor available and moves faster. Farther inward, it has less
and moves more slowly. Now add successive steps. They start quickly, then slow as they approach the
middle, so the step edges become more closely spaced there. The middle gains more step edge in
the same amount of surface.

Those moving lines mark the edges of growing layers. They are not pieces of old ice sliding along
the face, and they are not a stream of water molecules traveling all the way from corner to
center. New material joins locally as each step advances. The changing spacing gives the middle
more opportunities to incorporate the water available beside it.

### Visual and sources

- Carry the E04-05 face, corner/center markers and supply colors into an explicitly exaggerated
  molecular close-up. Keep the macroscopic parent face visible as a small reference.
- Create one step near a corner only on `new layers tend to start near the corners`. Follow its
  inward travel first; show speed decreasing with position before introducing additional steps.
- On `Now add successive steps`, introduce a spaced sequence and let it compress toward the middle.
  Draw inward propagation from both ends only once the one-side mechanism has meaning. Preserve a
  center gap or merge rule so steps do not simply pass through each other and reverse order.
- Layers are deposited in place behind the advancing step; old material and molecular-row guides
  remain stationary. No tracer crosses the entire facet as the compensating mechanism. Closely
  spaced steps are a qualitative schematic of figure 3.4, not a solved step-flow trajectory.

## E04-07 — Less supply, more opportunities

### Narration

We now have two parts of the same face to compare. The corner has more spare vapor beside it.
The middle has less. Keep the temperature fixed, and remember what happened to the moving steps:
they became more closely spaced toward the middle. What could help the middle keep up?

More closely spaced steps give the middle more places to add ice. Each patch's advance depends
on both the local spare vapor and how readily its surface incorporates it. In this steady-growth
picture, the middle's stronger response compensates for its smaller supply. At the corners the
pairing goes the other way: more supply, fewer step opportunities.

Now follow the whole face as it advances against its old outline. The two effects can balance,
so the face moves outward at nearly the same rate along its length. It has not become perfectly
flat at the molecular scale. It keeps a very shallow inward dish, enlarged enormously in our
close-up. Seen at the scale of the crystal, that tiny shape looks flat.

## E04-08 — A correction with a limit

### Narration

The balance can correct a small change. First imagine the face is a little too flat for these
conditions. The corners get ahead, making the inward dish a little deeper. As steps crowd toward
the middle, its response increases. Now imagine the face is too deeply dished. The middle can
advance faster and catch up, reducing the excess curvature.

That is a negative feedback: a small departure changes the growth in a way that tends to reduce
the departure. Nothing measures the face with a ruler. Local supply and local surface response
produce the correction. The stable shape depends on the crystal's size, its growth conditions and
how its surface behaves; it is not one universal curve.

And the correction has a limit. A surface cannot keep increasing its effective growth response
without bound. If the middle can no longer increase its response enough to keep pace with the
corners, the nearly flat face can fail. Faster growth or a larger crystal can make branching more
likely, but there is no single size or vapor level that switches every crystal into a star.

### Visual and sources

- Keep the same facet and temperature/supply reference. Explicitly mark the first initial profile
  `too flat`, then let the corners add material faster until the shallow reference curvature is
  restored. Only then introduce a separate `too concave` initial state and let the center catch up.
  Both repair paths add ice; do not erase a bump as if sublimation had been introduced.
- These are explanatory perturbation cases, not recorded oscillations or a solved dynamic law.
  Preserve the nearly flat macroscopic parent; enlarge the microscopic deviation locally.
- Name `negative feedback` only after both directional corrections are visible. Retire that label
  before introducing the response limit. Show increased central response reaching a qualitative
  ceiling, then a growing corner/center difference; do not draw mature branches yet.
- The ceiling belongs to the effective Hertz–Knudsen response under the source's local model, not

## E04-09 — When the correction fails

### Narration

Let's bring the story back to one face. A step gives arriving water more places to join. As the
step spreads, it leaves a new layer behind. Across the whole crystal, the slower-growing directions
remain visible as flat faces. Starting and moving new steps keeps those faces growing.

On our blocky crystal, the corners have more spare vapor nearby. Steps move inward and crowd
toward the middle, where more attachment opportunities can compensate for less supply. A tiny
inward curve helps the face advance nearly evenly. That is how a growing face can look flat
without being motionless or perfectly flat molecule by molecule.

But what happens once that correction is no longer enough? Keep one corner in view. If it begins
to get ahead, can the surrounding air help it grow farther ahead still? In the next episode, we
will explore the moment a corner becomes a branch.

## Reader depth retained, not discarded

### What the drawings leave out at molecular scale

A terrace step is a boundary between successive growth layers, not a macroscopic stair cut into
the crystal. Libbrecht's figure 2.9 gives a basal-layer spacing of 0.37 nm and a prism-layer spacing
of 0.39 nm. A nanometer is one billionth of a meter. The basal spacing refers to a molecular
bilayer; a screen row must not be read as a universal one-molecule-thick layer for both directions.
Our selected side face is a prism face, but the episode uses a general step cartoon rather than
an atom-by-atom lattice model.

The rigid neighbor picture is useful for understanding why a step can offer stronger binding than
an open terrace. It does not resolve every molecular process. The source warns that real ice
surfaces rearrange, exchange material rapidly and can exhibit surface premelting. Surface motion
over local distances contributes to the effective attachment response. Neither the cartoon nor
the flat-face account assumes that water migrates from a macroscopic corner across the full face
to feed its center. The inward-moving object in E04-06 is a growing layer edge.

### The product behind equal advance

The source expresses local growth normal to a surface as

`v_n = alphaHK × v_kin × sigma_surf`.

Here `v_n` is the speed at which the boundary advances perpendicular to itself, in meters per
second. `v_kin` is a reference speed, also in meters per second, set by temperature in the kinetic
description. `sigma_surf` is the fractional vapor surplus immediately beside that patch relative
to ice equilibrium at that temperature. It is dimensionless: 0.01 means a one-percent surplus.
`alphaHK` is the dimensionless Hertz–Knudsen attachment coefficient, summarizing the local net
surface response. It is not the project's separate G–G attachment threshold, and a set of drawn
collisions is not a measurement of it.

At fixed temperature, nearly equal `v_n` requires nearly equal `alphaHK × sigma_surf` along the
face. An illustrative calculation makes the compensation explicit. If the corner has
`sigma_surf = 0.020` and `alphaHK = 0.20`, their product is 0.0040. A center with
`sigma_surf = 0.010` and `alphaHK = 0.40` has the same product and hence the same local speed
`0.0040 × v_kin`. These are chosen teaching values, not measured profiles, a numerical solution,
or a transferable condition for a real crystal. The field and kinetics must be solved together
to predict actual growth.

In the source's local formulation `alphaHK` is bounded above by one. If the center's required
response reaches that limit, it cannot compensate by increasing indefinitely. This explains the
limit of the qualitative regulator, but it does not predict a universal branching threshold.
Crystal size, surrounding transport and the dependence of surface response on local conditions
all enter that question, continued in Episode 5.

### Why an island has a barrier, and how a defect changes the question

At fixed growth conditions, building an island gains material in proportion to its area, while
creating its rim costs energy in proportion to its perimeter. The edge cost therefore matters
especially strongly for small islands. In the simplified circular-island theory, a critical radius
separates islands that usually disappear from islands that are more likely to persist and grow.
Raising the local supersaturation lowers that radius. The critical size is not a universal count
of water molecules or a guarantee about an individual fluctuation.

The representative estimate on printed p. 142 uses a step energy near `10^-12 J/m` and a surface
supersaturation near one percent, yielding a critical radius of roughly ten molecular size units
and approximately 300 molecules in the island. That is a radius estimate under chosen typical
parameters; it is not a diameter of ten molecules. Exact barriers, prefactors, temperature
dependence and their inference from growth measurements belong to Episode 9.

Terrace nucleation is particularly relevant to a broad facet without an existing step source.
A screw dislocation can instead supply a persistent spiral step, permitting continued growth
without repeatedly creating new islands. Many layers may also be growing simultaneously on one
facet. Following one island in E04-04 isolates an understandable event; it does not establish a
universal sequence in which every layer finishes before the next starts.

[Libbrecht, *Snow Crystals*, printed pp. 141–142 and 168](https://arxiv.org/abs/1910.06389).

### Why this is not a lowest-energy-shape argument

Surface free energy matters in crystal physics, including the cost of making a terrace edge.
But the equilibrium shape of a crystal and the shape it develops while continuously gaining
material are different questions. The monograph describes relatively weak directional variation
in ice surface energy alongside much stronger directional variation in attachment kinetics.
The large difference in growth rates supplies the mechanism for the surviving facets in E04-03.
The lesson is not that energy is irrelevant: step-edge energy helps control terrace nucleation,
which in turn influences growth response.

The geometry illustration prescribes direction-dependent speeds and follows their envelope.
It is deliberately not a vapor-field solution and supplies no physical time scale. Six slow
planes alternate with six faster planes that initially cut off the corners. Their moving
intersection becomes a hexagon as the fast segments leave the boundary. Libbrecht's Caltech
faceting explanation uses a fourfold teaching shape to expose the same general geometry; this
episode retains a sixfold reference to reduce the visual change. The chosen speed ratios are
illustrative rather than measured ice kinetics.

### The corner-supply pattern is conditional

E04 uses the blocky-prism case of the source's figure 3.4. It must not be applied to every
crystal by silhouette alone. Figure 3.29 compares a blocky crystal with similar basal and prism
attachment responses to a thin plate whose basal response is much smaller than its edge response.
In the thin-plate calculation the highest surface supersaturation lies near the centers of the
basal faces, while the edge has a lower value. Shape, surface response and the vapor field are
coupled. Protrusion alone does not determine every local vapor value, and high local vapor
concentration is not identical to high net flux.
