Glossary

Every technical word and symbol this site uses, each in one plain sentence, with a link to the chapter that explains it properly.

The entries are grouped by what they are about rather than by chapter, and they run alphabetically inside each group. The link on every entry points at the place the term is explained, which is not always the first place it appears — several words are used in passing early on and unpacked properly much later, and the link takes you to the unpacking.

If you only came here for one thing

It is probably a symbol. σ is spare vapour, α is stickiness, σ0 is how hard a face is to start growing on, A is the ceiling that α climbs to, and vkin is the reference speed the other two get multiplied by. They are set out with the rest under the symbols, in one place.

One sentence is not the whole story and is not meant to be. Each definition is a compression of an argument a chapter makes at length, written so that a word you have half forgotten becomes usable again in about ten seconds. Where a definition rests on a number, the number is the one the site quotes from its sources; the references page says where every one of them came from.

The object: what falls out of the sky

What the words for snow, ice and cloud actually pick out.
TermIn one sentenceExplained in
artificial snow Ski-resort snow — water blasted through nozzles and frozen in mid-air — which comes down as small featureless globules of ice rather than as anything grown from vapour. 10 · How We Know
cloud droplet A microscopic drop of liquid water, about 10 to 20 microns across, condensed onto a speck of dust; a cloud is an enormous number of these, not vapour. 1 · Not a Frozen Raindrop
clumping (aggregation) Crystals colliding and sticking to each other on the way down, which is what makes the grey puffballs of mild weather; it happens after the shape is decided, so it records how crowded the fall was and nothing about growth. 9 · The Menagerie
deposition Water going straight from gas to solid with no liquid stage in between — the only way the body of a snow crystal is ever built. 1 · Not a Frozen Raindrop
dew point The temperature at which cooling air starts carrying more water vapour than it can hold on to, so the surplus condenses out — as dew on the grass, or as cloud droplets high up. 1 · Not a Frozen Raindrop
graupel (soft hail) A crystal so completely buried under frozen droplets that whatever shape was underneath has disappeared. 1 · Not a Frozen Raindrop
irregulars Small, somewhat malformed plate-like crystals, usually stuck to each other — “icy grains of sand” — and far more common than every photogenic type put together. 9 · The Menagerie
metastable Stuck in a state that is not the one it would settle into if it could, with no way to get there; a supercooled droplet is metastable liquid. 1 · Not a Frozen Raindrop
nucleation Getting the very first patch of something new started — the hard first step that makes a droplet freeze, and later the hard first step that begins a new molecular layer on a flat face. 1 · Not a Frozen Raindrop
rime (riming) Supercooled cloud droplets that hit a falling crystal and froze where they landed — weight without pattern, a crystal wearing a coat rather than a bigger crystal. 1 · Not a Frozen Raindrop
sleet A raindrop that froze solid on the way down: a bead of ice, and precisely the thing a snow crystal is not. 1 · Not a Frozen Raindrop
snow crystal A single crystal of ice — one continuous hexagonal array of water molecules with no seams and no joins — and the only object here that has six-fold symmetry to explain. 1 · Not a Frozen Raindrop
snowflake A weather word rather than a physics word: it can mean one snow crystal, or a clump of hundreds of them, which is why a snowflake need have no symmetry at all. 1 · Not a Frozen Raindrop
sublimation Solid going straight to vapour, the exact reverse of deposition; it attacks sharp features first, which is what rounds off a crystal that has travelled through dry air. 3 · Why Six Sides
supercooled Liquid, below its own freezing point, and staying that way because freezing has to start somewhere — the ordinary condition of a winter cloud, not a laboratory curiosity. 1 · Not a Frozen Raindrop
synthetic (snow crystal) A real snow crystal grown from vapour indoors under conditions somebody chose — “synthetic” meaning made on purpose, not fake. 10 · How We Know
travel-worn The softened, rounded look of a crystal that has spent time in air drier than it needed and lost molecules back to the air. 9 · The Menagerie

The ice crystal: lattice, axes and facets

The stack of water molecules that fixes the six, and the vocabulary for its faces, directions and defects.
TermIn one sentenceExplained in
a-axes The six directions lying in the flat hexagonal face and pointing out at its corners — where the six arms of a stellar crystal eventually grow. 3 · Why Six Sides
arrowhead twin Two crystals meeting at a mirror plane and growing into a flat pointed blade whose apex angle — 78, 57 or 89 degrees for the three known variants — is fixed by the lattice and is an angle no hexagon can make. 9 · The Menagerie
basal facet Either of the two flat ends of a hexagonal prism, written {0001} — the top and bottom of a plate. 1 · Not a Frozen Raindrop
Bernal–Fowler rules The 1933 rule that exactly one hydrogen atom sits between each adjacent pair of oxygen atoms in ice — never none, never two. 3 · Why Six Sides
c-axis The single direction running through the two flat ends, perpendicular to the basal face, the way the lead runs in a pencil — and the only direction from which the hexagonal rings are visible. 3 · Why Six Sides
columnar twin Two ordinary hexagonal columns fused end to end across a shared twin plane, betrayed from the outside only by a groove round the waist. 3 · Why Six Sides
covalent bond One of the two tight bonds holding a water molecule’s own two hydrogens onto its oxygen. 3 · Why Six Sides
crossed-plate twin Two plate-like crystals joined so that they pass through each other at an angle the lattice fixes in advance. 9 · The Menagerie
crystallography The study of how molecules stack in a solid — the lattice, its symmetry, its spacings — which has been settled for ice since the 1920s and is not the same subject as crystal growth. 2 · Four Hundred Years of Looking
deuterium and oxygen-18 Heavier-than-usual versions of hydrogen and oxygen, scattered at random through ordinary water — about one molecule in 5,000 and one in 500 — which is why no two crystals match molecule for molecule. 8 · A Snowflake Is a Record
dislocation A place where the molecules did not stack together with perfect regularity, scattered at random through a crystal. 8 · A Snowflake Is a Record
evaporation groove The pinched line round a twinned column’s waist, where the weaker twin plane gives up molecules faster than the ordinary ice on either side of it. 3 · Why Six Sides
facet A flat face, the sort of surface you see on a cut gemstone. 1 · Not a Frozen Raindrop
hexagonal prism The simplest solid ice can take — two flat ends and six flat sides — and the block every snow crystal, star or column, is built on. 1 · Not a Frozen Raindrop
hydrogen bond One of the two weaker bonds reaching from an oxygen atom across to a hydrogen belonging to a neighbouring molecule. 3 · Why Six Sides
ice Ic The cubic alternative to ordinary ice — the same tetrahedral bonding with one connecting bond twisted, giving diamond’s arrangement instead of a hexagon; nobody has ever definitively observed it pure. 3 · Why Six Sides
ice Ih Ordinary hexagonal ice, the only form commonly found on the Earth’s surface and the one every snow crystal is made of; the “h” is for hexagonal. 3 · Why Six Sides
lattice The repeating three-dimensional pattern molecules settle into when every one of them bonds to its neighbours the same way, at the same angle, over and over. 3 · Why Six Sides
lattice constants The two measured lengths from which every angle in ice follows: a0 = 0.452 nm across one repeat of the honeycomb, and c0 = 0.736 nm along the c-axis. 3 · Why Six Sides
layer spacing How much thicker or wider a crystal gets each time it completes one molecular layer: 0.37 nm on a flat end, 0.39 nm on a side wall. 3 · Why Six Sides
Miller–Bravais indices The four-digit addresses crystallographers give to planes and directions, where curly brackets name a family of faces, square brackets name a direction, and a bar over a digit means minus. 3 · Why Six Sides
phase diagram A map you read by picking a temperature and a pressure and looking up which state of matter wins there; every snow crystal ever made occupies one short arc of the ice/vapour line. 3 · Why Six Sides
prism facet Any of the six flat side walls running round the waist of a hexagonal prism, written {1̄010}. 1 · Not a Frozen Raindrop
pyramidal facet A rare third kind of flat face, slanting across the corner between an end and a side wall at an angle the lattice fixes at exactly 28.0 degrees from the prism facet. 3 · Why Six Sides
secondary prism surface The {112̄0} planes, each perpendicular to an a-axis, on which growing ice has never been observed to form facets at all. 3 · Why Six Sides
stacking disordered A crystal that changes its mind between hexagonal and cubic stacking from layer to layer — every bond perfectly satisfied, so it is locally fine and globally undecided. 3 · Why Six Sides
tetrahedral angle 109.5 degrees, the angle four directions make when they spread as evenly as four directions can in three dimensions; water’s own bond angle of 104.5 degrees misses it by five, and that near miss is what forces the hexagon. 3 · Why Six Sides
triple point The single temperature and pressure — 273.16 K and 611.657 Pa — at which ice, liquid water and vapour can all coexist; snow crystals grow on the ice/vapour line below it. 3 · Why Six Sides
twinning (twin plane) Two single crystals grown together in a fixed orientation across one shared internal plane, where the usual hexagonal bonds have been replaced by a plane of cubic ones. 3 · Why Six Sides
unit cell The smallest block of the lattice that repeats over and over to build the whole crystal. 9 · The Menagerie

The vapour: supply, saturation and diffusion

The fuel, how much of it there is, and how it gets to the crystal.
TermIn one sentenceExplained in
Arrhenius form The standard way of writing a process that carries an energy price tag, so its rate falls away exponentially as the temperature drops. 4 · The Fuel Supply
Berg effect The fact that the corners of a blocky crystal, sticking furthest out into air that has not been picked over, sit at a higher supersaturation than the middles of its faces do. 4 · The Fuel Supply
Boltzmann’s constant The conversion factor between temperature and energy per molecule, written k. 4 · The Fuel Supply
depleted halo The permanent shell of dried-out air a growing crystal has eaten out of its own surroundings — a few times wider than the crystal, and re-formed around every new bump the instant that bump appears. 4 · The Fuel Supply
diffusion Random jostling that, purely by statistics, moves molecules from crowded regions towards empty ones; nothing steers them and no wind is required. 4 · The Fuel Supply
diffusion constant How quickly diffusion shifts material — about 10−5 m2/s for water molecules in air, which makes short journeys quick and long ones very slow indeed. 4 · The Fuel Supply
diffusion-limited growth Growth held back by how fast the air can deliver vapour rather than by how willing the ice is to take it. 4 · The Fuel Supply
excess water vapour The surplus counted in grams per cubic metre instead of as a percentage — the quantity a crystal actually eats, and, unlike the percentage, one that peaks near −12 °C and falls away on both sides. 4 · The Fuel Supply
latent heat The energy bill for freeing one molecule from the ice — and the same energy handed back as warmth every time a molecule joins. 4 · The Fuel Supply
relative humidity Spare vapour measured against liquid water, which is why air at 100 per cent humidity already carries a 15.7 per cent surplus as far as ice is concerned at −15 °C. 4 · The Fuel Supply
saturated vapour pressure The amount of vapour at which molecules leave a surface exactly as fast as they land on it — a standoff, not a lid and not a full tank. 4 · The Fuel Supply
saturation The balance point at which a surface neither grows nor shrinks; below freezing the first question is always “saturated with respect to what?”, because ice and liquid water give different answers. 4 · The Fuel Supply
supersaturation How much spare water vapour the air is carrying, as a fraction of what ice can hold — written σ, and always measured against ice on this site. 4 · The Fuel Supply
water saturation line The ceiling drawn across the morphology diagram: push the humidity past it and droplets condense out and take up the surplus, so the supersaturation stops climbing. 7 · Plates, Columns, Plates, Columns
water vapour Individual water molecules flying around loose in the air, invisible and mixed in among the nitrogen and oxygen — the entire raw material of a snow crystal, and about one part in six hundred of the air. 4 · The Fuel Supply

The growing surface: steps, sticking and barriers

What happens in the top one or two molecular layers, where every arriving molecule is either kept or lost.
TermIn one sentenceExplained in
admolecule A molecule that has landed on the surface but has not yet locked into the lattice — skating about until it finds a step, or until heat throws it back into the air. 5 · The Restless Surface
anisotropy Being not the same in all directions; ice’s exceptionally strong directional preference about where molecules attach is what stops a dendrite tip from splitting. 5 · The Restless Surface
attachment coefficient The probability that a molecule striking this patch of surface actually joins the lattice and stays — written α, running from 0 to 1, and a property of the patch rather than of ice. 5 · The Restless Surface
attachment kinetics The whole collection of surface processes that decide whether a passing molecule sticks or bounces off. 5 · The Restless Surface
CAK model Comprehensive Attachment Kinetics — the agreed set of fitted σ0(T) and A(T) curves; bookkeeping for the measurements rather than a rival theory. 11 · The Stickiness of Ice
critical radius The size a newly formed island of ice on a facet must exceed to spread rather than fall apart — about ten molecular widths, some 300 molecules, at a surface supersaturation of one per cent. 5 · The Restless Surface
Ehrlich–Schwoebel barrier The energy penalty that makes it hard for an admolecule to hop down over the lip of the terrace it landed on, because for an instant it would be hanging off the crystal with almost nothing underneath it. 11 · The Stickiness of Ice
equilibrium crystal shape (ECS) The lowest-energy shape a crystal would settle into given unlimited time; for ice it has never been definitively measured, and a crystal a tenth of a millimetre across would need about eight days to reach it. 5 · The Restless Surface
faceting The survival of flat faces because they are the worst at growing: rough surfaces race outward until they collide with their neighbours and vanish from the outline, and the slow faces are what is left. 5 · The Restless Surface
frustrated QLL kinetics The proposed — and openly cartoon-level — explanation of why the prism ceiling sags below 1 near the melting point: that arriving molecules have to work down through a quasi-liquid film far more viscous than ordinary water. 11 · The Stickiness of Ice
Gibbs–Thomson effect The small correction that makes a strongly curved surface hold its molecules slightly less well — negligible almost everywhere, and decisive at the razor rim of a very thin plate. 5 · The Restless Surface
Hertz–Knudsen relation The growth law v = α vkin σsurf: speed equals stickiness, times a fixed conversion factor, times how much spare vapour is there. 11 · The Stickiness of Ice
molecularly rough / smooth A rough patch is jagged and full of half-finished bonding sites, so it catches nearly everything that hits it (α ≈ 1); a smooth facet is aligned with the lattice and catches very little. 5 · The Restless Surface
nucleation barrier The obstacle a flat face has to clear before it can grow at all: it must assemble a brand-new molecular island out of nothing, with every molecule in that island poorly held until the island is big enough to hold itself together. 5 · The Restless Surface
quasi-liquid layer (QLL) The disordered film that premelting leaves on an ice surface — not quite water and not quite ice — roughly one molecule deep near −10 °C and thickening without limit towards 0 °C. 5 · The Restless Surface
roughening transition The sharp switch from smooth to rough that the prism face was long expected to make near the melting point — and which the measurements do not show, since what they show is a gradual slide. 5 · The Restless Surface
screw dislocation A lattice flaw carrying a permanent spiral step that winds on for ever, so that face can keep advancing without ever having to start a new island. 11 · The Stickiness of Ice
statics and dynamics Statics is finding the lowest-energy arrangement of things sitting still, which science is good at and which is solved for ice; dynamics is enormous numbers of molecules arriving and leaving far from equilibrium, which is neither. 5 · The Restless Surface
step (terrace step) The ledge where one molecular terrace ends and the layer beneath it is exposed, exactly one layer high — and the only place a molecule reliably sticks, because there it is gripped from two sides instead of one. 5 · The Restless Surface
step energy The energy cost of creating one metre of terrace edge, written β; about 10−12 joules per metre for ice growing from vapour, far below the rigid-lattice prediction because real surface molecules relax into the gap. 5 · The Restless Surface
surface diffusion length How far an admolecule typically travels across a terrace before evaporating, written xdiff; if the steps sit closer together than this, nearly everything gets caught. 11 · The Stickiness of Ice
surface energy The cost of having a surface at all, per square metre, because every molecule on the boundary has unfulfilled bonds — real for ice, and far too even-handed between directions to explain faceting. 5 · The Restless Surface
surface premelting The loss of order in the topmost molecular layers of ice near the melting point while the crystal underneath stays perfectly solid — predicted by Faraday in 1859. 5 · The Restless Surface
terrace One flat molecular layer of the crystal, stacked on the ones below like a tread on a staircase. 5 · The Restless Surface
terrace nucleation The birth of a new molecular island on a bare facet — a lottery the face mostly loses, and the bottleneck that makes facets slow. 5 · The Restless Surface
vicinal surface A facet cut at a slight angle, so a regular staircase of steps crosses it; tilting a face by a degree or two can make it a hundred times stickier. 11 · The Stickiness of Ice
Wilson–Frenkel description The account of a solid growing from a liquid in which the sticking probability runs inversely to the liquid’s viscosity — the physics the frustrated-QLL story leans on. 11 · The Stickiness of Ice

Instability and branching: where the pattern comes from

The runaways that turn a plain hexagon into a star, and the ideas used to describe them.
TermIn one sentenceExplained in
branching instability (Mullins–Sekerka instability) The runaway in which any bump on a growing surface pokes into richer air, therefore grows faster, therefore pokes out further still — which is why a perfectly flat growing surface is a legal answer to the physics but not a stable one. 6 · The Runaway Bump
dendrite A branch growing outward like a tree, from the Greek for tree: a smooth travelling tip with sidebranches trailing behind it. 6 · The Runaway Bump
Edge-Sharpening Instability (ESI) The loop in which a sharpening edge lowers its own nucleation barrier, which sharpens it further, until the edge is one or two microns thick — proposed as what makes thin plates near −15 °C and hollow columns near −5 °C. 12 · Why the Shape Flips
fractal A shape that repeats at every scale for ever; a snow crystal nests only three or four levels deep, so calling one a fractal is a description with a floor rather than an explanation. 6 · The Runaway Bump
free dendrite A dendrite growing into open air, “free” meaning unconstrained by container walls or by competing crystals. 6 · The Runaway Bump
Ivantsov solution The 1947 solution of the diffusion equation describing a parabolic needle that advances at constant speed while its shape never changes — ride along with the tip and nothing in front of you appears to move at all. 6 · The Runaway Bump
Koch snowflake The mathematical curve drawn in 1904 with a finite area and an infinitely long perimeter, which looks like a snowflake and explains nothing about one. 6 · The Runaway Bump
Matthew effect Winners taking resources from losers and therefore winning harder — the name sometimes given to one sidebranch shooting ahead while its immediate neighbours are stunted. 6 · The Runaway Bump
mesoscopic The in-between scale: far bigger than one molecule, far smaller than the whole crystal. 12 · Why the Shape Flips
positive feedback Any loop whose output feeds back into its own input, so that a small advantage grows instead of evening out. 6 · The Runaway Bump
SDAK (structure-dependent attachment kinetics) The proposal that a facet only about 50 nanometres wide has a much lower effective nucleation barrier than a broad facet of the very same crystal plane, because molecules spilling in from its over-sharp corners crowd it — a working hypothesis, not a settled result. 12 · Why the Shape Flips
SDAK dip The band of temperatures over which a narrow facet’s barrier drops below the broad-facet curve — measured near −4 °C on the basal face and near −14 °C on the prism face, and the reason the shape can flip three times rather than once. 12 · Why the Shape Flips
seaweed The branched-but-shapeless structure you get when a growing crystal has no directional preference at all, so its tips split endlessly — just as intricate as a snowflake, and with no shape. 6 · The Runaway Bump
selection problem The fact that diffusion on its own fixes only the pairing of tip speed with tip radius, leaving a whole family of possible parabolas, so some extra physics has to pick one. 6 · The Runaway Bump
self-similarity Structure that looks the same at different magnifications; real in snow crystals but “relatively minor”, and not an explanation of how the structure got there. 6 · The Runaway Bump
sidebranch A branch sprouting from the flank of a larger branch, seeded by a chance bump — which is why the two sides of a single arm never match each other, and neither do any two arms. 6 · The Runaway Bump
tip radius The size of the roundest circle that fits inside a dendrite tip — about one micron, and essentially unchanged by how humid the air is. 6 · The Runaway Bump
tip splitting A dendrite tip dividing in two, which ice normally resists; when it does happen the tip divides sideways, staying inside the flat plane of the plate. 6 · The Runaway Bump

Shapes and habits: reading the map

The named forms, the one number that separates them, and the chart that says which grows where.
TermIn one sentenceExplained in
aspect ratio The whole shape in one number: how far the crystal reaches cap to cap divided by how far it reaches across — about 0.01 for a large thin plate, 20 or more for an especially slender needle. 7 · Plates, Columns, Plates, Columns
aspect-ratio rule Thin plates require αbasal ≪ αprism and slender columns require αbasal ≫ αprism — which turns a photograph of any crystal into a statement about its two families of face. 7 · Plates, Columns, Plates, Columns
capped column A stubby column with a flat plate grown on each end, like a cotton reel: the column grew near −5 °C and the plates later, after the crystal drifted somewhere colder. 2 · Four Hundred Years of Looking
column A crystal taller than it is wide, shaped like a hexagonal pencil, which is what grows when the two flat ends are the easy faces. 7 · Plates, Columns, Plates, Columns
fernlike stellar dendrite A stellar dendrite that has gone further still, carrying sidebranches on its sidebranches at 60 degrees — the largest snow crystals there are, and about a hundred times wider than they are thick. 9 · The Menagerie
fishbone dendrite A three-dimensional herringbone form needing supersaturations that do not occur in the sky, so it is entirely a laboratory creation and nobody has ever caught one outdoors. 7 · Plates, Columns, Plates, Columns
habit A crystal’s overall shape ignoring the decoration — plate, block, column or needle — so two crystals with wildly different ornament can share one. 7 · Plates, Columns, Plates, Columns
habit boundary (crossing) The temperature at which the basal and prism barrier curves swap order, so the faster face becomes the slower one and the crystal switches between plate and column. 12 · Why the Shape Flips
hollow column A column with a conical void driven into each end, formed when the rim of each flat cap outruns its own middle; the two pits match almost wiggle for wiggle, because both ends lived through the same weather. 7 · Plates, Columns, Plates, Columns
identical twins Two crystals grown side by side through one shared schedule of conditions, which come out nearly the same — the experiment showing that shape is set by environment rather than luck. 8 · A Snowflake Is a Record
morphology A crystal’s overall shape — star, plate, column, needle. 1 · Not a Frozen Raindrop
morphology diagram (Nakaya diagram) The map with temperature along the bottom and spare vapour up the side, showing which habit grows where; a superb description of what falls, and still not an explanation of why. 7 · Plates, Columns, Plates, Columns
needle A long slender column from warm, wet snowfalls near −5 °C; on a dark sleeve fresh ones look like short bits of white hair, which makes them the one type you can name without a lens. 7 · Plates, Columns, Plates, Columns
plate A crystal far wider than it is thick, which is what grows when the six side faces are the easy ones. 7 · Plates, Columns, Plates, Columns
ribs Hexagonal rings of slightly thicker ice on a plate, each one an old rim stranded behind by a single dip in humidity — which is why ribs cannot form under steady conditions at all. 8 · A Snowflake Is a Record
ridges Raised lines running outwards from each of the six corners of a plate, marking where each corner used to be as the crystal grew — quite literally a graph of corner position against time. 8 · A Snowflake Is a Record
sectored plate A stellar plate with ridges pronounced enough to look as though they divide it into slices; there is no sharp dividing line between the two names. 9 · The Menagerie
sheath A hollow column whose walls have been drawn out into a remarkably thin open tube. 9 · The Menagerie
simple prism A small, plain, faceted crystal with no branches, about the size of a full stop — which is to say simply a young snow crystal, since branching does not begin until about half a millimetre. 9 · The Menagerie
stellar dendrite The branched, tree-like star: narrow branches carrying numerous sidebranches, formed near −15 °C when the humidity is high. 9 · The Menagerie
stellar plate A thin, flat, six-fold crystal with broad branches, little sidebranching and a profusion of complex markings on its faces — the sparkle in falling snow is often these. 9 · The Menagerie
triangular crystal A small truncated triangular plate with a perfectly ordinary hexagonal lattice, made when three of the six prism faces ran slightly ahead of the other three and diffusion amplified the difference. 9 · The Menagerie
twelve-branched snowflake Not a twelve-fold crystal at all, but two ordinary six-branched ones that collided while small and stuck together about 30 degrees apart. 9 · The Menagerie

Measurement and evidence: how anyone knows

The instruments, the tricks that turn an easy measurement into a hard quantity, and the vocabulary for judging what a published line actually claims.
TermIn one sentenceExplained in
convenient shape A formula picked because it is easy to write down and happens to look right, with its numbers tuned until the curve lands near the data — the weakest of the four kinds of line on a graph. 13 · The Frontier
diffraction The splitting of a wave into a few sharp directions when it meets a regularly spaced set of obstacles, so the outgoing beam carries information about a spacing you never had to see. 2 · Four Hundred Years of Looking
diffraction grating Any regularly spaced set of obstacles that does that — and a crystal, as von Laue found in 1912, is one. 2 · Four Hundred Years of Looking
electric ice needles (e-needles) Slender single crystals grown in seconds by putting about 2,000 volts on a frost-covered wire in humid air near −6 °C, then used as clean isolated perches for the crystal you actually want to watch. 10 · How We Know
emulsion The thin light-sensitive coating that carries a photographic image; Bentley scraped it off the background of his copied negatives so that his crystals would print white on black. 2 · Four Hundred Years of Looking
error bar The whiskers on a data point saying how large the uncertainty is, because every measurement is a range rather than a value. 13 · The Frontier
eye guide A line drawn through data with no equation behind it at all, there so your eye can follow the trend and claiming nothing beyond that. 13 · The Frontier
factorial Written n!, the number of ways n things can be put in order — the arithmetic behind “no two complex snow crystals alike”. 8 · A Snowflake Is a Record
filling factor The fraction of a growth chamber’s floor covered by stray ice crystals; a coverage of one per cent, which looks through a microscope like an empty slide, can pull the real supersaturation down by a factor of twenty. 10 · How We Know
fit A smooth curve pushed through measured points: a summary whose shape was chosen by a person, and not itself a measurement. 13 · The Frontier
free-fall growth chamber Apparatus in which crystals grow while falling freely, touching nothing — one of the independent checks that the attachment measurements are not an artefact of whatever a crystal was resting on. 10 · How We Know
interference Waves adding where they arrive in step and cancelling where crest lands on trough, decided by nothing but how much further one of them travelled. 2 · Four Hundred Years of Looking
interferometry (fringes) Using the two reflections from the top and bottom of a crystal as a ruler: every full dim–bright–dim cycle means exactly the same fixed thickness of new ice, so counting flickers is measuring growth. 10 · How We Know
micron A thousandth of a millimetre. 4 · The Fuel Supply
nanometre A billionth of a metre, or a millionth of a millimetre. 3 · Why Six Sides
plate-on-pedestal (PoP) A method in which a thin plate grows sideways out of the top edge of a small ice prism resting on a glass slide, so it hangs free under a microscope — superb for photographs, and by its own author’s account not well suited to measurements. 10 · How We Know
reciprocal lattice The set of spots a diffraction pattern produces, whose spacings run inversely to the spacings of the rows of atoms that made them. 2 · Four Hundred Years of Looking
refractive index How much a material slows light down compared with empty space — the number that converts a fringe spacing into a thickness. 10 · How We Know
selection bias A distortion produced entirely by which honest measurements get kept; Bentley’s five thousand photographs are all real, and his album is still not a picture of the sky. 2 · Four Hundred Years of Looking
thermistor A bead-sized resistor whose resistance climbs about 4.5 per cent for every degree of cooling — good to 0.1 °C, costing about ten dollars, and the reason temperature is the easy half of a growth experiment. 10 · How We Know
VIG and VPG Two of the vacuum growth machines behind the sharpest numbers in the field; the first is the Vacuum Ice Growth apparatus. 10 · How We Know
witness-surface method Inferring a supersaturation you cannot measure directly by using a face whose behaviour is already known as a witness to it. 10 · How We Know
working hypothesis An account kept because nothing better exists rather than because it has been shown to be correct — the status its own authors give SDAK. 13 · The Frontier
X-ray diffraction Shining X-rays through a crystal and reading the pattern of spots back into an arrangement of atoms — how the hexagonal lattice of ice was found in 1929, 318 years after Kepler asked why six. 2 · Four Hundred Years of Looking

Symbols, what they stand for, and where they are introduced.

csat The saturated vapour density: how many water molecules per cubic metre the air holds when arrivals and departures exactly balance over ice. 4 · The Fuel Supply
dsv The length that sets the size of the Gibbs–Thomson correction for ice against vapour, about one nanometre. 5 · The Restless Surface
n! “n factorial”: n × (n − 1) × … × 2 × 1, the number of ways n things can be ordered. 8 · A Snowflake Is a Record
Rcrit The critical radius: the size a new island on a facet has to exceed before keeping it pays. 5 · The Restless Surface
Rtip The radius of curvature of a dendrite tip, about one micron. 6 · The Runaway Bump
σ (sigma) Supersaturation: how much spare water vapour the air carries, as a fraction of what ice can hold, always measured against ice. 4 · The Fuel Supply
σ0 (sigma-zero) The nucleation barrier for one facet, in the same units as σ: roughly how much spare vapour that face needs before new layers start with any regularity — and because it sits in a negative exponent, a lower σ0 means a faster face. 11 · The Stickiness of Ice
σfar, σ∞ Two names for the same thing: the supersaturation far away from the crystal, out in the surrounding air — the quantity an experimenter actually sets. 6 · The Runaway Bump
σsurf The supersaturation right at a patch of surface, after diffusion has taken its cut — always smaller than σfar, and the one the ice actually responds to. 5 · The Restless Surface
σwater The supersaturation, measured against ice, of air in balance with supercooled water droplets: 15.7 per cent at −15 °C, and the ceiling a cloud cannot climb past. 4 · The Fuel Supply
Tonset The temperature at which surface disorder makes the step lip leaky enough for the SDAK effect to switch on. 12 · Why the Shape Flips
v, vn How fast a patch of surface moves outward, the subscript n meaning “measured straight out from the patch itself”. 5 · The Restless Surface
vkin A reference speed fixed by temperature and by the mass of a water molecule: how fast a surface would advance if every arriving molecule stuck and the air carried 100 per cent spare vapour — real speeds are that figure multiplied by both α and σsurf, so always very much smaller. 5 · The Restless Surface
vtip The forward speed of a dendrite tip, a few microns a second, and roughly proportional to σfar. 6 · The Runaway Bump
w The width of the last flat strip at a crystal’s rim, roughly √(8Ra), which comes out as small as 50 nanometres — fewer than 200 molecules across. 12 · Why the Shape Flips
xbasal, xprism The two layer spacings: 0.37 nm added each time a layer completes on a flat end, 0.39 nm on a side wall. 3 · Why Six Sides
xdiff The diffusion length: how far an admolecule typically travels across a terrace before evaporating. 11 · The Stickiness of Ice
≪ and ≫ “Very much less than” and “very much greater than” — not merely smaller or larger but by a large factor, which is exactly the difference between a slightly squat prism and a wafer. 7 · Plates, Columns, Plates, Columns

Some of these words name things nobody has measured

A definition is not a guarantee that the thing defined is settled. The pyramidal step energy has never been measured, the equilibrium crystal shape of ice has never been observed, and the SDAK dips are real measurements with hand-drawn curves through them. Where each term sits on that spectrum is the business of Chapter 13, and it is worth knowing before you quote any of them.

If you meet a word on this site that is not in this list, treat that as a fault in the list rather than something you were meant to know already. The chapters are written so that every technical term is defined at its first use; this page only gathers those definitions into one place, and the references page holds the sources behind all of them.
