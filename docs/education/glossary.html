Glossary

Every technical term this site uses, defined in one line, under the chapter that introduces it.

The terms are grouped by chapter rather than alphabetically, and the chapters are in reading order. That is deliberate: the site is built so that nothing is used before it is explained, and an A–Z list would break that by putting Chapter 12’s vocabulary in front of a reader who is still on Chapter 3. If you know the word you want, your browser’s find-in-page will get you there in one keystroke.

One line is not the whole story

These definitions are reminders, not substitutes. Each one is a compressed version of an argument the chapter makes properly, usually with a figure and a citation attached. Where a term names something genuinely unsettled — SDAK is the clearest case — the one-line version says so rather than presenting a hypothesis as a fact.

Chapter 1 · Not a Frozen Raindrop

  • snow crystal

    A single crystal of ice grown directly out of water vapour. This is the six-sided object the whole site is about.

  • snowflake

    Loosely, anything white falling out of the sky; strictly, a clump of snow crystals stuck together on the way down. One snowflake can be many crystals.

  • sleet

    A raindrop that froze while falling. It was liquid first, so it is not a snow crystal — it never grew from vapour.

  • deposition

    Water vapour turning straight into ice without ever passing through liquid. Every snow crystal is built this way.

  • cloud droplet

    One of the tiny liquid water drops a cloud is actually made of — a few microns across, far too small to fall out.

  • supercooled

    Liquid water that is still liquid below 0 °C. Most cloud droplets are supercooled; freezing needs something to start on.

  • metastable

    Stable against small disturbances but not in the lowest-energy state available — it will stay put until something nudges it hard enough.

  • nucleation

    The first act of getting a new phase started: assembling a clump big enough that it grows rather than falls apart again.

  • micron

    A millionth of a metre. Cloud droplets are a few microns; a large snow crystal is a few thousand.

  • supersaturation (σ)

    How much more water vapour the air is carrying than it could hold in balance with ice, as a fraction of that balance value. This is the fuel supply.

  • dew point

    The temperature at which air, cooled without changing its water content, becomes saturated and starts condensing.

  • hexagonal prism

    The basic shape every snow crystal is built on: two hexagonal caps joined by six rectangular sides.

  • facet

    A flat face that appears on a crystal by itself as it grows, because that plane of the lattice grows more slowly than its neighbours.

  • basal facet

    Either of the two flat hexagonal caps of the prism — the top and the bottom.

  • prism facet

    Any of the six rectangular side faces of the prism.

  • attachment kinetics

    The rules deciding whether a water molecule that arrives at the surface actually sticks. One of the two forces that set a crystal's shape.

  • diffusion

    The drift of water vapour from where there is more of it to where there is less. The other of the two shaping forces.

  • anisotropic / anisotropy

    Direction-dependent. Ice grows at different rates in different lattice directions, and that difference is where all the structure comes from.

  • rime / riming

    Supercooled cloud droplets colliding with a falling crystal and freezing on contact, leaving a crust of frozen blobs.

  • graupel

    A crystal so heavily rimed that the original shape is buried and what lands is a soft white pellet.

Chapter 2 · Four Hundred Years of Looking

  • selection bias

    Choosing what to record by how good it looks, then treating the result as representative. The reason old photograph collections are full of perfect stars and short on lumps.

  • interference

    Two waves arriving at the same place and adding up — reinforcing where they are in step, cancelling where they are not.

  • diffraction

    Waves bending and spreading around obstacles or through regular gaps. Fire X-rays at a lattice and the pattern that comes back encodes the spacing.

  • crystallography

    Working out the internal arrangement of atoms in a solid from the diffraction pattern it produces.

  • capped column

    A column with a plate grown on each end — a crystal that was making a column, then changed conditions and started making plates.

Chapter 3 · Why Six Sides

  • phase diagram

    A map of which phase — solid, liquid, vapour — a substance settles into at each temperature and pressure.

  • triple point

    The single temperature and pressure at which solid, liquid and vapour can all coexist.

  • sublimation

    Ice going straight back to vapour without melting first — the reverse of deposition.

  • covalent bond

    The strong bond holding each water molecule together: two hydrogens sharing electrons with one oxygen.

  • tetrahedral angle

    The roughly 109° angle of a four-way corner. Water's two bonds sit at about 104.5°, close enough that each molecule ends up with four tetrahedral neighbours in ice.

  • hydrogen bond

    The weaker attraction between one molecule's hydrogen and a neighbour's oxygen. These are the bonds that build the ice lattice, and they are why the six is there.

  • Bernal–Fowler rules

    The bookkeeping every hydrogen bond in ice obeys: one hydrogen per bond, two close to each oxygen and two further off.

  • lattice

    The repeating three-dimensional arrangement of molecules in a crystal. Everything about a snow crystal's shape traces back to this.

  • ice Ih

    Ordinary hexagonal ice — the only form that occurs naturally on Earth's surface, and the one every snow crystal is made of.

  • ice Ic

    A cubic stacking of the same molecular layers. Long proposed as an explanation for oddly shaped crystals; never definitively observed in pure form.

  • stacking disordered

    Ice in which hexagonal and cubic stacking sequences are mixed, rather than either one being clean throughout.

  • twin / twin plane

    Two crystal grains sharing a boundary plane in a fixed mirrored relationship, so the pair behaves as one crystal with a seam through it.

  • c-axis

    The one special direction in the ice lattice: the axis running straight through both basal caps.

  • a-axis

    Any of the three equivalent axes lying in the basal plane, running across the hexagon.

  • Miller–Bravais indices

    The four-number notation, like {0001} or {101̄0}, that names a plane in a hexagonal lattice unambiguously.

  • secondary prism {112̄0}

    A plane sitting between the ordinary prism faces. Ice is not observed to facet on it — a candidate face that loses.

  • pyramidal facet

    A face angled between basal and prism. Real, but rare enough that it plays no part in ordinary snow crystal habit.

  • a0, c0

    The lattice constants of ice Ih: the repeat distances across the hexagon and along the c-axis.

  • terrace step

    The one-molecule-high edge where a flat molecular layer ends and the layer below is exposed.

Chapter 4 · The Fuel Supply

  • saturated vapour pressure

    The amount of water vapour air holds when it is exactly in balance with a surface — no net gain, no net loss.

  • csat

    The saturated vapour concentration over ice: the benchmark that supersaturation is measured against.

  • latent heat

    The energy released when vapour turns to ice. It has to go somewhere, and where it goes is one of the loose ends in Chapter 13.

  • σwater

    The supersaturation over ice that air sitting in equilibrium with liquid water would have. It sets a practical ceiling inside a cloud.

  • Berg effect

    The geometric fact that on a growing crystal the corners and tips sit closest to fresh vapour and the middles of flat faces sit deepest in the crystal's own shadow.

  • diffusion-limited

    Growth whose speed is set by how fast vapour can get to the crystal, rather than by what happens once it arrives.

  • depleted halo

    The shell of dried-out air a crystal eats around itself — a few times wider than the crystal, and shaped like it.

Chapter 5 · The Restless Surface

  • terrace

    One flat molecular tread on the crystal surface — the top of a single completed layer.

  • step

    The one-molecule drop from one terrace to the next. Steps are where arriving molecules can lock in properly.

  • nucleation barrier

    The difficulty of starting a brand-new layer on an otherwise flat face. This is the bottleneck that makes facets slow, and therefore makes them exist.

  • critical radius (Rcrit)

    The make-or-break size for a new island of ice: smaller and it evaporates away, larger and it takes over the face. About ten molecules across in realistic conditions.

  • step energy (β)

    The energy cost of a unit length of island edge. Nearly the only property of the surface that materially affects the nucleation barrier.

  • attachment coefficient (α)

    The probability that a water molecule striking a particular face actually sticks. Runs from 0 (dead face) to 1 (every arrival sticks).

  • vkin

    The speed a face would grow at if every arriving molecule stuck — the ceiling that α is a fraction of.

  • σsurf

    The spare vapour immediately above the surface, as opposed to far away. This, not the distant supply, is what the surface responds to.

  • αbasal, αprism, αrough

    The attachment coefficients of the two facet families, and of a surface disordered enough that nucleation has stopped mattering.

  • surface premelting

    The outermost molecular layers of ice becoming disordered below the bulk melting point, because a molecule at the surface has fewer neighbours holding it in place.

  • quasi-liquid layer (QLL)

    The thin, neither-quite-solid-nor-quite-liquid film that premelting leaves on the surface. It thickens as the temperature rises towards 0 °C.

  • surface energy

    The energy cost of having surface at all. It drives shape change in a crystal left alone at equilibrium — which is not the situation a growing snow crystal is in.

  • equilibrium crystal shape (ECS)

    The shape a crystal would settle into if surface energy alone decided, given unlimited time. For ice it has never been measured, and reaching it would take about eight days.

  • Gibbs–Thomson effect

    The way sharp curvature raises the vapour pressure of a surface, so very small or very sharply curved features are harder to keep.

  • roughening transition

    The temperature above which a facet loses its flatness altogether because steps become free to appear anywhere.

Chapter 6 · The Runaway Bump

  • Mullins–Sekerka instability

    The feedback that makes a smooth growing front impossible: a bump reaches slightly fresher vapour, so it grows faster, so it sticks out further still.

  • branching instability

    The same runaway seen in its result — the reason arms appear on a crystal that nothing ever told to have arms.

  • dendrite

    A branched, tree-like growth form, produced when the branching instability is allowed to run.

  • free dendrite

    A dendrite growing into open, undisturbed vapour, unconstrained by a substrate or by neighbouring arms.

  • Ivantsov solution

    The classical result that a parabolic tip advancing into a diffusion field fixes the product of its tip radius and its speed — but not either one on its own.

  • Rtip, vtip

    The radius of curvature of a growing tip, and the speed at which it advances.

  • selection problem

    The unresolved question of what picks one particular tip radius and speed out of the family the Ivantsov solution permits.

  • sidebranch

    A secondary arm growing off the side of a main arm, from the same instability acting on a smaller scale.

  • σfar / σ∞

    The supersaturation far from the crystal, out beyond the depleted halo — the undisturbed supply.

  • seaweed

    A branched growth form with no consistent directions in it, produced when branching runs without enough anisotropy to steer it.

  • fractal

    A structure that keeps showing similar detail as you look closer. Snow crystals are often called fractal; they are only roughly and over a limited range.

Chapter 7 · Plates, Columns, Plates, Columns

  • habit

    A crystal's overall shape ignoring the decoration — plate, block, column, needle. Two crystals with quite different ornament can share a habit.

  • aspect ratio

    The size along the c-axis divided by the size across an a-axis. Below 1 is flatter than wide; above 1 is taller than wide. Real crystals span about 0.01 to 20.

  • morphology diagram / Nakaya diagram

    The map of which habit appears at which temperature and supersaturation. It is a summary of observations, not an explanation of them.

  • water saturation line

    The line across the morphology diagram marking the supersaturation of air in balance with liquid water — roughly the ceiling available inside a cloud.

  • hollow column

    A column whose basal ends have grown faster at the rim than in the middle, leaving conical hollows sunk into each end.

  • needle

    An extremely slender column, with an aspect ratio that can reach 20 or more.

  • fishbone dendrite

    A distinctive branched form found in a narrow band of conditions, with sidebranches arranged like the ribs of a fish.

Chapter 8 · A Snowflake Is a Record

  • ridge

    A raised line running along an arm, left where the growing surface changed thickness. Part of the record of the crystal's path.

  • rib

    A finer raised marking across an arm, recording a smaller or shorter-lived change in conditions.

  • plate-on-pedestal (PoP)

    A laboratory technique in which a plate is grown on a raised support, so it grows clear of the substrate and can be watched and measured cleanly.

  • dislocation

    A line defect in the lattice — a place where the regular stacking is interrupted.

  • deuterium

    The heavy isotope of hydrogen. Its abundance differs slightly between water molecules, which is part of why no two crystals are identical at molecular resolution.

Chapter 9 · The Menagerie

  • simple prism

    A plain hexagonal prism with no branching or ornament — the commonest thing actually falling, and the least photographed.

  • stellar plate

    A flat crystal with six broad arms and little further branching.

  • sectored plate

    A plate whose arms are divided into flat panels by prominent ridges radiating from the centre.

  • stellar dendrite

    A flat six-armed crystal whose arms carry clear sidebranches.

  • fernlike stellar dendrite

    The most heavily branched form, with sidebranches dense enough to look like a fern. The largest snow crystals are these.

  • irregular

    A crystal with no clean classification — broken, clumped, or grown through too many changes of condition. Most real snow is this.

  • arrowhead twin

    A pair of crystals joined across a twin plane, giving a form with a sharp angled point that a single crystal cannot make.

  • crossed plates

    Two plates grown at an angle to one another from a shared origin, rather than in a single plane.

  • triangular crystal

    A crystal that comes out three-sided even though the lattice underneath is six-fold — three of the six prism faces having grown at the expense of the others.

Chapter 10 · How We Know

  • synthetic snow

    Snow crystals grown from vapour in a laboratory, by the same physics as natural ones. Not to be confused with the next entry.

  • artificial snow

    Sprayed and frozen water droplets, as made for ski slopes. Frozen liquid, so not snow crystals at all.

  • thermistor

    A resistor whose resistance changes sharply and predictably with temperature, used to measure a growth chamber to a small fraction of a degree.

  • interference fringe

    One of the light and dark bands produced when light reflected from the top and bottom of a crystal recombines. The closer the fringes, the thicker the crystal.

  • e-needle

    An electric ice needle: a slender c-axis needle grown on a charged wire, used as a clean, reproducible seed to grow a test crystal on.

  • witness surface

    A surface whose growth is measured indirectly, by the record it leaves, when it cannot be watched head-on.

Chapter 11 · The Stickiness of Ice

  • Hertz–Knudsen relation

    The formula for how many vapour molecules strike a surface per second, which sets the ceiling that the attachment coefficient is a fraction of.

  • vicinal surface

    A surface tilted slightly off a facet plane, so it is made of flat terraces separated by a regular train of steps.

  • admolecule

    A molecule that has landed on the surface and is loosely held there, skating about, not yet locked into the lattice.

  • diffusion length

    How far an admolecule typically travels across a terrace before it either finds a step or is thrown back into the vapour.

  • Ehrlich–Schwoebel barrier

    The extra energy penalty an admolecule pays to hop down off the terrace it is on, because for an instant it hangs off the crystal with almost nothing beneath it.

  • screw dislocation

    A defect that puts a permanent step on a face, letting it grow in a spiral without ever having to nucleate a new layer.

  • σ0

    The nucleation barrier of a face, in the same units as supersaturation: roughly, how much spare vapour that face needs before new layers start with any regularity. Lower σ0 means the face grows more easily.

  • A

    The ceiling in α = A e−σ0/σsurf — the value the attachment coefficient climbs towards once the barrier has stopped mattering. You would expect 1, and for the basal face it is.

  • CAK model

    The comprehensive attachment kinetics model: Libbrecht's name for the measured σ0 and A curves for large facets, plus everything later built on them.

  • VIG / VPG

    Two of the growth machines behind the measurements — the vacuum and the higher-pressure apparatus, whose agreement is what makes the numbers trustworthy.

  • frustrated QLL kinetics

    The proposal that the quasi-liquid layer near the melting point makes it harder, not easier, for molecules to find their lattice positions — a candidate explanation for the shape of the barrier curves.

Chapter 12 · Why the Shape Flips

  • mesoscopic

    The in-between scale: far bigger than one molecule, far smaller than the whole crystal.

  • SDAK

    Structure-Dependent Attachment Kinetics: the proposal that a facet only tens of nanometres wide has a substantially lower nucleation barrier than a broad facet of the same plane at the same temperature. A hypothesis, not a settled result.

  • SDAK dip

    The downward notch this puts into a σ0 curve over a narrow range of temperature — one in the basal barrier near −4 °C, one in the prism barrier near −14 °C. The two dips are what supply the extra habit boundaries a single crossing cannot.

  • Edge-Sharpening Instability (ESI)

    The runaway SDAK implies: a narrow edge grows faster, which packs its terrace steps closer, which narrows it further. Proposed as the origin of very thin plates and of the sheath-like edges on hollow columns.

  • habit boundary

    A temperature at which the basal and prism barrier curves cross, so the faster face becomes the slower one and the crystal switches between plate and column.

Chapter 13 · The Frontier

  • fit

    A curve chosen to pass through measured points. It carries the data's authority only where there are data; between and beyond them it is an assumption.

  • eye guide

    A line drawn on a plot to help the reader follow it, carrying no claim at all. Telling these three apart is most of what Chapter 13 asks you to do.

Where a term is not defined here

If you meet a word on this site that is not in this list, that is a bug in the site rather than something you were meant to already know. The chapters are written so that every technical term is defined at its first use, in the sentence that introduces it; this page only collects those definitions in one place.
