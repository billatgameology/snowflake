References

Everything on this site traces back to something you can go read yourself. Here is the whole list, with what each source actually contributes.

This page has two halves, because the site does. Part One’s sources are published papers written by scientists outside this project and refereed or posted openly for anyone to attack. Part Two’s sources are of a different kind entirely: they are this project’s own tracked files. Both lists are below, and the difference between them matters more than either list. Skip to Part Two’s sources if that is what you came for.

Part One · The published literature

Most of the modern science in this registered corpus comes from Kenneth G. Libbrecht, a physicist at Caltech who spent roughly two decades growing snow crystals under controlled conditions and measuring how fast their surfaces grow. His 523-page monograph is the course’s organizing source, supplemented by the later papers below. This is not a claim that one local bibliography contains every snow-crystal study ever published.

Everything here is free to read

Every arXiv item below is open access. If a claim on this site matters to you, follow the link, find the page number we cite, and check it. That is the whole point of citing it.

The primary source

  • Snow Crystals Kenneth G. Libbrecht, 2019 (revised 2021). 523 pages. arXiv:1910.06389v2 · arxiv.org/abs/1910.06389

    The comprehensive account: ice crystal structure, the physics of attachment and diffusion, the morphology diagram, laboratory technique, and hundreds of photographs. Most chapters on this site draw on it. Where we cite it we give both the PDF page and the printed page, because the two differ by one.

Measurement papers

These came after the monograph and carry the sharpest measurements, revised interpretations, and morphology surveys in the course. They are not all the same kind of evidence: some are dedicated experiments, some assemble selected specimens, and FACET and CM10 are interpretive models. Uncertainty reporting also differs from paper to paper.

  • A Comprehensive Model of Snow Crystal Faceting Kenneth G. Libbrecht & James Walkling, 6 June 2023. arXiv:2306.04042v1 · arxiv.org/abs/2306.04042

    Proposes an additive two-process account of faceting and prints Table 1 (printed page 9) from −1 °C to −15 °C. Its low-pressure particle-diffusion measurements, latent-heating correction, and interpretation do not establish a universal branch-selection law; CM7 offers a materially different, mutually exclusive branch picture.

  • Quantifying the Nakaya Diagram Libbrecht, June 2023. arXiv:2306.13087v1 · arxiv.org/abs/2306.13087

    Puts the habit bands directly onto a plot of the attachment parameters, and includes 206 photographs arranged on a temperature × supersaturation grid. This is the source for the central explanation on this site.

  • Comprehensive Model 10: On the Molecular Dynamics of SDAK Libbrecht, 23 December 2020. arXiv:2012.12916v1 · arxiv.org/abs/2012.12916

    Tests whether edge-enhancing surface diffusion can account for narrow-facet growth. The simple estimate predicts roughly a factor of two where the reported effect is nearer a factor of ten; the proposed mechanism is pressure-gated and depends on assumed molecular length scales.

  • Comprehensive Model 9: Characterizing SDAK near −4 °C Libbrecht, 4 November 2020. arXiv:2011.02353v1 · arxiv.org/abs/2011.02353

    Measures the dip in the basal-facet parameter that makes columns near −4 °C.

  • Comprehensive Model 8: Characterizing SDAK near −14 °C Libbrecht, 17 September 2020. arXiv:2009.08404v2 · arxiv.org/abs/2009.08404

    Measures the matching dip in the prism-facet parameter — the one behind the thin plates near −15 °C. Also prints the closed-form equations for the broad-facet curves.

  • Comprehensive Model 7: Ice Attachment Kinetics near −2 °C Libbrecht, 13 April 2020. arXiv:2004.06212v1 · arxiv.org/abs/2004.06212

    Reports overlapping large-prism and kinetic-roughening descriptions near −2 °C, with pressure and branch-selection limits. Its mutually exclusive regimes should not be silently collapsed into FACET’s later additive model.

  • Comprehensive Model 6: Ice Attachment Kinetics near −5 °C Libbrecht, 6 December 2019. arXiv:1912.03230v1 · arxiv.org/abs/1912.03230

    Shows broad- and narrow-basal behavior near −5 °C, including initial-fast growth, history dependence, and plate-versus-column outcomes under nominally similar conditions. The selected crystals do not define a population frequency.

  • A Versatile Apparatus for Measuring the Growth Rates of Small Ice Prisms from the Vapor Phase Libbrecht, 19 December 2019. arXiv:1912.09440v1 · arxiv.org/abs/1912.09440

    The instrument behind the measurement papers. Its optical record is a 1.5-D silhouette rather than a full three-dimensional surface; the analysis corrects for latent heating, and not every printed fitted value carries a conventional uncertainty bar.

  • Taxonomy 1: Using c-axis Ice Needles as Seed Crystals Libbrecht, 31 August 2021. arXiv:2109.00098v1 · arxiv.org/abs/2109.00098

    Establishes the c-axis-needle method and presents a 97-panel selected-specimen grid. Together with TAX2 it supplies the seed, growth-time, scale, and top-versus-bottom context needed to read a morphology matrix without mistaking it for a population frequency.

  • Triangular Snowflakes Libbrecht, 17 June 2021. arXiv:2106.09809v1 · arxiv.org/abs/2106.09809

    Why some snow crystals come out three-sided on a six-sided lattice.

Modelling papers

This site explains the science, not how anyone simulates it. These are listed because several ideas on this site were first stated cleanly in them.

  • A Quantitative Physical Model of the Snow Crystal Morphology Diagram Libbrecht, 2019. arXiv:1910.09067v2 · arxiv.org/abs/1910.09067

    States the attachment-coefficient equations compactly. The definitions of supersaturation and of the growth law used on this site follow this paper's convention.

  • Toward a Comprehensive Model of Snow Crystal Growth Dynamics: 1. Overarching Features and Physical Origins Libbrecht, 2012. arXiv:1211.5555v1 · arxiv.org/abs/1211.5555

    Its Figure 1 (printed page 2) is the version of the Nakaya morphology diagram this site measures its boundary temperatures from. The paper also surveys front-tracking, phase-field, and cellular-automaton approaches. It argues that diffusion explains, at least qualitatively, why complexity grows with supersaturation, crystal size, and background-gas pressure, while sharply faceted vapour growth makes otherwise successful smooth-interface methods numerically difficult. Its 2012 conclusion was that accurate crystals from defensible physical inputs over a range of conditions had not yet been achieved; it presents the proposed comprehensive model as a still-incomplete framework, not a validation result.

  • Modeling snow-crystal growth: A three-dimensional mesoscopic approach Janko Gravner & David Griffeath, Physical Review E 79, 011601 (2009) · doi:10.1103/PhysRevE.79.011601.

    A mathematical model rather than a measurement. Its three-dimensional cases include ridges, ribs, hollowing, and other structures; the course’s interactive zoo is an intentionally smaller two-dimensional teaching model, not a reproduction of the paper’s full simulation.

Rights-aware video evidence

  • SnowCrystals.com laboratory growth movies Kenneth G. Libbrecht · snowcrystals.com/videos

    The local research cache includes ten downloaded movies for personal study, including a crystal growing among water droplets. They teach depletion, competition, branching, and morphology in real time. The files and extracted frames are not redistributable: an offline personal build may use the local copy, while the published course shows a citation card and source link only.

The historical record

  • Strena seu de Nive Sexangula (On the Six-Cornered Snowflake) Johannes Kepler, 1611.

    The first serious attempt to ask why snowflakes have six sides. Kepler could not answer it — the atomic theory he needed was three centuries away — but he framed the question correctly.

  • Snow Crystals Wilson A. Bentley & W. J. Humphreys, 1931.

    Over two thousand photomicrographs by a Vermont farmer who spent his life photographing snowflakes. The images that made the subject famous.

  • Snow Crystals: Natural and Artificial Ukichiro Nakaya, Harvard University Press, 1954.

    Nakaya grew snow crystals in a laboratory for the first time and mapped which shapes appear at which temperature and humidity. The morphology diagram carries his name.

Reference standards

  • Review of the vapour pressures of ice and supercooled water for atmospheric applications D. M. Murphy & T. Koop, Quarterly Journal of the Royal Meteorological Society 131, 1539–1565 (2005).

    The standard reference for how much water vapour air can hold over ice and over supercooled water. Used on this site as an independent check on numbers read from published figures.

Part Two · This project’s own record

Part Two: a repository, not a literature

Part One cites papers. Part Two cites a directory tree. Every claim in chapters 14–29 — every number, every gate, every admission of failure — traces to a tracked file in this project’s own Git repository, and the chapters usually name the file and sometimes the line. This section lists those files, so you can open them yourself. Counts and versions below are as of 29 July 2026; the repository moves, so treat them as a snapshot rather than a fixed fact.

The difference in kind matters, and it cuts in a specific direction:

  • Nothing in Part Two has been refereed. Libbrecht’s papers were written to be attacked by other people who grow ice crystals for a living. Part Two’s sources were written by this project, about itself.
  • The same party makes the claim and keeps the score. There is no external examiner. The marking scheme in chapter 27 was written by the people being marked.
  • What you can check is consistency, not correctness. The record is dated, hash-stamped, and in places pre-registered — predictions written down and committed before the run that tested them. So you can check whether it contradicts itself: whether the decision record that predicted an outcome really predates the run (git log will tell you), whether the quoted SHA-256 hashes match the artifacts, whether a recorded failure was quietly deleted later. That is an unusual amount of checkable structure for a hobby project. It is still not the same thing as an outside expert saying the physics is right.
  • The evidence artifacts themselves are not in the repository. Run outputs land in out/, which is deliberately untracked, because they are reproducible from the recorded seed, dimensions, and command (Rule 6). What is tracked is the recipe, the hashes, and the commit — not the bytes. To check a number you re-run the command and compare hashes; you cannot simply download the answer.

How much weight to give this

Read Part Two as a claim about a piece of software and how it was built, not as a claim about nature. The strongest honest reading of the record below is: this project wrote down what it would count as success before it measured, measured, and then reported a bad score without moving the target. That is a claim about method, and the files let you audit it. Whether the physics inside the solver is right is a separate question, and Phase 6 has not answered it.

The governing documents

Two files sit at the top of the project’s authority chain. When the code and the documents disagree, the disagreement is settled against these — and then against the accepted decision records that amend them, which are the next section.

  • The project charter project charter.md — “The Virtual Cloud Chamber”, v1.18, revised 28 July 2026. 344 lines.

    What is being built, what would count as success (§1.4), the science it depends on (§2), the phase ladder with each phase’s registered gate (§3.2), and the standing guardrails (§3.3). §1.5, “Epistemic honesty as product identity”, is the clause the two labels in chapter 15 come from. Its whole revision history, v1.0 to v1.18, sits at the top: every amendment dated and tied to the decision number that forced it. You can read straight down it and watch the project change its mind.

  • The working rules AGENTS.md — 454 lines: thirteen numbered rules, plus a list of anti-rules.

    The operating discipline of the project, written for whichever model opens the repository next. The chapters cite Rule 2 (plan in a file before you build), Rule 3 (update the progress log as you go, not at the end), Rule 4 (record what failed, not just what worked), Rule 6 (claims are cheap; evidence is the deliverable), Rule 7 (a bare alpha is banned from this repository), Rule 8 (leave the next model a landing spot), Rule 9 (a verdict is computed from the artifact, never inherited from its producer), Rule 10 (reviews carry provenance and state their limits), Rule 11 (probes transfer only from the registered configuration), and Rule 12 (check source currency before any freeze). Rule 13’s substance — an adversarial audit gates any interpretive document before it is published — appears without being cited by number, most visibly in chapter 29’s account of the audit that retracted the Phase 6 structural bound. It also carries the repository map and a section called High-value traps already paid for — a list of mistakes that cost real time, kept so they are not made twice.

The decision records

An ADR is an architecture decision record: one small file per decision that contradicts or extends the charter. Rule 5 requires one. Each states the context, what was decided, what was rejected and why, and what follows — and once accepted it is never edited to look better. A later ADR supersedes an earlier one and both stay on disk. That is what makes the sequence readable as a history of being wrong rather than a tidy story.

There are 36 numbered records in the current authority, docs/decisions/0001-… through 0036-…: 34 accepted, one superseded (0002), and one proposed (0030), plus a template. The ones Part Two leans on hardest:

  • 0031 — Phase 6 parameter-set freeze row docs/decisions/0031-phase6-paramset-freeze-row.md

    The most consequential file in Part Two. It invalidated the first 204-point sweep (an unregistered CLI default supplied parameter set CAK_A1, in which A_prism is identically 1, violating a registered freeze row), chose the registered set CAK rather than editing the scheme to match what had run, and pre-registered the corrected expectation before the re-run: the headline “falls from 5/90 to approximately 2/90”, and “the corrected result is expected to be worse, and that is the point”. Chapters 27 and 28.

  • 0035 — Phase 6 run outcome and input pins docs/decisions/0035-phase6-run-outcome-and-input-pins.md

    The executed --steps 1 attack showed that a one-step seed could be scored as a crystal. This record registers the 100,000-step safety cap, requires a size-target stop, enforces largestExtent ≥ 21, and binds every child’s temperature, supersaturation, dimensions, domain, active-cell count, seed, and other inputs. Re-checking Arm 1 found all 204 rows at extent exactly 21, but cannot add per-row fields its historical artifacts never carried.

  • 0036 — the separate Phase 6 M1 arm docs/decisions/0036-phase6-sdak-arm-pre-registrations.md

    Registers Arm 2’s all-facets-narrow M1 interpretation, its own exclusions and its pre-run forecast. This is a separate protocol from Arm 1, never a patch to it: 42 of 90 on the common denominator and 42 of 78 under Arm 2’s own bistable-row exclusion are predictions, not measurements. Its values protocol is frozen. One launch was stopped about a minute in after its first line exposed Arm 1’s combined protocol hash. At the education audit’s 2026-07-30 10:23 PDT cutoff, the repaired canonical sweep was running; its local output remained mutable and did not yet constitute a completed, reviewed sweep result.

  • 0003 — Libbrecht attachment kinetics 0003-libbrecht-attachment-kinetics.md

    Puts measured attachment kinetics on the Gravner–Griffeath skeleton, and reframes Phase 6 from calibration to validation — the decision that made it possible to fail. Chapters 17 and 21.

  • 0024 and 0027 — the monopole-matched far field 0024-monopole-matched-far-field.md · 0027-charter-far-field-amendment.md

    The walls of chapter 23: the measurement showing that a fixed-supersaturation shell over-supplies vapour by an amount that grows with the crystal, and the charter amendment that replaced it for Phase 6.

  • 0013, 0014, 0020, 0021 — the floating-point drift decisions 0013-float64-smoother-drift-divergence-identity.md · 0014-bound-float64-smoother-drift.md · 0020-floor-phase5-float32-smoother-drift.md · 0021-bound-phase5-float32-two-cycles.md

    Chapter 20’s subject: metering the drift in the last bit, then requiring it to pass an operation-count-derived bound rather than merely being small.

  • 0025 and 0026 — the marking scheme 0025-phase6-agreement-scoring-rule.md · 0026-phase6-grid-extrapolation-operator.md

    How a model habit is scored against a reference regime, and how an extrapolated quantity is computed and when it is refused — the two freeze rows chapter 27 is about, both registered before the sweep ran.

  • 0022 — observed negative-control ownership 0022-phase5-observed-negative-control-ownership.md

    Chapter 25: a negative control must actually break the thing it claims to break, and the right criterion must be the one that catches it.

  • 0028 — parameter-table erratum 0028-parameter-table-erratum-exponent.md

    An exponent wrong in the project’s own transcription of a published table. Chapter 16.

  • 0033 and 0034 — splitting the frozen manifest 0033-phase6-values-justification-hash-split.md · 0034-phase6-e1-justification-correction.md

    Hashing prose together with registered values had made it impossible to fix a wrong sentence without invalidating the sweep. 0033 split the hash in two; 0034 then used the split to correct an erratum at zero cost, and to walk back an over-strong claim 0033 had itself made.

  • 0030 — proposed precursor to the Phase 6 SDAK arm 0030-phase6-sdak-arm.md

    A Status: proposed design record for the second arm. It is not the accepted registration and must not be cited as one; accepted decision 0036, the M1 implementation, and the later Arm 2 values freeze supersede its preparation status. Both completed Arm 1 sweeps were no-SDAK. At the education audit cutoff, Arm 2 had mutable partial rows but no completed, reviewed sweep result.

  • 0018 — Phase 5 is Windows-only 0018-phase5-windows-only-gate.md

    Narrows the GPU claim to one Windows D3D12 lane and makes no general-WebGPU or Metal portability claim at all — an example of a decision whose whole content is claiming less.

Live state and evidence

  • The progress log docs/PROGRESS.md — mutable live-state record.

    The file to open first. It carries the phase table with each gate’s status and the metric that closed it, a Next step section that is the current state of play, and long passages of “traps already known” recording defects, dead ends, and interrupted runs. Rule 3 requires it be written as work happens rather than tidied up afterwards, which is why it reads the way it does. This audit found a concrete lag at 8c781b1: Next step still says to freeze Arm 2 even though 483f7ee froze the value content, 0cb52bf recorded the freeze ancestry and final values pin, and 8c781b1 recorded the arm-specific combined revision hash. That disagreement is stated here because the project’s own rule forbids choosing a source silently.

  • The findings ledger docs/findings-ledger.md — 167 lines.

    Results the project considers worth showing anyone, in three classes: A the model reproducing measured reality, B uncharted territory entered with supporting evidence, C queued items not there yet. Entries are never softened or deleted — a correction is appended as a note, the way ADRs work. The 29 July 2026 audit pass is visible in it: one entry retracted in its strongest form, one superseded in part, one entry’s stated limit reversed. Every entry names its own verification status, and the ledger grants nothing by itself.

  • The Phase 6 sweep report research/phase6-sweep-report.md

    The corrected 204-point no-SDAK sweep, and the single most important document behind chapter 28. It carries the provenance table (protocol hash over 25 freeze rows, parameter set CAK, execution commit, the freeze commit verified as its ancestor, engine version, exact command, exit code), byte counts and SHA-256 for each artifact, the per-regime results behind 3 of 90, and the class totals. It opens with a retraction of its own Finding 1 — a structural bound an adversarial audit refuted — and states in bold that its numbers are not yet gate evidence.

  • The Phase 6 plan docs/plans/phase-6-nakaya-validation.md

    The registered plan, with the work-package checklist showing exactly what is done and what is not (WP5 is still unchecked), the freeze list, an explicit Out of scope section, and a Tried and rejected section that includes running the sweep on the GPU — registered and retracted the same day on measurement.

  • The frozen-protocol errata docs/phase6-protocol-errata.md

    Errors found inside the hashed protocol after the sweep had already run, recorded here instead of edited in place.

  • The soundness audit transcript docs/phase6-soundness-audit-2026-07-29.raw.txt

    The raw output of the adversarial audit that forced the retraction above, kept unedited. Useful if you want to see what a review of this project actually looks like before deciding how much it is worth.

The derivation notes

Between Part One’s papers and Part Two’s code sit four long tracked notes. They are where a published equation becomes a constant in a source file, and they are the documents the solvers are written against.

  • The Libbrecht parameter mapping docs/libbrecht-parameters.md — 547 lines. Frozen 27 July 2026.

    The mapping layer: which published number, from which page, becomes which constant. Chapter 16 is about this file. Its content hash is registered as PHASE6_PARAMETER_TABLE_SHA256 in runner/src/phase6-protocol.ts and enforced, so editing it now breaks the protocol on purpose.

  • Attachment kinetics — the physics spec docs/attachment-kinetics.md — 724 lines.

    The only physically parameterized step of the update cycle, specified in full. Its own header carries a correction: it used to claim this was “the only step that is physics”, which overstated it.

  • The Gravner–Griffeath machinery — implementation spec docs/gg-machinery.md — 431 lines.

    The shared computational skeleton: lattice, diffusion, state fields, mass bookkeeping, melting, noise — and which of those are physical transport and which are phenomenological knobs. Chapters 18 through 22, and 24, draw on it.

  • The monograph review docs/monograph-review.md — 480 lines.

    A living note comparing the 523-page monograph against the plan. It is explicitly not a plan and not an ADR: nothing in it changes the project by itself, and every item that survives discussion has to land through the normal authority chain.

The source trees

The root package.json lists exactly five npm workspaces: core, solver-cpu, solver-gpu, runner, and app. Each currently has a src/ and a test/ directory. The archived spike/ prototype has no package manifest and is not in that workspace list. The chapters quote from these by path, and often by line. Dependency direction is core → solver-cpu → runner; solver code stays free of Node APIs and file I/O so the same oracle can run in a browser worker.

  • core/ core/src · core/test — 7 test files

    Environment-neutral contracts: the lattice and its D6h symmetry transforms (core/src/lattice.ts, chapter 19), state fields, parameters, metrics (core/src/metrics.ts), a seeded counter-based random generator (core/src/prng.ts, chapter 20), strict checkpoint codecs, and the Libbrecht mapping core/src/libbrecht.ts — the single most-cited file in Part Two.

  • solver-cpu/ solver-cpu/src · solver-cpu/test — 10 test files

    The permanent float64 oracle and the thing everything else is measured against: gg-solver.ts, lk-solver.ts, their shared SurfaceOperator contract (chapter 21’s seam), the 1D spherical reference solver, and the monopole far field (chapter 23).

  • solver-gpu/ solver-gpu/src · solver-gpu/test — 9 test files

    The Phase 5 WebGPU port: memory layout, diffusion and surface shaders, submission and readback auditing. Chapter 26. Certified on one Windows D3D12 lane and nothing wider (ADR 0018), and not used for the Phase 6 sweep: it passed Phase 5’s own frozen field and scalar tolerances (16 of 16 criteria, after an explicit FMA fix), but a later calibration probe measured it roughly 6× slower than the CPU oracle at sweep-scale workloads, and the separate, stricter Phase 6 sweep’s divTol bound sits below the float32 epsilon — unreachable by construction, not by misfortune.

  • runner/ runner/src · runner/test — 27 test files

    The evidence boundary, and where a claim becomes an exit code: the CLI (runner/src/main.ts), argument validation, stopping rules, checkpoint round-trips, the enforced gates, the frozen protocols (runner/src/phase6-protocol.ts, runner/src/phase5-protocol.ts) and the sweep driver (runner/src/phase6-sweep.ts). Chapters 24, 25, 27 and 28 live here.

  • app/ app/src · app/test — 20 test files

    The browser instrument: a Web Worker copy of the CPU solver, the overlays and vapour slice that let you watch the crystal starve (chapter 24), picking and readouts, and a deterministic visual harness so the pictures can be tested too.

  • spike/ spike/js · spike/index.html — frozen, deliberately outside the npm workspace

    The archived Phase 1 UX spike: a 2D Reiter cellular automaton with an editable environmental timeline, closed 2026-07-15 on a maker-asserted gate. AGENTS.md’s repository map states the boundary directly — “deliberately outside the npm workspace”, kept so nobody “evolve[s] it into the product”. Nothing in the real solver imports from it, and nothing here imports from the workspace; its own 38-check suite (node spike/check.mjs) runs outside npm test.

One command, npm test, runs the Rule 7 naming scan, both strict TypeScript projects, and every suite above; quoting a bare test runner instead is explicitly not verification. The exact pass count at each gate is recorded with that gate in docs/PROGRESS.md, not asserted here.

What Phase 6 has and has not shown

Chapter 28 reports a score. Because the number is easy to quote out of context, here is the status in plain terms:

  • Phase 6 is still in development. It has not concluded, and Phase 7 has not started. This does not erase the Phase 3 browser development instrument, which already exists; Phase 7 is the separate polished product layer.
  • The first 204-point sweep scored 5 of 90 and was invalidated by ADR 0031 — an unregistered command-line default supplied parameter set CAK_A1, in which A_prism is identically 1, violating a registered freeze row. Its artifacts are preserved unmodified rather than deleted.
  • The corrected re-sweep, under the registered set CAK, scored 3 of 90: warm plates 3/6, columns 0/24, cold plates 0/60. Class totals across all 204 points: 6 plate, 168 neutral, 30 column, 0 invalid.
  • That corrected headline was predicted in writing before the re-run. ADR 0031 registered “approximately 2/90” and said the corrected result was expected to be worse. Outcome: direction correct, magnitude one point pessimistic.
  • 3 of 90 is not gate evidence, and the project says so itself. The sweep report states it in bold. WP5 — run the registered gate command at one exact clean commit, authenticate the artifacts, and obtain independent review to zero blockers — is still unchecked in the Phase 6 plan. The historical report says no independent verifier had run. Current history is later: commit 990840a’s hardened verifier asserts all three artifact digests, requires the exact 204-row set, re-derives all report fields, recomputes ambiguity and domain-contact status, passes the unchanged artifacts, and rejects the two real forgeries its first version accepted. Seven negative controls executed. Arm 1’s flagless closing gate and zero-blocker review still have not happened, so this remains a measured result with verified provenance, which is a different and lesser thing.
  • Arm 1’s historical verification hazard remains historical: its 204 child runs loaded the working tree after provenance was stamped. Nine commits landed during the run; a later diff found no physics change, which the report calls luck, not design. Commit 154359d now fingerprints the full source graph before and after a future sweep and refuses publication on drift. That closes the timing path forward; it does not retro-upgrade Arm 1.
  • Decision record 0035’s checks at b701285 bind future child inputs and require a size-target stop at largestExtent ≥ 21. All 204 Arm 1 rows record extent exactly 21, so its classes and headline do not move. They do not record per-row configuration or stop reason, and later code does not pretend otherwise.
  • The SDAK arm is separate. Its M1 value content was frozen at 483f7ee; 0cb52bf then recorded that freeze commit in the manifest and repinned the final gated values hash. The corrected arm-specific combined protocol hash is recorded at commit 8c781b1, but it moves with prose and is revision-pinned, not a durable gate; the values hash is the freeze. That commit also records the launch stopped about a minute in after its first line exposed the wrong-arm header. At the education audit’s 2026-07-30 10:23 PDT cutoff, its repaired canonical sweep was running; partial local output was still mutable and was not a completed, reviewed result. Forecasts of 42/90 on Arm 1’s common denominator and 42/78 under Arm 2’s own bistable exclusion are predictions, not measurements, and are never merged with Arm 1’s 3/90. Phase 6 reports the two arms together before drawing a conclusion.
  • The x64 cross-platform baseline exists; the Apple-silicon half has not run and its runbook still says MAC RUN NEEDED. No cross-platform reproducibility claim has been earned.

Who has actually checked this

Every review this project has had has been an LLM review. The code, the evidence bundles, the decision records, the sweep report, the chapters of this website including this page — all of it was written by language models and reviewed by other language models. No human snow-crystal scientist, numerical-methods specialist, or graphics engineer has read any of it.

The project does not present machine review as a substitute for expert review, and neither does this site. What it does instead is make the limit explicit and structural:

  • Rule 10 requires every review to record three things — the reviewing model and whether it shared context with the developer, what it independently re-executed, and what it did not check, stated as a limit of the evidence rather than left implicit. Gate-bearing reviews prefer a different model from the one that wrote the code.
  • Rule 9 forbids inheriting a verdict from whoever produced the artifact; the verdict is recomputed from the artifact itself.
  • Rule 13 requires an adversarial audit of any interpretation before it is published. That rule was earned the hard way: the audit that retracted the sweep report’s Finding 1 found exactly the errors it was asked to look for, but only after the claim had already spread into a memory entry, the findings ledger, and chapters of this site — each of which then needed its own correction pass.

That machinery raises the floor. It does not turn machine review into peer review. Cross-checking between models catches implementation seams, arithmetic slips, and prose that overreaches its evidence — the corrections listed on this page are all of that kind. It cannot tell you the physics is right, and any blind spot the models share stays invisible to all of them. Treat that as a disclosed limitation covering the whole of Part Two, including these references.

The disclosure is also an invitation. If you know ice physics, numerical methods for diffusion problems, or GPU compute, the files listed above are the entire record — there is no unpublished version that looks better.

About the figures

Two kinds of image appear on this site, and they are labelled differently on purpose.

  • Source figures are photographs, plots, and diagrams from the papers above. They remain copyrighted by their named authors or rightsholders and appear only in the personal offline build under citation for teaching. Each one carries a strip naming the paper, figure number, and page.
  • Original diagrams and animations are ours. Each carries a “built from” line naming the equation, table, or measurement it encodes, so you can check our arithmetic against the source.

The source figures are not stored in this repository. They are third-party copyrighted work, and committing them would republish them. On this published site they are therefore never shown: each one appears as a card naming the paper, the figure number and the page, with a link to the open-access original. The citation is always present even when the image is not. The figure list records all of them. To see the cached media inline, run the personal offline builder; authored pages do not probe research/, even when opened inside a checkout.

What we did not do

Numbers read off a graph are not measurements

Where a quantity exists in the literature only as a curve on a plot, we say so rather than reporting a number as if it had been measured. Values quoted on this site come from printed tables, printed equations, or text — not from our reading of a plotted line. Where we do describe a curve's shape, we describe it qualitatively and point you at the figure.

Where the sources disagree with each other, we say that too, rather than picking a winner quietly. Science in progress looks like this, and it is more interesting than a tidy story.

The course teaches the concepts in its registered local corpus. It does not claim a global systematic review. The recorded 2026 source-currency sweep covered Libbrecht’s arXiv output, but not every non-arXiv paper, journal erratum, later website revision, or subsequent Gravner–Griffeath publication.

Several monograph subjects are named but deliberately not expanded into full lessons: ice dendrites grown from liquid water, snow-crystal cartography, the complete numerical- methods survey, and detailed photographic craft. They are useful neighboring subjects, but expanding each would obscure this course’s main line from vapor transport through attachment kinetics to observed habit.

The locally archived transcript of a popular “snowflake mystery” video is useful communication material, not a primary scientific study. It was checked for misconceptions but is not used as evidence when the papers and monograph are available.
