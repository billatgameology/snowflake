How a snowflake is made

A snow crystal is built entirely out of water vapour, by nothing but physics, with no plan and no template anywhere in the process. This is a guided account of the registered research corpus behind that story — and then the story of somebody trying to build one in software and prove it wrong. Every scientific claim points to a source. Every interactive tells you what it encodes and where its limits begin.

How to read this site

Read it straight through

The chapters follow a dependency-aware order. A few ideas are previewed before their full treatment, and those previews are signposted; the machinery needed to judge them arrives before the course asks you to rely on them. Longer if you stop to play with the interactives, which you should. Part One (1–13) and Part Two (14–29) are each a natural sitting.

Or jump to what you want

Each chapter opens with the question it answers and closes by handing you to the next one. If you only want to know why snowflakes have six sides, Chapter 3 is self-contained enough to read alone.

Two kinds of picture

Source figures come from the published papers and carry a strip naming the paper, figure and page. Original diagrams and animations are ours and carry a “built from” line naming the equation or measurement behind them. You can always tell which is which.

Why the source photographs are not shown here

The source photographs, plots, and paper pages belong to their named authors or rightsholders; Kenneth G. Libbrecht’s work supplies most of them. We cite them but do not republish them. Where one is discussed you will find a card naming the paper, figure, and page, with a link to the open-access original.

Nothing else depends on them. The original diagrams and animations are ours and render everywhere. The generated figure list records every source image reference and its local provenance.

Contents

Twenty-nine chapters in two halves. Part One (1–13) is the science: what the object is, the two forces that shape it, the shapes themselves, and the puzzle they pose — with the answer arriving only after you have seen how the measurements behind it were made.

Part Two (14–29) is about an attempt to build one. Somebody wrote a program to grow a snow crystal from that physics and then set out to prove it wrong. It answers three questions: what the machine is meant to do, whether there is real science in it and how you could possibly check, and what computer science makes both possible. It ends with the result so far — a failure, reported while the checks that could still overturn it are unfinished, and the most useful thing in it. A Phase 3 browser development instrument already exists; the Phase 7 polished product is a separate future layer.

Part Two — Building one, and trying to prove it wrong

From here on the subject changes. Part One was about snowflakes; Part Two is about an attempt to grow one in software and hold it to the standard of an experiment. You do not need to be able to program.

Where this stood at the audit cutoff

Part Two describes work that is still in flight, and every chapter in it says so. The exam it ends with — Phase 6 — has run and produced a number, 3 of 90, but that number is not yet gate evidence: the project’s own verification work package (WP5) is still open. Its hardened independent verifier now asserts all three artifact digests, re-derives the exact 204 rows and every report field, and rejects two real forgeries its first version accepted. Seven negative controls executed; the two gaps they exposed led to decision 0035’s input/size checks and a completion-time source fingerprint for future sweeps. Those later safeguards do not retroactively add missing per-row configuration and stop-reason fields to Arm 1. The Arm 1 flagless closing gate and zero-blocker review have not happened; the Apple-silicon control is still pending; and Arm 2 is implemented and separately protocol-frozen. Its first launch was stopped about a minute in after its first line exposed Arm 1’s combined protocol hash. During this audit’s closing review, on 2026-07-30, the corrected canonical Arm 2 sweep was observed running and writing a mutable partial artifact. That dated observation is execution progress, not a completed, reviewed sweep result. The project therefore draws no Phase 6 conclusion at this cutoff. Phase 7 has not started. Read the result as measured, not as settled. Chapter 28’s status control keeps the report snapshot, committed authority and dated live observation visibly separate.

And no human expert has read any of this. Every review the project or this site has had — including the reviews of these chapters — has been an LLM review. The project’s own rules require a human domain scientist before anything is submitted anywhere; that has not happened, and it is a disclosed limitation rather than a substitute.

    Six arms should be identical. They were not — because a computer adds the same six numbers in a different order depending on which arm you are standing on.

    The Seam

    Physics says the surface moves at so many metres per second. The grid only knows ’ice’ and ’not ice’. Bridging those two is the whole job — and it is where temperature stops being a label.

  • Chapter 22

    When the Numbers Stop Changing

    The solver printed ’converged’. It had converged on the wrong answer — and the error got worse the bigger the problem got, which is the worst way for a bug to behave.

  • Chapter 23

    Walls That Pretend to Be Sky

    A real crystal grows under an effectively infinite sky. A simulated one grows in a box — and the box was quietly feeding it.
