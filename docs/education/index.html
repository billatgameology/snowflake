How a snowflake is made

A snow crystal is built entirely out of water vapour, by nothing but physics, with no plan and no template anywhere in the process. This is a guided account of the registered research corpus behind that story — and then the story of somebody trying to build one in software and prove it wrong. Scientific claims are routed to named sources, and the load-bearing interactives are checked by the verifier; this is not a machine-complete census of every possible claim. Each interactive states what it encodes and where its limits begin.

How to read this site

Read it straight through

The chapters follow a dependency-aware order. A few ideas are previewed before their full treatment, and those previews are signposted; the machinery needed to judge them arrives before the course asks you to rely on them. Longer if you stop to play with the interactives, which you should. Part One (1–13) and Part Two (14–29) are each a natural sitting.

Or jump to what you want

Each chapter opens with the question it answers and closes by handing you to the next one. If you only want to know why snowflakes have six sides, Chapter 3 is self-contained enough to read alone.

Two kinds of picture

Source figures come from the published papers and carry a strip naming the paper, figure and page. Original diagrams and animations are ours and carry a “built from” line naming the equation or measurement behind them. You can always tell which is which.

Why the source photographs are not shown here

The source photographs, plots, and paper pages belong to their named authors or rightsholders; Kenneth G. Libbrecht’s work supplies most of them. We cite them but do not republish them. Where one is discussed you will find a card naming the paper, figure, and page, with a link to the open-access original.

The canonical public pages use cited placeholders rather than loading those cached bytes; the site-authored diagrams and animations remain available without them. The generated figure list records the source-image references discovered in chapter figure metadata and their local provenance. It is not a census of all scientific claims.

Contents

Twenty-nine chapters in two halves. Part One (1–13) is the science: what the object is, the two forces that shape it, the shapes themselves, and the puzzle they pose — with the answer arriving only after you have seen how the measurements behind it were made.

Part Two (14–29) is about an attempt to build one. Somebody wrote a program to grow a snow crystal from that physics and then set out to prove it wrong. It answers three questions: what the machine is meant to do, whether there is real science in it and how you could possibly check, and what computer science makes both possible. It ends with the result so far — a failure, reported while the checks that could still overturn it are unfinished, and the most useful thing in it. A Phase 3 browser development instrument already exists; the Phase 7 polished product is a separate future layer.

Part Two — Building one, and trying to prove it wrong

From here on the subject changes. Part One was about snowflakes; Part Two is about an attempt to grow one in software and hold it to the standard of an experiment. You do not need to be able to program.

Where Phase 6 stands now

Part Two describes work that is still in flight. Phase 6 has two preserved, independently re-derived historical arms. CAK Arm 1 completed 204 rows, matched 3 of 90 headline targets and classified 6 plate, 168 neutral and 30 column cases. M1 Arm 2 also completed 204 rows, matched 54 of 90 over the common scope (54 of 78 over its own), and classified 75 plate, 119 neutral and 10 column cases. Both had zero invalid rows. These are measured-only records, not replacement-gate evidence. The CAK-to-M1 difference is confounded because broad σ0 functions and A prefactors change along with dip factors. Only ADR 0026’s conservative-intersection scoring rule is registered. R15 and M1_NO_DIP_ABLATION are planned but unfrozen, unimplemented and unexecuted; numerical adequacy, the GPU cohort and all four held-out families remain open. The cross-platform control found 9 of 448 Tier 1 entries different; that fingerprint omits −31 through −35 °C. A historical Tier 2 table reports four matching CAK rows attributed to a self-reported darwin/arm64 fixture, but its raw output and exit records are unavailable and not independently rederivable; the fixture header is not physical-host authentication. There is no CAK transfer, M1, full-grid or digit-level portability claim. Phase 7 has not started. Read the historical results as measured, not as settled. Chapter 28’s status control keeps the historical report snapshot separate from the current tracked authority.

No human domain-expert review is recorded. Every recorded non-maker reviewer-agent round — including the recorded reviews of these chapters — is an LLM review. Maker audits are recorded separately, and the maker is not identified as a domain scientist. The project’s own rules require a human domain scientist before anything is submitted anywhere; no such review is recorded, which is a disclosed limitation rather than a substitute.
