Chapter 23

Walls That Pretend to Be Sky

A real snow crystal grows under a sky that goes on forever. A simulated one grows inside a box 48 cells across. What is the box doing to the answer?

~14 min read 6 figures 3 interactives

By the end you will be able to

  • Tell consistency (the answer stops moving as you refine) apart from accuracy (the answer agrees with a known truth).
  • Design an invariance test: vary something that should not matter, and check whether it does.
  • Explain why a safeguard with an honestly stated limit is stronger than one advertised as total.
  • Say what it means to correct an expert's printed work without claiming to have beaten one.

A box with walls

Chapter 22 ended with a program that could tell you honestly when its vapour field had finished settling. This chapter asks a harder question about the same field: settled to what?

The simulation is a finite grid — in the runs that matter, a hexagonal prism carved out of a cube about 48 cells on a side, with a cell 0.35 µm across. The crystal sits in the middle and eats vapour. Vapour has to come from somewhere, and in a finite grid "somewhere" is the outermost layer of cells: the shell. Whatever rule you write for those cells is not a plumbing detail. It is a statement about what kind of world the crystal is growing in.

A real snow crystal, drifting in a cloud, is surrounded by an effectively infinite supply. It depletes the air near it, but a metre away nothing has noticed. A simulated crystal is surrounded by a wall roughly twenty cells away. If you tell that wall "hold the full cloud value here, always", you have not modelled an infinite sky. You have modelled a crystal sitting at the centre of a very small sphere of unlimited vapour — which is a different experiment, and a more generous one.

Key idea

The edge of the box is physics, not plumbing. Every rule you can write there is a different experiment, and the crystal can tell the difference.

Three edges, three experiments

The solver supports three far-field conditions — three rules for what the shell does — and it will not let you confuse them. The type that names them lives in core/src/state.ts, and its comment ends with the sentence that governs the whole chapter: "The three conditions are never mixed within a result set."

Reflecting. When the solver averages a cell against its neighbours, any neighbour that would sit outside the box is replaced by the cell itself, so nothing crosses the wall in either direction. Vapour is exactly conserved; the crystal is eating a sealed reservoir, and the supersaturation falls over the run. This is the Gravner–Griffeath rule — what the project calls the G–G-fidelity condition — and it makes total mass an exact invariant rather than an approximate one. Under the Libbrecht attachment rule it is also useless for physics, and the project says so outright: docs/attachment-kinetics.md — "A quasi-static solve with reflecting outer walls and an absorbing crystal has only the fully-depleted steady state (sigma → 0 everywhere): no physical growth claim can be made from it. It remains available for machinery tests (symmetry of the operator, determinism), never for physics."

Fixed value. The shell cells are pinned at the set supersaturation, so vapour is replenished as fast as the crystal drinks it. Mathematicians call this a Dirichlet condition — a boundary where you dictate the value rather than the flow. This is much closer to a laboratory, where an experimenter maintains the chamber conditions, and it is what the project used until Phase 6. It also breaks the exact conservation invariant on purpose, so the code meters the breakage: it counts every unit of vapour it injects at the wall, and requires that count to balance against what the crystal absorbed.

Monopole-matched. Same shell, same meter, same convergence checks — one thing different, the value each shell cell is held at. Instead of the full sky value, each cell is held at the value an infinite sky would actually have at that distance, given how fast the crystal is currently drinking.

σB(ρfar) → σ∞ − (dV/dt) / (4π · ρfar · X0 · vkin)

where
→
here means "is held at", not "tends to" — ADR 0024 writes the rule this way
σB
the supersaturation one outer-shell cell is clamped to
ρfar
the distance from the model's physical centre to that cell — per cell, not one number for the whole wall
σ∞
the supersaturation an infinitely distant sky would carry
dV/dt
how fast the crystal is gaining volume right now, summed over its surface (Eq. 5.31)
X0
the kinetic length — the distance scale at which attachment and diffusion trade places
vkin
the kinetic velocity, the speed scale set by how fast molecules hit the surface

Libbrecht, Snow Crystals, Eqs. 5.30–5.31, printed p. 207 / PDF p. 208; transcribed in docs/decisions/0024-monopole-matched-far-field.md

Read the minus sign. The correction is proportional to how fast the crystal is growing, and inversely proportional to distance — so a bigger, hungrier crystal pulls the wall value further down, and a more distant wall is pulled down less. That is what a real sky does.

Because the three are different experiments, the project treats them as different experiments. Every run records which condition it used in its checkpoint metadata — the JSON header written beside the binary field snapshot — and neither the charter nor the type that names them will let two conditions share a result set. The sentence quoted above is core/src/state.ts's; the charter backs it at §3.3 with "Results are never compared across far-field boundary conditions silently." The internal handbook states it as a trap already paid for: AGENTS.md — "Never compare results across far-field conditions silently. The checkpoint records the condition because reflecting is a finite reservoir and fixed-σ Dirichlet is replenished."

Vary what should not matter

Here is the test that broke the fixed-value wall, and it is the single most transferable idea in this half of the site. Take something that is not supposed to affect the answer, change it, and see whether the answer changes. The box size is not physics. It is a budget decision — how much empty air you can afford to simulate. If the crystal comes out different in a bigger box, the box is in your result.

Start with the idealised version, because it can be computed exactly. Chapter 22's solver has no closed-form answer, but a sphere growing in still air does: Libbrecht solves it in the monograph, and the project transcribed that solution into solver-cpu/src/spherical-reference.ts. Drag the wall in and out below and watch what a fixed-value wall does to a growth rate it is not supposed to touch.

A wall at 5.6 crystal radii inflates the growth rate by 19%. That is not a numerical error and refining the grid will not reduce it — the file says so in the comment on the field that reports it: "This is a property of the CONTINUUM problem — it is not a discretization error and refining the grid does not reduce it." It is the wrong problem, solved accurately.

Now the real thing. The same experiment was run on the actual 3-D solver: one crystal, −5 °C, σ∞ = 0.0075, grown for exactly 60 steps, in two different boxes.

291 cells in the small box, 279 in the bigger one. A 4.1% swing produced by nothing but the wall. Under monopole matching: 231 in both, with an identical aspect ratio and a symmetry error of exactly zero. The test that pins this does not check that the code runs; it checks the claim. Its own header says so:

solver-cpu/test/monopole-far-field.test.ts, the assertion at the heart of the file. Comments are verbatim.
What is assertedWhy
expect(Math.abs(df.attached − dn.attached) / dn.attached).toBeGreaterThan(0.03) "Fixed-sigma Dirichlet: the same crystal, grown the same way, comes out a different size purely because the boundary moved."
expect(mn.attached).toBe(mf.attached) "Monopole-matched: it does not." Exact equality, not a tolerance.
expect(mn.ar).not.toBe(dn.ar) "the correction is not cosmetic — it moves the habit metric, which is why no Dirichlet-measured threshold transfers to a monopole-matched sweep"

That third line is the expensive one. ADR 0024 records the aspect ratio — the number the whole validation rests on — moving from 0.5000 under fixed-σ Dirichlet to 0.3000 under monopole matching. A materially thinner plate. The ADR spells out what that costs — WP0c, in the quotation below, is the work package that freezes the sweep's settings before the sweep is allowed to run: "it means no calibration number, threshold or habit classification measured under the Dirichlet shell transfers to a monopole-matched sweep. Everything WP0c freezes has to be measured under the condition the sweep will actually run." Fixing the wall invalidated a pile of already-measured settings and forced them to be measured again.

How we know

Nobody's code was broken. The fixed-value wall did exactly what it was written to do, and did it correctly, at every grid spacing, passing every convergence check in chapter 22. It was measuring the wrong problem. The only reason anyone found out is that somebody varied a knob that was not supposed to matter and looked at the number afterwards.

Whose idea was it

It would be easy to write the last section as though the project invented monopole matching. It did not, and its own records are careful to say so. Monopole matching is Libbrecht's own published proposal — it is in the monograph, in a section called "Monopole Matching", with the 3-D equations printed. What the project's own literature sweep could not find was anyone doing it on a full 3-D lattice, or a number for what the common fixed-value wall costs.

Careful

The project's own claim boundary, written before any of this was shown to anyone. docs/stretch-sharing-and-investigation.md §S2 records the verdict as "method preempted, measurement novel", and then states the claim it will and will not make: "first quantitative measurement and removal of far-field domain-size bias in a 3D snow-crystal lattice model … A methods section or short note, framed as verifying Libbrecht's proposal; presenting the boundary condition itself as novel would be badly received and rightly so." The same file notes that the technique is also the l = 0 truncation of a classical artificial-boundary method from 1989. The physics is borrowed. The 3-D implementation and the measured number are the project's.

There is a second piece of honesty attached to the same change, and it is about paperwork rather than physics. ADR 0024 — the decision record that introduced the new wall — declared "Charter impact: none." That was wrong. The charter at the time did not merely require every run to name its wall condition; the same sentence also mandated the fixed-value wall for every Phase 6 validation run. Registering monopole-matched therefore contradicted the governing document. A later review caught it, and ADR 0027 amended the charter rather than quietly editing the ADR. The original mistake is still printed at the top of ADR 0024, struck through, with a note explaining why it is left visible: "the error is part of the record and its shape is instructive: … The test for 'charter impact' is whether the charter as written would FORBID the change, not whether the ADR's author intended to touch it."

The guard that says INVALID

Long before any of this, the project had a cruder defence against the wall: don't let the crystal get near it.

The rule is a single number. If the crystal's bounding box — the smallest box that contains it — exceeds 65% of any domain extent, the run is automatically flagged invalid. The charter gives the reason: "a crystal near the wall interacts with its own mirror image (reflecting boundary) or with a clamped edge (Dirichlet), corrupting the field silently while the shape still looks plausible. Flagged runs never enter validation results."

Silently is the operative word, and it is why this guard exists at all. A crystal that has grown into its own wall does not produce a garbled picture. It produces a perfectly pretty, perfectly symmetric, completely untrustworthy one. There is no visual symptom to notice, so the check has to be arithmetic and automatic.

Two details in that demo are not decoration. First, the guard is computed, not trusted. The sweep harness re-derives it from the geometry rather than reading whatever the run happened to print, with the reason in the comment: "the guard is a property of the geometry, and a run that reached it must be excluded whether or not the runner happened to print it." Second, a failing run is not deleted. It is excluded by name. The function that scores a sweep point carries this in its own documentation: "A run is invalid — and therefore EXCLUDED BY NAME, never dropped — if it failed to converge, broke D6h symmetry with noise off, or tripped the domain-contact guard. Those are runs that did not happen properly, not statements about the model."

The difference matters more than it looks. A pipeline that silently drops runs it dislikes can be tuned by choosing what to dislike. A pipeline that must print a reason for every exclusion cannot.

What the guard cannot prove

Now the part that makes the guard trustworthy: the project keeps writing down what it does not establish. The charter says it in one sentence, in the section that introduces the numerical-verification controls: "The 65% domain-contact guard is a collision heuristic; it does not prove the far boundary is irrelevant — only a domain-convergence study does." The internal handbook repeats it in the list of traps: "The 65% domain-contact guard detects collision; it does not prove boundary independence."

That is a claim you can check, and the project checked it. The Phase 6 convergence study grew the same two crystals — a warm plate at −5 °C and a cold one at −15 °C — to the same registered size in five different boxes, all under the monopole-matched wall, and compared the results. Every point converged, every point reported a symmetry error of exactly zero.

The smallest box the guard allows at this crystal size is 33 cells across. The smallest box that actually gives the right answer is 64. In between sits N = 40 — a domain the guard is perfectly happy with, which still reports 0.50% too many cells. The convergence report states it plainly: "The 65% domain-contact guard does not imply domain convergence. At extent 21 it requires N ≥ 33, which happens to exclude the one warm configuration that breaks — but it admits cold at N = 40, which still carries a +0.50% attached-count error. The guard is a collision heuristic and cannot be read as a convergence criterion."

Look at what the chart does show, though. The aspect ratio — the quantity that decides whether the model calls a crystal a plate or a column — is identical at all five domains, to four decimal places, at both temperatures. The classification is rock solid; the cell count underneath it is not. That split runs through the whole convergence report, and it is what licenses the sweep to make claims about habit and forbids it from making claims about volume. The registered domain is 48, chosen with its residual attached to it rather than rounded away: N = 64 is the exact answer and costs roughly three times as much per point.

Key idea

A safeguard that advertises what it catches and what it misses is stronger than one advertised as total, because the second kind quietly invites you to stop checking. "The crystal did not touch the wall" and "the wall did not affect the answer" are different sentences, and only one of them was tested.

Consistency is not accuracy

Notice what the convergence study actually proved. It compared the solver to itself at five box sizes and found that the answer stopped moving. That is real evidence, and it is not evidence of correctness. A program with a wrong constant in it will happily converge — smoothly, monotonically, beautifully — onto the wrong number.

The file that fixes this is the most quietly important one in the repository, and its opening comment is worth reading as written:

solver-cpu/src/spherical-reference.ts, lines 3–8 and 17–18, verbatim.
"Every other numerical control in this project measures the solver against ITSELF: a convergence study shows the answer stops moving under refinement, which is consistency, not accuracy. The spherical problem is the one case Libbrecht solves exactly …, so a solver of the same equations in spherical symmetry can be checked against TRUTH. That is what this file is for, and it is the only absolute accuracy anchor the project has."
"SCOPE. This is a reference for the numerics, not a model of a snow crystal. A sphere has no facets, so nothing here speaks to habit."

Both halves matter. The first is the only place in the project where the machinery meets an answer that exists independently of it. The second gives that anchor away again: a sphere is round, snow crystals are not, and nothing this file certifies says a word about plates and columns. It is a ruler for the numerics, and it refuses to be used as anything else.

The reference is also careful not to check itself with itself. Alongside the transcribed closed form there is a small independent solver of the same boundary-value problem, and its comment explains why the independence is the point: "This exists to confirm the closed form through an INDEPENDENT code path — it is not a discretization-error probe, and saying so matters, because a reference that shares a derivation with the thing it checks is not a check."

One wrong symbol

Transcribing those equations from the printed monograph is exactly the kind of task that looks like clerical work and is not. One of them did not behave.

Equation 3.36 says how much a wall at a finite distance inflates the growth rate. It uses a quantity called γ, defined one line earlier in Eq. 3.35. Printed, that definition puts the diffusive coefficient in the denominator. Every other equation in the same section is consistent with the attachment coefficient being there instead.

amplification = [ 1 − R / (γ · Rfar) ]−1

printed: γ = (a + X0/R) / (X0/R)      corrected: γ = (a + X0/R) / a

where
R
the crystal's radius
Rfar
the distance to the wall
a
the attachment coefficient — the fraction of arriving molecules that stick
X0/R
the diffusive coefficient of Eq. 3.18; large when diffusion is easy compared with attachment

Both forms as recorded in solver-cpu/src/spherical-reference.ts, the ERRATUM comment at lines 98–124

One symbol. The difference is not cosmetic: under the printed form the wall's influence is a couple of percent and barely depends on crystal size, while under the corrected form it grows with the crystal, reaching roughly 160% at one of the project's own earlier configurations. Which is precisely why the wall had to be rebuilt.

Here is the part worth learning. There is a natural check for a formula like this — send the wall to infinity and confirm the correction goes away — and the monograph states it. It passes for both forms. The bracket tends to 1 for any γ, so the one check the source gives you cannot tell the two apart. Somebody had to design checks that could.

Check 3 is the one a teenager can hold in their head, and it is the last of the three the erratum note lists: if a crystal is not growing, it is not drawing vapour, so a distant boundary cannot possibly be biasing it. The corrected form agrees — the bias falls to nothing. The printed form insists on about 22% for a crystal that is doing nothing at all.

Three independent checks, each failed by the printed form and passed by the corrected one. Then the project went looking for who was right, and found that Libbrecht already was. docs/findings-ledger.md entry B6 records it: "The corrected form is Libbrecht's own — printed correctly in JCMP 2013 Eq. (29), the very source the book cites — so the physics is his; the misprint report is ours."

Careful

This is not "an AI project beat a physicist". A one-symbol transcription slip between a researcher's own 2013 paper and his own 2019 book is a proofreading failure, not a physics failure, and the correct version was in print the whole time under the same author's name. The project's register files it as "a courtesy erratum, not research", and the recommended action is a friendly email framed as "the book's Eq. 3.35 contradicts its own cited source" — maker-gated, still unsent, with one more place a published correction could be hiding still to be checked.

Two things about that erratum are worth keeping. It was found because somebody needed the equation to work, not because somebody was proofreading — you find printed mistakes by depending on them. And it survived years in print because the check its own author supplied could not detect it. When a source hands you a sanity check, that is one check. It is not verification.

What is still open

Monopole matching does not make box size irrelevant. It moves the point where box size starts to matter further out, and — this is the useful part — it makes the residual measurable. At the crystal size used for that probe, boxes of 24 cells and up all return 81 attached cells; a 20-cell box returns 105. Something changes below a threshold.

ADR 0024 first blamed the point-source approximation — treating the whole crystal as a single point when you work out what the sky value should be at the wall. If that were the cause, the thing that decided whether a box was big enough would be a ratio: how far the nearest wall sits from the centre, divided by the crystal's half-width. The convergence study tested that and destroyed it. One configuration breaks at a ratio of 1.74; a different one is bit-for-bit identical all the way down to a ratio of 1.24 — a tighter squeeze that does not break. Absolute clearance in cells does not sort them either, and the first correction you would add to a point source predicts the wrong one to be the fragile one. The report's verdict — a multipole argument being that same point-source picture with its corrections added back on — is blunt: "Both metrics say the smaller crystal is the more sensitive one, which is the opposite of what a multipole argument predicts, so the residual is probably not a multipole-truncation effect at all. It is not resolved here what the governing quantity is."

Still unsettled

Nobody knows what sets the limit. Rather than invent a scaling rule, the project wrote down the operative consequence — the minimum box has to be measured at the configuration that will actually be run, because there is no rule available to extrapolate one configuration's limit to another — and left the mechanism on the open list. The correction also lags one growth step behind, because the wall has to be set before the calculation that would tell you what to set it to. That lag is registered as a real approximation, not a bookkeeping convenience, and it is untested at large steps.

And one limitation covers everything on this page. Every review this project has had — including the two-reviewer process that checked this chapter — has been a review by a large language model. No human domain scientist has read any of it. The project's process requirements — docs/stretch-sharing-and-investigation.md §4, item 3 — state the standard it is holding itself to and admit it has not met it yet: "At least one human domain scientist reads any manuscript before submission. All reviews to date, internal and external-facing, are LLM reviews; for publication that is a disclosed limitation, not a substitute." The right way to read the last three chapters is therefore: here is machinery that has survived every test its builders could devise, and that is a strictly weaker statement than "here is machinery that is right".

That is Part VII finished. The ladder of gates that forbids physics before machinery, the grid, the arithmetic, the seam where temperature enters, the two convergence tests, and now the wall. Every one arrived because something measurable went wrong first, and every one has its known weaknesses written down next to it. Phase 6 — the validation itself — is in flight as this is written; Phase 7, the product, has not started.

The machinery is now trustworthy enough to be pointed at something. In chapter 24 it gets pointed at a crystal that is supposed to starve in its own middle and hollow out — and the first thing that happens is that the most obvious instrument in the project, the human eye looking at a picture, gets demoted.
