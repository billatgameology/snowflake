Chapter 3

Why Six Sides

A snow crystal has six of everything — six corners, six arms, six flat walls. Six is not decoration and it is not a coincidence. So where does the six come from?

  • Follow the chain from one bent molecule, with a bond angle of 104.5 degrees, to a ring of six — and show for yourself why a ring of three, four or five cannot close.
  • Say what actually separates hexagonal ice from cubic ice — it is one twist.

Where, exactly, is a snowflake made?

Kepler guessed in 1611 that the snowflake’s six corners were a stack showing through, and he guessed the wrong stack: cannonballs, crowded as tightly as they will go. Three hundred and eighteen years later Barnes’s X-ray spots showed the right one. This chapter is that stack — the arrangement water molecules actually take up in ice, and why it ends up with six of everything.

One short detour first, because it settles which stack we are even discussing. Water is not a substance with one solid form; it has about fifteen known ones, and before you can ask how ice stacks you have to know which ice snow is made of. The answer is one short arc of one line on one chart, so this takes a page, and then we are at the lattice for good.

The chart is called a phase diagram — a map you read by picking a temperature and a pressure and looking up which state of matter wins there.

Water’s map is enormous. The version below runs from absolute zero to 650 kelvin along the top edge — the same axis appears in Celsius along the bottom — and from one pascal to one terapascal up the side, twelve powers of ten of pressure. Libbrecht calls it “a complicated beast… including some 15 known forms of ice” (printed p. 47), and almost all of those forms need crushing pressures you would only ever meet inside a laboratory press or the interior of an icy moon.

Snow crystals occupy one short arc of one line on that map. Find the triple point, the single temperature and pressure where solid, liquid and vapour can all coexist — on this chart it is labelled 273.16 K and 611.657 Pa. Now follow the boundary between ice and vapour down and to the left from there. That line, from 0 °C down to about −40 °C, is where every snow crystal that has ever fallen was assembled. It is circled in red.

Vapour turning directly into solid is called deposition. It is the reverse of the frost that vanishes off a freezer shelf without ever getting wet. This one fact removes a great deal of physics from the rest of this site: once it is growing, a snow crystal never has to freeze anything, because every molecule it adds arrives out of the air as vapour and lands straight onto solid ice. Only the very first speck was ever liquid — a cloud droplet that froze, as Chapter 1 describes — and that one brief liquid moment comes back to matter later in this chapter. After it, growth is catching molecules, nothing else.

There is a limit to what the map can tell you, and it is worth stating plainly, because it is the reason the rest of this site exists. A phase diagram shows the lowest-energy state at each temperature and pressure — it shows where a system ends up if you leave it alone forever. In the monograph’s words, “Each line in Figure 2.1 tells us under what conditions water will change phase, but it says nothing about the nonequilibrium physical processes that define the character of that phase transition” (printed p. 48). The map says ice wins. It does not say what the ice looks like when it arrives. Libbrecht is candid about why he spends a whole book on this one line and not the others: “We restrict our attention to one particular phase transition because it has proven itself worthy of study, plus the others remain almost completely unexplored” (printed p. 48).

Key idea

Every snow crystal is built on the ice/vapour line below the triple point, between about 0 °C and −40 °C. Water vapour becomes ice directly, with no liquid stage in between.

It starts with one awkward angle

A water molecule is one oxygen atom with two hydrogens attached. It is not straight. The two oxygen–hydrogen bonds meet at an angle of 104.5 degrees. That number is the seed of everything in this chapter, for one reason: it is close to 109.5 degrees.

109.5 degrees is the tetrahedral angle. Put a point at the centre of a regular tetrahedron — a pyramid with four triangular faces — and draw lines out to its four corners. Any two of those lines meet at 109.5 degrees. It is the angle you get when four directions spread out as evenly as four directions possibly can in three dimensions.

Water’s 104.5 misses it by five degrees. Five degrees is nothing. And in ice, that near miss decides the architecture. Each oxygen atom in the lattice ends up with four oxygen–hydrogen bonds radiating from it: two covalent bonds, the tight ones holding its own two hydrogens, and two hydrogen bonds, the weaker ones reaching across to hydrogens belonging to neighbouring molecules. The monograph puts the consequence in one sentence: “Because of this near match, the four O-H bonds emanating from each oxygen atom in the ice crystal (two tight bonds, two weaker bonds) are essentially in a tetrahedral arrangement” (printed p. 49).

So every oxygen in ice sits at the centre of its own little tetrahedron of bonds. That is the single building block, and the rest of the chapter is about what happens when you stack it.

There is a rule keeping the hydrogens tidy, first written down by John Bernal and Ralph Fowler in 1933: exactly one hydrogen atom sits between each adjacent pair of oxygen atoms. Never zero, never two. This is why ice can be thought of as a stack of whole water molecules rather than a soup of loose atoms — as the monograph notes, “two hydrogen atoms closely flank each oxygen atom, so the trio forms an essentially intact H2O molecule” (printed p. 48).

The rule is looser than it sounds, and the looseness is worth knowing about. The oxygen skeleton of ice is rigidly fixed. The hydrogens are not. You can rotate individual water molecules into an enormous number of different arrangements and still satisfy Bernal and Fowler on every bond. Libbrecht records that this ambiguity “appears to play no important role in snow crystal growth, but it is a basic feature of the ice Ih lattice” (printed p. 49). Remember it as a general lesson: the thing that fixes a crystal’s shape is where the heavy atoms sit, not where every last atom sits.

Careful

You will sometimes read that snowflakes are six-sided “because water molecules are hexagonal”. A water molecule is not hexagonal. It has three atoms and one angle in it. Nothing about a single molecule is six-fold. The hexagon is a property of how molecules pack together, and it only appears once molecules start bonding to each other — which is exactly what makes it worth explaining rather than asserting.

Stack tetrahedra, get a honeycomb

Here is the step that answers the chapter’s question. Take that four-bonds-in-a-tetrahedron unit and let it repeat: every molecule bonds to its neighbours the same way, at the same angle, over and over. Nobody imposes a shape from outside. What comes out is a repeating three-dimensional pattern — a lattice — and the one nature builds on Earth is called ice Ih. The h stands for hexagonal, and it is the only form of ice commonly found on the Earth’s surface, including in snow crystals. Be careful with the word the in that sentence, though: the tetrahedral angle permits exactly two lattices, not one, and the second one is the subject of the next section. That is what keeps “why six” a real question rather than a formality.

So where does the six come from, in the lattice we actually get? Count hops. Start on one oxygen atom, step along a bond to the next oxygen, and keep stepping, always leaving by a bond that points at another corner of the tetrahedron. The question is how many steps it takes to arrive back where you started — and the honest way to answer it is to try to build the loop and watch it fail.

Two facts settle it, and you can check both without taking anything on trust. The first is plane geometry. A flat ring made of equal bonds has a corner angle fixed by how many bonds are in it: three bonds give corners of 60 degrees, four give 90, five give 108, six give 120. (The rule is (n − 2) × 180, divided by n — the same rule that makes the three angles of a triangle add to 180.) The second fact is about what folding does. Lift some atoms of a closed loop and drop others, and its corners can only get sharper. Never wider. A flat loop is the most open a loop of that many bonds will ever be.

Put those two together and the answer falls out. A loop can only sit at the tetrahedral 109.5 degrees if its flat version already reaches that far, because folding can only bring the corners down from there. Three falls short by nearly fifty degrees. Four falls short by nineteen and a half. Five is the near miss of the whole story — 108 degrees, short by one and a half, close enough to be irritating and still not close enough. Six is the first loop that clears the tetrahedral angle, and it clears it by overshooting, at 120 — and an overshoot is something you can give back. Buckle the ring so that three oxygens ride slightly high and three slightly low, and the corners close from 120 down to 109.5 exactly, with every bond still the same length as every other and not one of them bent. That buckled ring is the thing the rest of this chapter is built on, so build one.

Longer loops close too. Eight will do it, and ten, and twelve: each overshoots when flat and each buckles back down. But six is the shortest loop this geometry permits, and in the finished lattice every longer one turns out to be several six-loops sharing edges, the way a patch of honeycomb has a big outline made entirely of small cells. The shortest loop is the one you see when you look at the lattice.

So here is what a whole plane of those rings tiles into. Molecules drift, lock on at their fixed angle, and the arrangement they settle into — nobody chose it for them — is a honeycomb of six-membered rings. One thing to hold on to as you watch: this is the view straight down onto the plane, the same view as the first panel of Figure 2.2, so the buckle you just built points straight at you and flattens to nothing. It is still there. Every ring in this honeycomb has three oxygens riding a little above the page and three a little below.

Now for the part that is easy to miss and impossible to unsee. The hexagons are not visible from everywhere. They appear along one specific direction through the crystal, called the c-axis. Look down the c-axis and the six-membered rings are unmistakable. Rotate the same lattice by ninety degrees and they vanish into rows. Key idea

The six comes from the lattice, not from the molecule. In the ice Ih lattice the shortest closed loop of oxygen atoms is a ring of six, and those rings give the crystal six equivalent directions to grow in. A snowflake’s symmetry is that ring, scaled up by a factor of several million. Note that it is the Ih lattice specifically that does this — near-tetrahedral bonding on its own is not enough, because it also allows a second lattice that is not hexagonal at all.

One precision point, because it gets garbled everywhere. The lattice makes the six directions equivalent. It guarantees that a molecule arriving on one arm faces exactly the same molecular landscape as a molecule arriving on any of the other five. It does not guarantee that the six arms come out looking the same — that depends on whether all six experienced the same conditions on the way down, which is a separate question and a much later chapter.

Why not cubic?

Here is the promised second option, and it is the reason “why six” is worth a chapter. Tetrahedral bonding does not force a hexagon. It leaves exactly two ways to build a lattice, and nature on Earth picks one of them. Had it picked the other, everything above would still be true about the bonds and none of it would be true about the six.

The monograph is direct about it: “Given this bond structure, there are two ways to form a crystal lattice: the hexagonal (ice-like) structure and a cubic (diamond-like) structure… The difference between these two structures comes down to a twist in the bonding of adjacent tetrahedra separating the basal planes” (printed p. 49). Go back to Figure 2.4 above and compare the two sketches properly this time. Same atoms, same four-bond tetrahedra, same bond lengths. One connecting bond is rotated. Choose the left version everywhere and you get ordinary hexagonal ice, ice Ih. Choose the right version everywhere and you get ice Ic, which has cubic symmetry — the same arrangement carbon uses in diamond.

There is a second way to see the same choice, and it needs no chemistry at all. It does need one warning first, because Chapter 2 spent a page killing sphere-stacking as an explanation of the hexagon: Kepler had the right idea and the wrong stack, water molecules are not free to crowd like cannonballs, and it is the bond angle rather than tight packing that forces the six. That verdict stands, and nothing below reopens it. The spheres here are not molecules. Each one is a stand-in for a whole layer of the lattice, and every one of those layers is already hexagonal for the reasons you have just read. The only question left is which of two landing positions each layer takes as it goes down.

So: stack spheres. Lay down a first layer packed as tightly as it will go. The second layer has only one sensible home: the dimples in the first. But the third layer has a genuine choice, because a close-packed layer offers two different sets of dimples. Put the third layer directly above the first and keep doing that, and you build the Ih pattern. Put it in the other set of dimples and keep doing that, and you build Ic. Mix your choices and you get something with a name of its own.

A crystal that changes its mind from layer to layer is stacking disordered. It is not a defect in the usual sense of a missing atom or a crack — every bond is perfectly satisfied. It is a crystal that is locally fine and globally undecided.

And it leaves fingerprints. Some snow crystals are twins: two crystals fused along a shared internal plane. Some twins give themselves away only by a groove, and otherwise pass for perfectly ordinary crystals; others — arrowheads and crossed plates, which this chapter does not show — do not look six-fold at all. The example below is the first kind: a twin column, two ordinary columns that fit together perfectly at the shared plane, because the usual hexagonal bonds along it have been replaced by a plane of cubic ones.

Where does such a plane come from? This is where the crystal’s one liquid moment comes back. Two words first. The moment a droplet first turns to ice is called nucleation. Just before it happens the water is supercooled — still liquid, but already below its freezing point — and the colder it is, the more abruptly the freezing runs. Libbrecht’s answer is that a twin plane “likely originates when the initial crystal nucleation (typically from a liquid water droplet) produces some stacking disorder and at least one plane of cubic bonds. Nucleation can be a somewhat violent event, as the supercooling just prior to nucleation is often quite high” (printed p. 66). Ice makes its stacking choice in a hurry, and occasionally gets one layer wrong. That is what is meant by the remark that cubic ice bonding “appears to play a small role in normal snow crystal structures” (printed p. 50).

Still unsettled

Nobody has ever definitively observed pure ice Ic — not in a laboratory and not in nature. Samples made in the lab are “generally stacking-disordered to a substantial degree and are thus essentially mixtures of ice Ic and Ih” (printed p. 51). No faceted “cubic” snow crystal has ever been made, “although this would certainly be an interesting sight” (printed p. 50). There is even evidence that ice Ic exists as tiny grains in high-altitude clouds, “although this is not known with certainty” (printed p. 51). That uncertainty is why the Ih/Ic line on the phase diagram at the top of this page is drawn dotted: it is not a proper phase boundary, and it is not well understood.

Basal, prism, c and a

Set the cubic exceptions aside. The crystal this site is about is ice Ih. You met its simplest shape in Chapter 1 — the hexagonal prism, a six-sided pencil, with two basal facets at the ends and six prism facets running round the waist. What that vocabulary is missing is directions, and the lattice has just handed us two families of those. So here they are, to go with the two families of face.

  • The c-axis runs through the two ends, perpendicular to the basal face — the way a pencil lead runs. It is the axis you were looking straight down in the first panel of Figure 2.2.
  • The six a-axes lie in the basal plane and point out at the six corners of the hexagon — which is where the six arms of a stellar crystal will eventually grow out.

Crystallographers write all of this with Miller–Bravais indices. You will never have to decode a set of digits on this site, but the strings themselves recur for the rest of it, so it is worth knowing what kind of thing each one names. Curly brackets name a family of flat faces; square brackets name a direction; and the bar over a digit is a minus sign, written on top to save space, so 1̄ reads as minus one. Three strings do nearly all the work here: {0001} is the flat ends, {1̄010} is the six side walls, and {112̄0} is a third family you meet in a moment, which never facets at all. The four digits are counted against the four axes drawn in Figure 2.5 — three of them lying in the flat hexagonal face, the fourth running up the c-axis.

Now the detail that should nag at you. There is a third family of planes in the ice lattice, the {112̄0} planes, each one perpendicular to an a-axis. They are sometimes called the secondary prism faces. And growing ice crystals are not observed to form facets on them — the monograph’s own caption for the figure below says the {112̄0} surface “does not exhibit any known faceting” (printed p. 52). The figure cuts one honeycomb two different ways so you can see why anyone would expect that. Against a prism plane the lattice ends on a short, even beat; against a {112̄0} plane it ends raggedly, with atoms standing proud of the line and gaps between them. The monograph’s summary of the difference is deliberately mild: the prism surfaces have “a somewhat simpler lattice structure” than the {112̄0} surfaces (printed p. 52). That is a difference of degree, not a difference between neat and chaotic. Hold on to it anyway — it is the first sign that ice grows flat walls in some directions and not others.

Which means flat faces are not simply “where the lattice happens to end”. The lattice offers plenty of planes. Ice facets readily on only two of them — and, far more rarely, on a third we will meet shortly. Something must be choosing, and the lattice alone is not enough to say what. The figure below is as far as the lattice can take you, and it is worth seeing exactly how far that is.

How we know

Matching the {1̄010} planes to the flat walls you see on a real snow crystal sounds like bookkeeping, but it was an assumption for a long time. From the early days of X-ray crystallography it was thought that {1̄010} were the prism facets, on the lattice-cutting grounds described above. Nobody had seen it. The guess turned out to be right, but as the monograph records, it “was only definitively confirmed by direct observation rather recently” (printed p. 52) — in work published in 2017, roughly a century after the argument was first made. That is worth sitting with: a structural argument this reasonable is still only a hypothesis until somebody manages to look, and the looking took a hundred years to become possible.

Put a nanometre in perspective. A synthetic snow crystal photographed for the monograph measured 2.3 mm from tip to tip (printed p. 46). Divide that by a0 and you get about five million lattice repeats across a single crystal — five million rings of six, all sharing one orientation. That is what “a snowflake is a magnified molecular ring” actually means as a number.

One piece of notation in that last row. The inverse tangent, written tan−1, is the operation that turns a ratio of two lengths back into the angle they make — the calculator button you would use to get a slope’s angle from its rise over its run. And this one is literally a rise over a run, so it is worth seeing where the ratio comes from rather than accepting it. A pyramidal facet is not a smooth ramp. It is a staircase of lattice sites, and that staircase moves sideways by exactly one prism column for every two basal layers it drops. One prism column is (√3/2) a0 wide. Two basal layers are 2 × c0/2, which is just c0, tall. So the run is (√3/2) a0, the rise is c0, and the angle the staircase makes with the vertical prism wall is tan−1(run / rise) — which is tan−1(√3 a0 / 2c0), exactly the expression in the table. Put the two measured lengths in and it comes out at 28.0 degrees. That is a phone-calculator’s worth of work, and you should do it.

That last row is the payoff, and it is worth dwelling on. Nothing about it was measured on a crystal. It is what you get by dividing one lattice constant by another and taking an inverse tangent. If the lattice model of ice is right, then wherever ice grows a pyramidal facet — a slanted face cutting the corner between an end and a side wall — that face has no freedom about its angle. It must sit 28.0 degrees from the prism facet. Drag the two lengths below and watch the angle obey.

And pyramidal facets are real. They have been photographed on snow crystals collected at the South Pole. They are rare: the evidence suggests they form only at quite low temperatures, below −20 °C, and perhaps only in air rather than in a near-vacuum. Little is known about how they grow.

What the lattice does not explain

It is tempting to stop here and declare the mystery solved. Ice is hexagonal, therefore snowflakes are hexagonal, the end. That inference is doing far more work than it can carry, and the most common version of it is wrong in an interesting way.

Careful

The usual popular explanation is that a snow crystal grows flat faces because flat faces are the lowest-energy faces, so the crystal settles into the shape that costs least. It is true that the basal and prism planes cut the lattice in a particularly smooth way, and true that this gives them a lower-than-average surface energy. But that is not what makes the facets. The same smoothness also makes it hard for an arriving molecule to attach at all — and, in Libbrecht’s words, “The latter property, more than the former, controls the formation of snow crystal facets” (printed p. 46). Facets are a story about rates, not about energy minimisation. Chapter 5 unpacks it properly.

So take from this chapter what it genuinely earns. The lattice explains the six. It tells you why the shortest ring in ice is six atoms round rather than five or eight, why the six directions that ring opens up are 60 degrees apart, why the two flat ends behave as one family and the six side walls as another, and why every angle in a faceted ice crystal is arithmetic rather than an accident. That is a great deal for two measured lengths and one bond angle.

It also tells you what it cannot do. Essentially every ice crystal you will ever meet — the frost on a window, a hailstone, a snowflake — is built on the same ice Ih lattice, with the same a0 and the same c0. Yet on a single winter afternoon the same cloud can produce thin plates, stubby blocks and long slender columns. The lattice is identical in all of them. So whatever decides between a plate and a column is not in the lattice at all. It is in what happens at the crystal’s surface, molecule by molecule, as it grows.

The lattice explains why six. It says nothing about whether the crystal becomes a plate, a column or a star — that is decided at the surface, one arriving molecule at a time. And before we can look at the surface, we have to know what is being delivered to it: how much water vapour the surrounding air is carrying, and how it gets there.
