Chapter 13

The Frontier

What is still unknown about the thing landing on your sleeve — and why has so little of it been worked out?

~17 min read 10 figures 4 original graphics

By the end you will be able to

  • Tell a measurement from a fitted curve, a fitted curve from a line drawn to guide the eye, and any of those from a formula chosen because it was convenient.
  • Name half a dozen specific places where the account of snow crystals runs out, and say for each one whether it is genuinely hard or has simply not been done.
  • Explain why a field with almost nobody in it is a reason a problem stays open, not evidence that the problem is unimportant.
  • Say what a scientist means by “we do not know” — and why it is a position rather than a failure.

The researcher behind most of the measurements on this site calls what he does “‘forbidden’ research, because there is no way any of the usual funding agencies will touch it” (printed p. 41). He is not being dramatic. He is describing his budget. That is the field that produced the explanation you accepted last chapter.

Recall what that explanation was. Two curves, one for the flat top and bottom faces and one for the six side faces, tracking how hard it is to start a new layer of ice on each. Whichever curve is lower, that face grows more easily. When the flat top and bottom are the easy ones the crystal grows taller, into a column; when the six side faces are the easy ones it spreads sideways, into a plate. The curves cross, and at every crossing the crystal switches from the one to the other.

That is a real explanation, built on real measurements. It is also, in the words of the people who built it, a working hypothesis with ill-defined curves. This chapter is about how to hold both of those thoughts at once — and about the rather long list of things that nobody has worked out at all.

Four kinds of line

Every chart in this site has lines on it. They are not all the same kind of object, and the difference matters more than almost anything else you can learn about reading science. There are four kinds, and published papers usually tell you which is which — in the caption, in one clause, easy to skim past.

A measurement is a set of points. Somebody grew a crystal, timed it, measured it, and each point is the result. It comes with error bars, because every measurement is a range rather than a value. A fit is a smooth curve pushed through those points. The curve is not a measurement; it is a summary, and its shape was chosen by a person. An eye guide is a line drawn through data with no equation behind it at all — it is there so your eye can follow the trend, and it makes no claim beyond that. A convenient shape is a formula picked because it is easy to write down and happens to look right, with numbers in it tuned until the curve lands near the data.

The same measurements appear in the paper they came from, and there the caption is blunter about the line drawn through them: “Measured values of σ0,prism,SDAK(T) (data points) obtained from growth measurements taken at σfar = 32%. The dotted line was drawn to guide the eye”. (σ0,prism,SDAK is the height of the barrier to starting a new layer on a narrow side face; σfar is how much spare water vapour the air well away from the crystal is carrying — here 32% more water vapour than air sitting in equilibrium with ice can hold. Supersaturation is always measured against ice, never against liquid water; the same air reads a very different number the other way, which is why Chapter 4 kept the two apart.) Nine words at the end of a caption tell you that the most memorable feature of the graph — the swooping dotted curve — is not a result.

How we know

Uncertainty is not decoration either. The photographic survey that maps out which shape grows where states its own error bars plainly: temperature uncertainties “typically ±0.2 C”, and supersaturations that lie “between 0.8 and 1.2 times the stated values” (arXiv:2306.13087v1, pp. 7–8). So the horizontal position of every crystal in that survey is good to about two-tenths of a degree, and its vertical position could be a fifth out in either direction. Any conclusion that depends on a shift smaller than that is not a conclusion.

Careful

An equation is not the same thing as an explanation. It is easy to assume that once a formula is printed, the physics behind it is understood. Often the formula came first: a shape was chosen because it was simple, and its numbers were adjusted until it matched. The author of the curves in Chapters 11 and 12 says exactly that about his own — the fourth panel of the animation above quotes him on it. The numbers come from experiments. The shape is a convenience.

The answer is a working hypothesis

Now turn that lens on the explanation you just accepted. Chapter 12 needed two dips: a region near −5 °C where the top and bottom faces suddenly become easy to grow, and a region near −15 °C where the side faces do. Without them the two curves cross once, and the real world flips three times. The dips are what make the explanation work.

Here is what is known about those dips, stated as precisely as the sources state it.

They are measured, in part. Two dedicated experiments went after them directly — one centred near −4 °C, one near −14 °C, which the monograph’s own figure rounds to −5 and −15 — and both produced real data points with error bars. Figure 4.26 above is one of them; here is the other. The dips are not invented.

Their shape, however, was chosen. Where the dips sit and how wide they are did not come out of any theory of the ice surface. They were selected to make the answer come out right — and “right” here means reproducing the Nakaya diagram, the map of shape against temperature and humidity from Chapter 7: “With little guidance from molecular theory, the positions and widths of the SDAK dips shown in Figure 4 were judiciously chosen to provide a reasonable explanation for the temperature-dependent behaviors seen in the Nakaya diagram” (arXiv:2009.08404v2, p. 5). A companion paper is blunter still: “Our understanding of the molecular dynamics of the ice surface is not sufficient to realistically quantify the SDAK-1 effect on the principal facets, or even to roughly estimate the temperatures of the two SDAK dips” (arXiv:2011.02353v1, p. 4).

You can read the choosing straight off the page. This is the whole printed expression for the basal barrier with its dip included:

σ0,basal(T) = ( 0.02 T1.75 + 0.3 ) · ( 1 − 0.87 exp( −(log T − log 4.5)2 / 0.07 ) )

where
σ0,basal
how hard it is to start a new layer of ice on the flat top or bottom face, in percent. Bigger means harder, so a smaller number means that face grows more easily
T
how many degrees below freezing the air is, so T = 5 means −5 °C. The paper says only “temperature … in degrees Celsius”, but a logarithm only works on a positive number, and a centre at 4.5 lines up with the dip measured near −4 °C — so degrees below freezing is the reading that makes sense
first bracket
the broad-face curve, the well-grounded part. Its numbers come from growth experiments on wide facets
second bracket
the dip factor. 1 means “no dip here”; at the very bottom of the dip it is 0.13, so the barrier falls to about an eighth of its broad-face value
0.87
how deep the dip goes. Chosen, and the least anchored of the three: no measured depth for the basal dip is printed anywhere in these papers. The only printed dip depth of any kind is for the prism dip, which is said to fall to “about one-tenth of its broad-facet value” at −14 °C (arXiv:2012.12916v1, p. 9)
4.5
where the dip is centred, in degrees below freezing. Chosen to sit on the measured dip, not derived from any theory of the ice surface — the dedicated experiment puts the basal dip’s centre near −4 °C, and 4.5 is a round number in that neighbourhood
0.07
how wide the dip is. Chosen, not derived — widths are one of the two things the paper says outright were “judiciously chosen”
log
the logarithm, which is how these curves are plotted — it makes the dip symmetric on the graph rather than in degrees. Which logarithm (base 10, or natural) is not stated. The chart below uses the natural one, which is a reading, not a reported value

Libbrecht, arXiv:2306.13087v1, p. 6 — the printed expression, with its two missing closing brackets supplied

Two small things about that line are worth noticing, since this chapter is about how sources state things. As it stands in the paper it is missing the brackets that would close it off — they are supplied above so the expression can be read — and it does not say which logarithm it means. Neither is a scandal; both are the ordinary texture of a working document rather than a polished result. From here on this site writes the second bracket on its own as Dbasal(T) — the dip factor — because that bracket is the part this chapter is about. The name is ours, not the paper’s.

Three numbers in that second bracket — the depth 0.87, the centre 4.5, the width 0.07 — were written down rather than derived, and no theory of the ice surface says what they should be. They are not all equally loose. The centre has a measurement standing behind it; the width was chosen to fit; the depth has nothing printed behind it at all. That is not a scandal. It is an honest placeholder, and it is labelled as one. The chart below lets you move all three and watch what the explanation does.

The measurements have gaps of their own. The prism dip curve was mapped at a single humidity, σfar = 32%, though a second run at 22% did confirm where its minimum sits: “A simple quadratic fit yields the peak location at T = -14.15 ± 0.15 C. This temperature also corresponds to the minimum of the prism SDAK dip seen in Figure 18” (arXiv:2009.08404v2, Figure 19 caption, p. 16). How the whole curve moves as the air gets wetter or drier is still open. And where the effect is strongest, the description breaks: “The measurements near the peak at -14 C do not fit the simple exponential functional form, but the fit is reasonable on either side of the peak”, and “this simple picture of σ0,prism,SDAK(T) is not adequate to fully describe the growth behavior near -14 C … when the SDAK effect is especially strong” (both arXiv:2009.08404v2, p. 15). The explanation is weakest exactly at the point it is trying to explain.

Still unsettled

The status of the whole idea is stated in one sentence by the people who proposed it: “We still treat the SDAK phenomenon as a working hypothesis … the SDAK phenomenon provides the only viable option currently available that can adequately explain the Nakaya diagram together with a plethora of other ice-growth data” (arXiv:2306.13087v1, p. 5). Read that carefully. “The only viable option currently available” is a much weaker claim than “correct”. It means: nobody has anything better, and that is the honest reason to use it.

The monograph itself anticipates the objection and answers it without flinching: “Of course, the critical reader may balk at the speculative nature of this model, along with its complexity and ill-defined curves. Fair enough.” The defence follows immediately — the broad-face curves are well grounded in experiments, and the dips “or something similarly complex, are required to explain a substantial body of additional data” (printed p. 153). Something has to make the shape flip three times. The dips are a candidate that fits a lot of measurements. They are not a proof.

The same researcher has publicly withdrawn several of his own earlier analyses of these quantities — “I now believe that the analysis for αbasal and αprism in that paper was largely incorrect” (arXiv:2004.06212v1, p. 7) is one of them. That is what a live field looks like from the inside. Results get withdrawn by the people who published them.

Six places the account runs out

Below the working hypothesis sits a layer of plain unknowns. These are not vague philosophical gaps. Each one is a specific, stateable, unfinished job. Five of them are open because they are hard, and the sixth is open because somebody chose to leave it for later — and telling those two apart is the skill this section is really for.

1. Nobody has ever definitively seen pure cubic ice. Water can in principle stack in two ways: the ordinary hexagonal way (ice Ih), which gives you six-sided snow crystals, and a cubic way (ice Ic). Cubic ice has been made in the laboratory at low temperatures, but the real thing has never been isolated: “to my understanding, pure cubic ice Ic has not even been definitively observed in the laboratory or in nature; laboratory samples are generally stacking-disordered to a substantial degree and are thus essentially mixtures of ice Ic and Ih” (printed pp. 50–51). “Stacking-disordered” means the two stacking styles are jumbled together inside one crystal. Whether cubic snow crystals exist somewhere in a very cold cloud is open, and what stands in the way is that the samples keep coming out mixed.

2. Nobody knows what shape a snow crystal would settle into if you just left it alone. Every crystal in this site is a growing crystal, driven by an excess of vapour. Take that away and let it come to rest, and the crystal has an equilibrium shape — the shape with the least surface energy. For ice, one experiment tried, in 1985 — “the sole experimental investigation of the subject” — and its result is not trusted, because the crystal almost certainly never got there: “the time needed to reach full equilibrium is so long … that the true ice/vapor ECS has probably not been definitely observed” (printed p. 65). The reason is beautifully mundane. Starting from a slightly lumpy crystal 50 micrometres in radius — about a tenth of a millimetre across — settling into equilibrium takes about eight days (same page). Nobody has the patience, or an apparatus that holds still that long.

3. The surface of ice is slushy, and nobody has connected that to growth. Near the melting point the topmost molecular layers of ice lose their order and behave a little like liquid — a quasi-liquid layer, first predicted by Michael Faraday in 1859. It is real; many experiments see it. It has been suspected for decades of being the thing that governs how snow crystals grow. And yet: “For the present, there seems to be no concrete, well-established physical connection between surface melting and snow crystal growth, although this situation may change at any time” (printed p. 55). The layer reaches about one molecule thick around −10 °C and gets rapidly thicker as you approach 0 °C, which is suggestive — and suggestive is as far as it goes.

What stands in the way is that nobody can line the answers up. Nothing images individual molecules on a growing snow crystal, so the evidence arrives indirectly, and in pieces that do not match: “different experiments measure different surface properties, and it is not always clear how to compare different results”, while for the computed pictures like the one below, “quantitative comparison with experiments remains a nontrivial challenge” (both printed p. 55).

4. There is a third kind of flat face, and its key number has never been measured. Almost every snow crystal shows two kinds of facet: the flat top and bottom, and the six sides. Very occasionally, in very cold air, a third kind appears — slanted pyramidal faces. The quantity that decides how hard a face is to grow is its step energy — the energy cost of the exposed edge of a new one-molecule-high layer sitting on a flat face — and for these faces it is unknown: “only the basal and prism step energies have been measured to date” (printed p. 54). Notice which way the reasoning has to run without it. There is no account of why pyramidal facets appear only below about −20 °C; instead the monograph guesses backwards, from the sightings to the missing number — the pyramidal step energy “likely becomes substantial at low temperatures, as pyramidal facets apparently only form in quite cold conditions” — and then labels the guess: “These are both speculative statements” (printed p. 54).

5. A whiff of the wrong vapour rewrites the shape, and nobody understands why. This one is startling. Add 10 parts per million of butyl alcohol to the air and crystals at −20 °C grow as columns instead of plates. Trace silicone vapour turns the thin plates at −15 °C into columns. Acetic acid changes shapes at concentrations as low as 1 part per million. The list of such observations runs from the 1940s to the 2010s, and the verdict on all of it is one clause: “There is much experimental evidence supporting this general conclusion, and little understanding of any of it” (printed p. 170).

What makes this one hard is the size of the numbers. To study it properly you would have to hold one chosen vapour steady at a few parts per million inside a cold chamber for the whole of a growth run — and at that concentration, anything you did not intend to be there is doing the same job unseen. The monograph puts it as a working hazard rather than a curiosity: contamination is “a constant concern that unwanted chemical impurities can lead to erroneous experimental conclusions” (printed p. 170).

Even plain air is unresolved, and you can watch the monograph argue with itself about it. Chapter 2 reports that the evidence “suggests that the attachment kinetics on faceted prism surfaces are significantly altered in the presence of air at normal atmospheric pressures”, with “no obvious mechanism that would bring about such a change … so the mystery remains for another day” (printed p. 61). Chapter 7, weighing newer and more careful measurements, comes down the other way: “it appears that the molecular dynamics determining the ice/vapor attachment kinetics is little affected by a background gas of ordinary air” (printed p. 170). One book, two chapters, two verdicts. That is what an open question looks like while it is still open.

6. Freezing releases heat, and that heat is usually ignored. When vapour turns to ice it dumps energy, which warms the crystal, which slows its own growth. This is not controversial physics; it is simply left out of most calculations, deliberately and with a stated reason: “For snow crystal growth in air, thermal effects are generally negligible below -10 C, becoming progressively more important as one approaches the melting point. … For this reason, I will ignore latent heating and thermal diffusion for the remainder of this paper” (arXiv:2306.13087v1, p. 5). That is a defensible simplification for cold air and a known weakness near 0 °C — which happens to be exactly where the surface is slushiest and least understood.

This is the one item on the list that is not difficult. It is deferred. The physics is standard, the reason for setting it aside is printed in the open, and putting it back is a matter of somebody deciding to. It belongs on the list because the account genuinely does run out near 0 °C — but it runs out on purpose, and a reader who cannot tell this item from the other five has not really been told anything.

There is one more gap, and it is structural rather than factual. The Nakaya diagram itself — temperature across, humidity up — may not have enough axes on it: “the traditional Nakaya diagram cannot capture the full richness of snow crystal formation. … Background pressure is another interesting environmental factor, and initial conditions play a role via the SDAK effect” (printed p. 160). Two crystals in identical air can end up different because of what happened to them earlier. A diagram with two axes cannot show that. So the target the chosen numbers were tuned to hit is itself an incomplete summary of what crystals do.

A field of a handful of people

A reasonable reaction to that list is: how can this still be open? Water is the most studied substance on Earth. Snow falls on half the planet every winter. Surely thousands of people have attacked this.

They have not. That is the answer, and it is worth sitting with: “One of the main reasons humanity still does not better understand how snowflakes work is that very few people have studied it over the years. Typically, there are a handful of interested souls around the globe at any given time thinking about this problem, but that is about it” (printed p. 41).

The reason is money, and the reason for that is expectations. Snow crystal science has no application driving it — no device to build, no product at the end. Funding agencies pay for research that promises something, and so, as the phrase this chapter opened with says, none of them will touch it. Private donors do not either; they “generally opt for grander projects”. So the work gets done on personal time and personal money. The monograph behind most of this site was written by someone with a day job, with equipment paid for in part by the sales of his own popular books, and he describes the rest of the enterprise as “mainly just me and my credit card” (all printed p. 41).

Key idea

These questions are not open because they defeated ten thousand physicists. They are open because only a handful of people have ever had time to think about them. Difficulty and neglect look identical from the outside, and they are not the same thing.

There is a second reason, and it is technical rather than social: the experiments are genuinely hard. You are measuring an object smaller than a grain of salt, growing at a fraction of a micrometre per second, in air whose humidity you cannot measure directly at the crystal’s surface. And the crystal melts if you warm it. Chapter 10 walked through what that takes. The people who do it say the same thing plainly: “precision ice-growth experiments are quite difficult to perform and are often affected by subtle systematic effects” (arXiv:2012.12916v1, p. 11).

Both reasons point the same way. Progress here is limited by attention and by careful technique, not by needing a machine the size of a city. A well-designed cold chamber, a microscope, patience, and honest error bars are the tools of this field. That is unusual, and it is the most encouraging fact in this chapter.

Why bother

Before any of that, settle the account this site opened. Chapter 1 struck a bargain: a snow crystal is one substance, in one phase, with two dials on it — how cold it is, and how much spare vapour is around — and if complex form can arise from nothing but those two dials being turned, we ought to be able to follow it all the way down. Most of the way down, we did. The six is answered and stays answered — the lattice is hexagonal and the crystal inherits it (Chapter 3), which took from Kepler’s 1611 treatise to the X-ray photographs of 1929, three hundred and eighteen years for one fact. The vapour supply and the diffusion that starves the air around a growing crystal are ordinary settled physics (Chapter 4), and so is the branching that follows from them: a corner that poked out reached richer air and ran away, and nothing decided where the side branches went (Chapter 6). Nobody is arranging any of it. Turn the two dials and the pattern comes out — which is D’Arcy Thompson’s question answered, at least for this one deliberately simple case.

And the place it runs out is narrower than you would guess from the length of the list. It is the same place every time: the topmost molecular layers of the ice surface, the last step between a water molecule arriving and a water molecule being part of the crystal. That step is what decides which face is harder to build on, which is what sets plate against column, which is what the whole of Chapters 11 to 13 has been circling. The first chapter said as much before you had any way to check it — “several rather basic aspects of the surface structure and dynamics of ice at the molecular level remain quite mysterious” (printed pp. 16–17). Everything under that skin is in hand. The skin is the frontier, and nothing has ever photographed it a molecule at a time.

No cure is waiting at the end of this. The honest reason to care is the one Richard Feynman gave for caring about anything small, quoted in the monograph’s opening chapter: “Nature uses only the longest threads to weave her patterns, so each small piece of her fabric reveals the organization of the entire tapestry” — and, the monograph adds, there is hardly a better example than snowflakes, because “the full panoply of modern scientific knowledge is still not quite enough to explain their origin” (printed p. 23).

And there is still the part you cannot read. Why this crystal and not a column, given the temperature it grew at — that answer runs through a dip whose width was chosen because it made the picture work. What its surface looked like at the molecular scale while it grew — unknown. What shape it would relax into if the cloud froze in place for a week — never measured. The photograph contains settled physics and open questions in the same frame, and no line between them.

Henry David Thoreau watched these land on his coat in 1856 and wrote: “How full of the creative genius is the air in which these are generated! I should hardly admire more if real stars fell and lodged on my coat.” He had no idea what a water molecule was. One hundred and seventy years later we know the lattice, the diffusion, the branching, the two competing faces — and we still cannot say exactly why a crystal looks the way it does. The monograph says so on its first page of text: “snowflake science is very much a work in progress” (printed p. 16).

That is not a disappointing ending. “We do not know” is a scientific position, not a shrug. It is what you say when you have looked hard enough to know precisely what is missing, and it is the only sentence that tells the next person where to start.

Two asides before the last thing. There is a reference list at the back of the site — every figure, printed page and quoted sentence this half leaned on — so that when a line here struck you as too convenient you can go and see for yourself whether it was a measurement or a hand; all of it is free to read. And next time it snows on a cold day — the big stars grow near −15 °C up in the cloud, so cold days are when they reach the ground intact — take a magnifier and a piece of dark card outside and look. The field is small enough that careful looking still counts.

And there is one more thing you can do with an answer nobody has proved, which is what the rest of this site is. Something has to make the shape flip three times; the two crossing curves are a candidate that fits a lot of measurements and they are not a proof. Nobody had ever installed those curves in a full three-dimensional crystal, grown it, and compared the shapes that came out against Nakaya’s diagram. Somebody tried. Part Two, beginning at Chapter 14, is that attempt — and mostly it is not physics at all but the machinery underneath it: the arithmetic, the tests, and a marking scheme written down and sealed before the answer was known. Without all of that you cannot tell a wrong theory from a broken program. It is not finished. The scores it has produced so far are bad ones, and nobody outside the project has checked them yet. Both of those are the reason to read it.
