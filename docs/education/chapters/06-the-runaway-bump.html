Chapter 6

The Runaway Bump

Where do the arms come from — and why can a growing crystal never just stay smooth?

~18 min read 13 figures 5 animations

By the end you will be able to

  • Describe the positive-feedback loop that makes any bump on a growing crystal grow faster still.
  • Explain why a perfectly flat growing surface is a legal answer to the physics but not a stable one.
  • Say why the change from flat-faced to branched happens all at once rather than gradually.
  • Explain why six branches sprout together, and what that tells you about a crystal's past.
  • Describe a dendrite tip as a shape that travels without changing, roughly a micron in radius.
  • Predict, correctly, that the two sides of one arm will not match — and check it on a real photograph.
  • Explain why a crystal with no preferred directions grows into seaweed instead of a snow crystal.
  • Say precisely what is right and what is wrong about calling a snowflake a fractal.

The bump that eats the crystal

Chapter 5 left a growing ice crystal with flat faces held flat by a self-correcting loop. Now break it. Take a growing ice surface, make it as flat as you like, and put one tiny bump on it — a few molecular layers high, thrown up by nothing more than chance.

The bump sticks out. Not far, but far enough, because the air right next to a growing crystal is drier than the air further away: the crystal has been eating the vapour around it, and replacements have to diffuse in from outside. So there is a gradient. Poke out from the surface and you reach richer air, and the top of the bump is poking into it, so slightly more vapour lands there than on the flat surface beside it.

More vapour means faster growth. Faster growth means the bump sticks out further. Sticking out further means reaching into still richer air, which means more vapour again. The loop feeds itself. Libbrecht traces it one step at a time: the top of the bump “grows slightly faster than its surroundings, and so it grows taller. Soon the bump sticks out even farther than it did before, causing it to grow even faster, which makes it stick out still farther, and so on.”

This runaway has a name. It is the Mullins–Sekerka instability, after the American physicists William Mullins and Robert Sekerka, who showed in a 1964 paper that “many of the simplest solutions to the equations describing diffusion-limited growth were mathematically unstable to perturbations.” Libbrecht prefers a plainer name, and so will we: he “also like[s] to call it the Branching Instability because this is a more descriptive moniker, and using it avoids unnecessary jargon.”

The word unstable is doing something specific here. A perfectly flat surface, growing evenly, is a correct answer to the diffusion equation. Nothing about it is forbidden. It is simply an answer nature cannot hold on to — like a pencil balanced on its point. In Libbrecht's words: “the uniform surface growth shown in the top panel in Figure 3.6 is a perfectly valid solution to the diffusion equation; it is just not a stable solution. Any deviation from perfect flatness, no matter how small, will grow larger.”

Key idea

A growing crystal creates a shortage of vapour around itself. Anything that pokes out of the surface escapes part of that shortage, grows faster, and therefore pokes out further. That is the whole of the branching instability, and it is why — as Libbrecht puts it — “the simple process of water vapor freezing into ice creates the beautiful, complex snow crystal patterns we observe falling from the winter clouds.”

Why the switch is sudden

If every bump runs away, why is any snow crystal ever flat-faced? Because faceting is a competing loop pulling the other way, and for a while it wins.

Recall the self-correcting loop from chapter 5. The corners of a hexagon catch more vapour than the middle of a face does — but the middle of the face is crowded with terrace steps, and steps are far better at using the vapour that reaches them. More fuel and less skill at one end, less fuel and more skill at the other: the two effects cancel, the whole face advances at one speed, and that cancellation is what a flat face is.

The bookkeeping behind the cancellation is one short equation.

vn = α · vkin · σsurf

where the three factors on the right are exactly the ones from chapter 5: α the attachment coefficient, which can never exceed 1; vkin the reference speed set by temperature; and σsurf the spare vapour right at this patch of surface. Only the subscript on vn is new, and it just means “measured straight out from the patch itself”.

Libbrecht, Snow Crystals, printed p. 80

Two more subscripts turn up in the published captions later in this chapter, so it is worth settling them now. σfar and σ∞ mean the same thing: the spare vapour in the air far away from the crystal, again measured against what ice can hold, and it is the quantity an experimenter actually sets. σsurf is the smaller value the crystal itself feels, after it has eaten the vapour in its immediate neighbourhood.

Read the equation as a negotiation. The corners have the higher σsurf; the face centre has the higher α; the products match, so vn is the same everywhere and the face stays flat. Now push the crystal harder — raise the humidity, or just let the crystal get bigger, which increases the difference between the corners and the middle. The face centre answers by raising α. And then it hits the ceiling. There is no α above 1: you cannot capture more than every molecule that arrives.

Libbrecht states the consequence flatly: “the faceting mechanism continues working only until α≈1 at the facet centers. Once this happens, α can no longer increase, and this causes the self-regulating process described above to break down… When this happens, branches sprout from the hexagonal corners.”

And the break is not gentle. “This transition from faceted to branched growth tends to be rather abrupt. Once the hexagonal tips sprout branches, they quickly grow outward and leave the regions between the branches far behind.” Abruptness is characteristic of instabilities, and Libbrecht offers an analogy you can build on a kitchen table: stand a cylindrical rod upright on one of its flat ends, in a shallow bowl of honey so the motion is heavily damped. Tap it. “It will wobble a bit and then go back to its upright, balanced state… For a sufficiently tall, slender rod, even a small tap will topple it. Moreover, when it topples, it will topple over completely; the transition from upright to not upright will be abrupt and dramatic.”

So the rule of thumb for faceting is about size and speed, not temperature alone. Faceting dominates when crystals are small, when the attachment kinetics differ strongly from one face direction to another, and when growth is slow. Weaken any of those three and branching takes over. A large crystal in humid air is a long rod in a shallow bowl.

Six corners, one decision

Why six branches, and why do they leave together?

Because the hexagonal cross-section of the crystal has six corners, and they are identical. (The ones that matter are the six vertical edges where two side walls meet — what you see as corners when you look at a thin plate face-on.) Each sticks out into the humid air by the same amount, sees the same supersaturation, has the same attachment coefficient, and is fed by the same crystal. Whatever number decides when a corner runs away, all six carry the same value of it, so all six cross the threshold at the same moment. The six-fold symmetry of a snow crystal is not imposed by anything the branches do; it is inherited from the small faceted hexagon they started on, and the hexagon got it from the arrangement of water molecules in ice.

That gives you a piece of forensic evidence you can use on any photograph. If a crystal has six arms arranged symmetrically, then at some point in its past it was a small, plainly faceted hexagonal prism — because that is the only object whose corners could have launched them in unison. Libbrecht makes the inference explicit: “Whenever you see a branched snow crystal with six-fold symmetry, it is almost certain that the primary branches first sprouted in unison from a faceted ice crystal.”

Key idea

Branching does not create the six-fold symmetry. It copies it. The symmetry comes from the faceted hexagon that existed before the branches did, which is why a six-armed crystal is proof of a hexagonal past.

Watch it happen

Everything above is a chain of words. Here it is as a running process instead. A round seed sits on a hexagonal grid — hexagonal because ice is — and eats the vapour it touches, so the air next to it is depleted; that is the darkening halo. No shape is programmed in and no arms are drawn: the only rule is that a surface cell fills at a rate set by how much vapour reaches it.

Watch what the halo does as the crystal grows. It is not a uniform ring. It is deepest in the notches between the developing arms, which are shielded on both sides, and thinnest at the arm tips, which reach out past it. That difference is the instability, drawn.

Play with the slider before you scroll on. All the way left, and molecules stick wherever they land: the shape goes wild and stringy, the instability with nothing opposing it. All the way right, and the arms fatten into compact blocks. Every real snow crystal lives between those two extremes, and the rest of this site is largely about what decides where.

The tip that never changes

Once a branch has escaped, it becomes a thing worth studying on its own. A branch growing outward into open air, far from the walls of any container and far from competing crystals, is called a free dendrite — “free” meaning unconstrained, and “dendrite” from the Greek for tree. In the laboratory you can grow one deliberately off the end of a wire and film it.

What you see is stranger than it sounds. The tip does not sharpen as it grows, and it does not blunt. It advances at a steady speed with an unchanging shape. Photograph the near-tip region at one moment and again a minute later and the two photographs look the same — different sidebranches in different places, but the same tip, the same curvature, the same everything.

There is a mathematical object that behaves exactly like this. In 1947 the Russian physicist G. P. Ivantsov found a solution of the diffusion equation shaped like a parabolic needle: a smooth point of fixed radius travelling forward at constant speed while its shape never changes.

“Completely static” is the sentence to hold on to. If you could ride along with the tip — a camera moving forward at exactly the tip's speed — nothing in front of you would appear to move at all. Ice would be flowing through your field of view from front to back, but the pattern would sit perfectly still. Try both viewpoints.

Real snow crystal tips are not exactly Ivantsov parabolas, and diffusion on its own does not even pick one answer: it fixes only the pairing of tip speed and tip radius, leaving a whole family of parabolas that all obey the equation. That indeterminacy is the selection problem (printed pp. 102–103), and for snow crystals the extra physics that settles it is the attachment kinetics — the same directional fussiness that turns up two sections from now as the thing that stops a tip splitting in two. What real tips do inherit is the Ivantsov stubbornness, and laboratory measurements near −15 °C pin down six specific traits of a fernlike branch.

The six characteristic traits of fernlike dendritic branches, as listed in Libbrecht, Snow Crystals, printed pp. 84–85. σ∞ is the supersaturation far from the crystal; Rtip is the radius of curvature of the tip.
TraitWhat it means in practice
The branching structure is mainly confined to a flat plane, because of strong basal faceting The arm is a flat cut-out, not a bush. Slow growth on the two flat ends is part of why it stays that thin — but only part; Chapter 12 supplies the rest.
The tip advances at a constant velocity vtip that increases roughly in proportion to σ∞ Twice as much spare vapour in the air, roughly twice the forward speed.
Rtip ≈ 1 micron, roughly independent of supersaturation The tip's sharpness is essentially fixed. Humidity changes the speed, not the shape.
Each distinct sidebranch leaves at 60 degrees to the primary branch The angle is set by the ice lattice, not by the flow of vapour.
New sidebranches sprout at a characteristic distance behind the tip, roughly several times Rtip Sidebranches are born just behind the point, never on it.
The spacing between sidebranches is erratic, with little correlation on the two sides of a branch The gaps are not equal, and the pattern of gaps on one flank of an arm tells you almost nothing about the pattern on the other.

A micron is a thousandth of a millimetre. The whole arm may be several millimetres long, so the point that carves it out is thousands of times smaller than the object it produces — and it stays that size whether the air is barely humid or drenched. (Humidity is what is being held to account there; lower the air pressure and Rtip does increase, printed p. 85.) Hold on to the last row of the table as well. The next section is where it pays out.

How we know

The one-micron tip radius is not something anyone read off a photograph. A micron is at the edge of what an optical microscope can resolve, and Libbrecht is careful about this: the low-resolution tip images are “at least consistent with Rtip being independent of σfar” — consistent with, not a measurement of. The number comes instead from feeding the measured tip speeds in Figure 3.24 into the theory of how a dendrite selects its own tip size, and “a fit to the data… yields Rtip≈1 µm” (printed p. 105). Fishbone dendrites — a laboratory-only form you will meet in chapter 7 — come out at about 1.5 µm by the same route. So treat “about a micron” as a well-supported inference rather than a reading off a ruler.

One more piece of evidence, and it is the kind that should make you trust the whole picture more. Take the water out. In the 1970s the American materials scientist Martin Glicksman and his colleagues studied solidification in an organic compound picked for reasons that have nothing to do with snow: “succinonitrile has a simple crystalline structure, it is transparent, and it freezes near room temperature.” It froze into this.

Still unsettled

Theory and the tip-speed measurements agree nicely, and it would be easy to declare the dendrite problem finished. Libbrecht does not, because the theory leans on attachment kinetics that “are not well known from independent measurements” (printed pp. 105–106): “the theory is somewhat under-constrained due to a poor knowledge of material properties, so we should not read too much into the linear trend seen in Figure 3.24.” Numerical models cannot settle it either, being “not yet sophisticated enough to include the combined effects of diffusion, surface energy, and attachment kinetics” (printed p. 107). A line passing through the data is not the same thing as understanding why.

Sidebranches that do not match

A branch is a surface, and the instability does not stop being true just because the surface now belongs to an arm. Random bumps on the flanks of a growing branch are amplified exactly as the first bump was: “Once a branch sprouts and grows outward, perturbations on its surfaces may again become amplified, thereby sprouting additional sidebranches, as illustrated in Figure 3.7. In principle, this could lead to sidebranches on the sidebranches, and so on, yielding intricate structures.”

Sidebranches then fight each other, by the same mechanism again. If one sidebranch happens to get slightly ahead of its neighbours, it reaches into humid air that they are no longer shielded from, takes vapour that would have gone to them, and stunts them. “Soon the long branch shoots ahead while its immediate neighbors are greatly stunted” (printed p. 90). Winners take resources from losers, which makes them win harder. Libbrecht notes that the same pattern is sometimes called the Matthew Effect in socio-economic circles.

Now for the consequence that most people get wrong. The bumps that seed sidebranches are random. Random, but not scale-free — there is a characteristic length involved, so adjacent sidebranches end up somewhat regularly spaced even though no two gaps are equal (Figure 3.7). What is genuinely uncorrelated is which side gets which gap: the left flank of an arm and the right flank of the same arm are seeded independently. So they do not match — and neither do any two arms of the same crystal.

Careful

“Every snow crystal is perfectly six-fold symmetric” is false, and it is false in a specific, checkable way. (Note that this is about a single crystal. A snowflake is usually a clump of many crystals and has no symmetry to explain in the first place.) The layout of one crystal is six-fold: six arms, evenly spaced and roughly matched in length, because they launched together from six identical corners. The decoration is not, because each sidebranch was seeded by a chance fluctuation. Libbrecht's assessment of the crystal in Figure 3.8 is blunt: the sidebranches “are largely uncorrelated among the primary branches, and even the two sides of a single primary branch exhibit quite different sidebranches. Thus, the detailed six-fold symmetry of the crystal in Figure 3.8 is quite poor.” He adds that symmetrical sidebranches can be stimulated deliberately, with perturbations that are not random.

Turn off the directions

Here is a question that sounds pedantic and turns out to be the key to the whole subject. Why does a dendrite tip stay a single point at all?

Nothing in the feedback loop protects it. A tip is the most exposed part of the crystal, so it is the most unstable part of the crystal. A bump on the tip should run away just like any other bump — splitting the point in two, and then splitting each of those, forever.

What stops it is that ice cares about direction. The rate at which molecules attach to an ice surface depends strongly on which face of the crystal they are landing on. That directional preference — anisotropy, which means nothing more than “not the same in all directions” — gives the tip a favoured direction to point in, and pulls it back when it wanders. Take the anisotropy away and the protection goes with it: “With perfect isotropy, the Mullins-Sekerka instability brings about not only sidebranches, but also tip splitting.”

This can be tested by simulation, dialling the anisotropy down and leaving everything else alone. “With no anisotropy, the dendritic branches exhibited frequent tip splitting that resulted in a complex ‘seaweed-like’ structure. Above some threshold anisotropy, dendrites with stable tip structures appeared.”

You can run that experiment yourself, on this page. Scroll back to the live simulation and drag Face-seeking all the way to the left: the six arms dissolve and you get the right-hand panel above — branched everywhere and shaped nowhere. Drag it back to the right and the arms come home. That slider is the same knob Figure 3.26 is turning.

Snow crystals sit at the left-hand end of that row. “Tip splitting is largely absent in snow-crystal dendrites, owing to the exceptionally large underlying anisotropy in the attachment kinetics.” Ice has an unusually strong opinion about direction, which is precisely why it makes such well-behaved stars.

But you can push ice into the seaweed regime, and this is the satisfying part. The anisotropy that protects the tip comes from the fact that molecules find some faces hard to stick to. Raise the humidity far enough and the prism faces — the six side walls of the hexagon — stop being hard: αprism climbs to about 1, so the difference between the six in-plane directions collapses and the protection is gone. Real snow crystal tips then start splitting.

The basal faces do not follow, and that detail decides which way a tip splits. Making a new step on a basal face costs energy even at 0 °C, so “the basal anisotropy is relatively high under all growth conditions” — there is no humidity that turns it off. Libbrecht therefore expects “a complete absence of basal tip splitting, and this expectation is consistent with observations” (printed p. 107). So a splitting tip divides sideways, staying inside the flat plane of the plate. It never divides off the two flat faces and goes three-dimensional.

Key idea

Diffusion supplies the branching. The lattice supplies the directions. Remove the directions and you still get branching — you just get seaweed instead of a snow crystal. A snow crystal needs both.

Order and chaos

You now have the complete engine, and it has exactly two moving parts.

“Attachment kinetics brings about ordered, faceted surfaces with sharp edges and corners, defined precisely by the crystal lattice structure. Diffusion brings about instability, yielding complex structures and the chaotic sidebranching seen in dendritic growth. These are the competing forces of order and chaos that drive the formation of snow crystals.”

This is a tool, not a slogan. Take any snow crystal photograph and sort what you see into two piles. Razor-straight edges, arms exactly 60 degrees apart, a flat sheet rather than a lump: order, all of it traceable to the way molecules attach to particular crystal faces. Feathery decoration, mismatched sidebranches, one sidebranch huge and its neighbour stunted: chaos, all of it traceable to diffusion. Every crystal you will ever see is a particular ratio of those two, and the interplay “is what gives snow crystal growth its especially rich phenomenology.”

The ratio has a control. Raise the supersaturation and the surface's attachment coefficient climbs toward 1, faceting's grip weakens, and branching takes more of the crystal — “branching becomes more prevalent at higher σ∞, thus leading to more complex snow crystal morphologies at higher supersaturations.” That is why the most elaborate crystals fall from the wettest clouds, and why a dry cloud produces plain, simply faceted crystals — bare hexagonal prisms with nothing on them. The three panels of Figure 3.5, near the top of this chapter, are that dial being turned on a single crystal: straight edges, then hollowed edges, then six points.

The fractal that is not the answer

Branches carrying branches carrying branches: that description belongs to a famous piece of mathematics. Start with a triangle, push a smaller triangle out of the middle of each side, and repeat forever. You get the Koch snowflake, drawn in 1904, one of the first fractals ever described — a shape whose area settles down to a finite value while its edge grows without limit.

Real crystals do nest. Photograph a large enough fernlike dendrite and you can find sidebranches on sidebranches on sidebranches.

Careful

“A snowflake is a fractal” is a description, not an explanation, and it is a description with a floor. The Koch curve repeats at every scale forever; a snow crystal stops after a few levels. It has to: the smallest feature it can carve is set by the tip radius, about a micron (printed p. 105), and the whole arm is only a few millimetres long — which is why “the degree of self-similarity seen in snow crystal structure is relatively minor” (printed p. 91). And the resemblance predicts nothing: not when branches appear, not how fast they grow, not why they leave at 60 degrees. Libbrecht's judgement is that “the concept of self-similarity does not provide a physics-based explanation of the formation of the dendritic structure in the first place – that requires the Mullins-Sekerka instability” (printed p. 91). Saying a snowflake has fractal characteristics is accurate. It is just not an answer.

Faceting and branching between them tell you how ornate a crystal will get. They do not tell you the most obvious thing about snow: why some days it falls as flat stars and other days as tiny pencils. To answer that we need a map.
