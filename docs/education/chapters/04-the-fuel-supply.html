The Fuel Supply

Where does a growing snow crystal get its water, and how does it arrive?

~15 min read 10 figures 4 animations

By the end you will be able to

  • Explain why “saturated” describes a two-way balance of molecules, not a container that has been filled.
  • Read the gap between the ice and supercooled-water curves and turn it into a percentage surplus.
  • Say why a snow crystal growing in a cloud is quietly destroying the droplets around it.
  • Show that the actual surplus of vapour peaks near −12 °C even though the percentage keeps climbing all the way to −40.
  • Describe the dried-out halo around a growing crystal, and predict which parts of the crystal get fed best.

Where the water actually is

The last chapter explained the shape of the scaffolding: ice Ih — the ordinary hexagonal form of ice, the only one that falls out of clouds — a lattice that is six-fold by construction. This chapter asks the blunt follow-up question. A large snow crystal is a piece of solid ice that did not exist an hour ago. Where did the water come from, and how did it get to the crystal?

The answer is not what most people picture. A snowflake is not a frozen raindrop, and it does not build its pattern by colliding with cloud droplets and gluing them on — that process does happen, it is called riming, and you met it in Chapter 1, but it deposits lumps, not arms. The pattern grows out of water vapour — individual water molecules flying around loose in the air, invisible, mixed in among the nitrogen and oxygen. Each one arrives separately and locks into the lattice separately. Every snow crystal you have ever seen was assembled one molecule at a time.

There is not much of this vapour to go around. At −15 °C, the water vapour sitting above a flat ice surface presses on it with about 1.65 millibar — against roughly 1000 millibar for the atmosphere as a whole. (That number turns out to be a balance point rather than a ceiling, which is what the next section is about.) The entire fuel supply for a snowstorm is a trace ingredient, something like one part in six hundred of the air.

What “saturated” really means

Put a block of ice in a sealed box and wait. Two things happen at once, and they never stop. Molecules at the surface occasionally get a lucky thermal kick and fly off into the air. Molecules already in the air occasionally hit the surface and stay. Leaving and arriving are independent processes, running the whole time.

When those two rates are equal, nothing changes on the outside: the block stops shrinking and stops growing. The amount of vapour in the box at that point is the saturated vapour pressure. It is not a lid, and the air is not a sponge that has soaked up all it can carry. It is a standoff. Vapour is still pouring off the ice at a furious rate; exactly as much is landing again.

Key idea

Saturation is a balance, not a capacity. If the air carries more vapour than the balance value, arrivals beat departures and the ice grows. If it carries less, the ice shrinks. The whole of snow-crystal growth is the size of that imbalance.

Now cool the box. Kicking a molecule out of ice costs energy — the latent heat — and the fraction of molecules with enough energy to pay it falls away exponentially as the temperature drops. Libbrecht writes the saturated vapour density in the Arrhenius form, which is the standard way of writing “a process with an energy price tag.”

csat ≈ C(T) · exp(−ℓ / kT)

where
csat
the saturated vapour density — how many water molecules per cubic metre the air holds when leaving and arriving balance over ice
ℓ
the latent heat per molecule: the energy bill for freeing one molecule from the ice
k
Boltzmann’s constant — the conversion factor between temperature and energy per molecule
T
temperature in kelvin
C(T)
a prefactor that drifts only weakly with temperature, so the exponential does essentially all the work

Libbrecht, Snow Crystals, Eq. 2.7, printed p. 58

“Exponentially” is easy to nod along to and hard to feel, so here are the printed numbers. The saturated vapour pressure over ice runs 6.11 millibar at 0 °C, 2.60 at −10, 1.03 at −20, 0.38 at −30, and 0.13 at −40. That is a drop of a factor of 47 across forty degrees. Counted as molecules rather than pressure, the table falls from 1.62 × 1023 per cubic metre at 0 °C to 3.99 × 1021 at −40 °C — about forty times fewer.

This one fact already settles something people notice every winter. Very cold air simply has almost nothing in it to build with. Libbrecht mentions in passing that Antarctic snow crystals “tend to have simpler shapes, because the climate is so cold and dry” (printed p. 43). The physics has not changed down there. The shelves are bare.

Two surfaces, two answers

Saturation is defined against a surface, and below 0 °C there are two different surfaces in play. That is the whole reason snow crystals can exist.

Cloud droplets do not freeze at 0 °C. They go on being liquid, at temperatures far below freezing — supercooled, from Chapter 1: liquid, below its own freezing point, and staying that way, because freezing needs something to start on. A cloud at −15 °C is typically full of supercooled droplets.

Liquid water holds its molecules a little less tightly than ice does. Close to freezing it costs about 2.5 × 106 joules to evaporate a kilogram of water but about 2.8 × 106 to sublimate a kilogram of ice straight to vapour (printed p. 56). A cheaper escape means more escapees, so liquid water’s saturated vapour pressure is the higher of the two. At every temperature below zero the water curve sits above the ice curve, and measured as a fraction of what ice can hold, the gap widens steadily all the way down.

The gap is usually quoted as a fraction rather than a pressure difference, and that fraction is called the supersaturation. Read it as “how much spare vapour the air is carrying, as a percentage of what ice can hold.” The symbol is σ, and it will follow you through the rest of this site.

The arithmetic behind that fourth column is worth doing once by hand, so σ never feels like a black box. Take the −15 °C row: (1.91 − 1.65) / 1.65 = 0.157. Surplus divided by the balance value. That is the whole calculation.

Careful

A weather report saying “100 percent humidity” sounds like a full tank. It is not a full tank, and it is not even one number. Relative humidity is normally quoted against liquid water. The same air, measured against ice, reads 115.7 percent at −15 °C. Nothing about the air changed; only the surface you compared it to. Whenever you meet the word “saturated” below freezing, the first question is always: saturated with respect to what?

A snow crystal eats its own cloud

Put the two facts together and watch what happens inside a real cloud.

A winter cloud is a mist of supercooled droplets, typically 10 to 20 microns across (a micron is a thousandth of a millimetre) and small enough to stay suspended almost indefinitely (printed p. 18). Those droplets hold the air between them very close to saturation with respect to water: they evaporate whenever the air runs dry and condense whenever it runs rich. But at −15 °C that same air is 15.7 percent oversupplied as far as ice is concerned. Drop one ice crystal into it, and the crystal is standing in air that is permanently too rich for it.

So the crystal grows. Growing pulls vapour out of the air. The air around it dips below water saturation, so the nearest droplets start evaporating to make the difference up — and that vapour, in turn, ends up on the ice. The net traffic runs one way: liquid to vapour to solid. The droplets are being taken apart, molecule by molecule, and reassembled as a snow crystal.

A hundred thousand droplets per crystal is the number that turns an abstraction into a supply chain. Every arm you admire under a magnifier was, an hour earlier, a small cloud of liquid drops that no longer exists.

Careful

“It’s too cold to snow” is usually filed under folk nonsense. It is roughly right, and now you can say why. Snowfall gets properly going once a cloud drops well below −5 °C and droplets start freezing in numbers. By around −20 °C most of the liquid droplets are gone — some frozen, many evaporated away to feed the crystals. Libbrecht’s wording is exact: “when no liquid water remains to feed growing snowflakes, there can be no snowfall” (printed p. 20). What runs out is not cold. It is the reservoir.

Percentages are not grams

You have already met the reservoir running out one way: by around −20 °C most of the supercooled droplets are gone, and a cloud with no droplets has nothing to give. There is a second way it runs out. That one bites earlier and harder, and it is hiding in the table you just read. Spotting it is the most useful thing in this chapter.

The percentage surplus climbs relentlessly with cold: 5 percent at −5, 15.7 at −15, 21.5 at −20, 47.4 at −40. Read that column alone and you would predict that the coldest clouds grow the most spectacular crystals. They do not. The reason is that σ is a ratio, and the quantity it is a ratio of is collapsing at the same time — the same collapse the five sealed boxes drew earlier in the chapter. Forty-seven percent of almost nothing is almost nothing. The two failures compound, and this one comes first: the surplus measured in grams is already falling away well before the droplets themselves run out near −20.

What the crystal actually consumes is grams of water, not percentages. So the honest quantity to plot is the surplus itself: how many grams of spare vapour a cubic metre of cloud air is carrying over what ice can hold. That curve does something the percentage curve does not — it turns over.

How we know

Neither curve here is a fresh measurement, and neither is a guess. The saturated vapour pressures of ice and of supercooled water are among the most carefully measured quantities in physical chemistry, and the standard formulas fitted to those measurements agree with the monograph’s printed table to about one percent or better across 0 to −40 °C — which is as close as a two-decimal printed table can be checked. Computing the surplus from them puts the crest at −12.3 °C, consistent with where the hump peaks in the published figure above.

Hold onto that hump. It is a clue, not an explanation. If the best-fed clouds sit somewhere around −12 to −15 °C, you would expect the largest and fastest-growing crystals to come from that band — and they do. Libbrecht is blunt about it: large, well-formed stellar crystals “can only be found when the cloud temperatures are around −15 C” (printed p. 383). But the fuel supply cannot explain the shape. It has no opinion about whether the ice grows as a flat plate or a stubby column, and that turns out to be the hard problem.

The last millimetre

We have a surplus. Now the delivery problem: how does a molecule sitting a millimetre away find the crystal?

Nothing steers it. There is no attraction, no suction, no wind required. A water molecule in air is knocked off course constantly by collisions with nitrogen and oxygen, staggering away in a fresh random direction after each one. What it does is diffuse: random jostling that, purely by statistics, moves molecules from crowded regions toward empty ones. As Libbrecht puts it, diffusion “results from the normal thermal jostling of air and water molecules, which tends to mix the two species together” (printed p. 76).

Diffusion needs a downhill to run along, and the downhill is written in supersaturation. Here is σ stated properly, as a field — one number attached to every point in the air, not a single value for the whole cloud.

σ(x⃗) = [ c(x⃗) − csat ] / csat

where
σ(x⃗)
the spare vapour at one particular place, as a fraction — 0.157 means “15.7 percent more than ice can hold here”
c(x⃗)
how many water molecules per cubic metre are actually at that place
csat
the balance value over a flat ice surface at that temperature — the csat from the equation above
x⃗
the position you are asking about

Libbrecht, Snow Crystals, Eq. 3.3, printed p. 92

That is the same ratio you worked out by hand on the table — surplus divided by the balance value — written with molecule counts instead of pressures. At a fixed temperature the two are proportional to each other, so it does not matter which you divide; (1.91 − 1.65) / 1.65 and the same subtraction done in molecules per cubic metre both give 0.157. The only new thing here is the arrow on the x: σ now has a different value at every point in the air.

Diffusion is slow. The diffusion constant for water molecules in air is about 10−5 m2/s under typical atmospheric conditions (printed p. 91) — which means molecules cross short distances quickly and long distances very slowly indeed, because random-walk time grows with the square of the distance. This is often why a crystal grows slower than it theoretically could: the ice would happily absorb molecules faster than the air can deliver them. Whether the air or the ice surface is the real bottleneck depends on the conditions — the surface half of that question is the next chapter. Libbrecht names the situation directly: “Because diffusion is a slow process, it tends to limit the development of the crystal, so we say its growth is diffusion-limited” (printed p. 76).

One honest complication, since we are keeping score. There are really two diffusion problems running at once: water molecules diffusing in, and heat diffusing out, because every molecule that freezes onto the crystal releases its latent heat and warms the ice slightly. Libbrecht checks the sizes and concludes that “the effects of heating and heat diffusion are relatively small compared to particle diffusion” (printed p. 77), so he sets heat aside by default. So will we — but it is a simplification, not an absence.

The hungry halo

Put a crystal into supersaturated air and follow the consequences one step at a time. The crystal absorbs the vapour immediately next to it. That leaves the air immediately next to it drier than the air further out. Now there is a downhill, so more vapour drifts inward — but slowly, because diffusion is slow. The crystal therefore lives permanently inside a depleted halo: a shell of dried-out air, a few times wider than the crystal, that it has eaten out of its own surroundings.

The halo is not an accident, and it is not a transient. It is the steady state. Every molecule that ever reaches the crystal has to random-walk in through it.

That is a calculation, and a fair reader should be suspicious of a calculation making its own case. Water vapour is invisible; you cannot photograph a humidity field. Except, with one trick, you can.

How we know

The droplet-fog photograph is a genuine measurement dressed up as a snapshot. Droplets can only condense where the local supersaturation exceeds σwater — the same number from the table above. So the edge of the droplet-free moat is not decoration: it is the σ = σwater contour, made visible by choosing a detector that switches on at exactly that threshold. Everything inside the moat is drier than a cloud; everything outside is not. The control experiment is two figures below: turn the supersaturation of the whole chamber down below σwater and the entire fog vanishes, while the crystal carries on growing. The droplets really are reading σ, and nothing else.

The rest is geometry. The halo is centred on the crystal and deepest at its surface. Anything that sticks out pokes into air that has not been picked over yet, so the supersaturation climbs away steeply from a protruding tip and only gently from a sheltered face. Steep climb means fast delivery: the diffusive flow is proportional to how sharply σ changes with distance, not to how big σ is. So a tip receives the steepest inward flow of vapour, a face centre a slacker one, and a point buried between two arms the weakest of all.

Libbrecht states the consequence exactly: “the gradient is highest near the tips of a growing crystal, resulting in a high flow of water vapor at these points, and thus fast growth at the tips. In the interior parts of the crystal, the supersaturation gradients are lower, providing lower water-vapor flow and slower growth” (printed p. 77). Run a time-lapse and you see it plainly — “the outer regions grow outward quickly while the interior structures evolve more slowly.”

You may have noticed the animation doing something this chapter has not earned: the crystal does not just grow unevenly, it sprouts arms. Uneven feeding is only the first half of that story, and the second half — why a small advantage runs away instead of evening out — is a chapter of its own, further on. For now, take away only the half we can prove: the parts of a crystal that stick out receive the steepest inward flow of vapour, and the parts that are enclosed receive the weakest.

The tempting extra step — that a protruding tip must therefore be standing in the richest air — does not, and the thin plate on the right is the counter-example. Nothing about diffusion is different there. What is different is how choosy the ice surface is about where a molecule lands, and that is the next chapter.

Key idea

The small hexagon at the centre of a sprawling stellar crystal is very nearly the whole crystal as it was early on. Once the outer edge starts intercepting the supply, the middle stops being fed and stops changing. The centre is a fossil, preserved by starvation.

One more number, because it protects you from a wrong mental picture. The time the halo needs to establish itself is roughly R2/D, where R is the crystal’s size and D the diffusion constant. Libbrecht puts in typical values — a millimetre-sized crystal and D about 2 × 10−5 m2/s — and gets about 50 milliseconds (printed p. 92). That is the same constant quoted a page earlier as about 10−5, written out here a little more precisely; nothing in this argument turns on the factor of two. Meanwhile the crystal needs minutes to change size appreciably. The halo is thousands of times quicker than the ice. It never lags, never trails behind, never sloshes — it re-forms around every new bump the instant that bump appears. That is why it looks painted onto the outline in every picture in this chapter.

Still unsettled

Almost nothing in this chapter is controversial. Vapour pressures are measured to a fraction of a percent, and the physics of diffusion has been solid for more than a century — Libbrecht calls it “extremely well understood,” with only the arithmetic of complicated shapes remaining hard. The trouble starts one step later. Of the molecules that diffusion delivers to the ice, which ones actually stick? On that question he is scathing about his own field: “our grasp of the underlying molecular dynamics is depressingly poor” (printed p. 78). Everything genuinely unresolved about snowflakes lives on the far side of that sentence.

Vapour is now arriving everywhere on the crystal at once, and we can say how much reaches each part of it. Whether any of it stays is a completely separate question — and the answer is settled in the top one or two molecular layers of the ice surface, which is where the next chapter goes.
