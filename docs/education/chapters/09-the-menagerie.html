Naming what you caught

Something has just landed on your dark sleeve. What do you call it?

The last chapter taught you to read a crystal as a record — branch by branch, a legible account of the air it fell through. This chapter asks the blunter question that comes next: how much of what actually falls is legible at all? Less than the photographs suggest. So this is a chapter about the honest population, and about what to call the parts of it that no pretty picture prepared you for.

We name snow crystals for the ordinary reason we name anything — so that two people can talk about the same object. Some shapes turn up often enough, and look distinctive enough, that their names have settled down. Here is the working vocabulary, with the sizes attached, because size is what makes these names usable in the field rather than on a page. It is laid out to be glanced at, which is the only way anyone uses a field guide in the cold.

Simple prism

Small, plain and faceted, anywhere from plate-like to columnar, with no branches. Common in most snowfalls at any temperature. Branching does not begin until a crystal passes roughly half a millimetre, so this is simply a young snow crystal.

≈0.3 mm — “about as large as the period at the end of this sentence” · p. 391

Stellar plate

Thin, flat and six-fold, with broad branches, little sidebranching and “a profusion of complex surface markings” on its faces. The sparkle in falling snow is often stellar plates catching the light.

≈2 mm across a good specimen · p. 395

Sectored plate

The same kind of object, decorated with pronounced radiating ridges that appear to divide the plate into slices. At times the markings look like veins on a leaf, giving the crystal an almost plant-like look.

as a stellar plate · p. 403

Stellar dendrite

The branched, tree-like version: narrow branches carrying numerous sidebranches. Forms near −15 °C when the humidity is high, and arrives in great numbers when it arrives at all.

≈3 mm tip to tip · p. 407

Fernlike stellar dendrite

A stellar dendrite that has gone further, with sidebranches on its sidebranches, set at 60 degrees to the branch carrying them. The largest snowflakes there are, and about a hundred times thinner than they are wide.

on rare occasions over 10 mm · p. 413

Hollow column

A hexagonal prism with a conical void running in from each end, “with the tips nearly touching at the waist”. Turns up near −5 °C, and is easy to overlook with the naked eye.

close to 1 mm long · p. 419

Needle

A long, slender column grown in warm, wet snowfalls near −5 °C. The one entry on this list you can identify without a lens: fresh needles on a dark sleeve look like “short bits of white hair”.

often up to 3 mm — the longest columnar crystal · p. 421

Capped column

A column with a plate grown on each end — “a stubby axle flanked by two hexagonal wheels”. Not common, but distinctive enough that a trained eye picks a few out of a warmer snowfall.

just large enough to spot with the naked eye · p. 423

Irregulars

Small, somewhat malformed plate-like crystals, usually in clumps — granular snow, crystals that “look a lot like icy grains of sand”. Far more common than everything above them put together.

small, and usually stuck to each other · p. 388

Rimed

Not a shape a crystal grew into but a stubble of frozen cloud droplets stuck to one that had already grown. A typical droplet is “roughly 0.03 millimeters, which is half the diameter of a human hair”.

arrives after the shape is set · p. 401

Graupel

Also called soft hail. What a crystal becomes when that studding takes over completely and buries whatever shape was underneath. Some snowfalls deliver mostly this.

arrives after the shape is set · p. 389

The last two entries are the odd ones out, and deliberately so. Rimed and graupel are not shapes a crystal grew into; they are things that happened to a crystal after its shape had been decided, so they are held over to section three, where the physics is.

Every size, field description and quoted phrase in the guide above comes from the field guide in Libbrecht, Snow Crystals, printed pp. 388–441. The individual pages are given on each entry.

Those 35 boxes are not 35 different kinds of thing. Some of them are single ice crystals whose shape was set by the temperature and humidity they grew in. Some are several crystals stuck together or fused at birth. And two of them describe something that happened to a crystal after its shape had already been decided. Sorting the chart that way is more useful than memorising it, because it keeps the right thing in view: a name is a tool for talking, not a fact about the crystal. The crystal only has a growth history.

Why the list never finishes

If names are useful, why not just write down all of them? People have tried, repeatedly, and the attempt has a shape.

Ukichiro Nakaya — who called snow crystals “hieroglyphs from the sky” — built the first system in 1954: seven primary categories branching into 41 types. Magono and Lee expanded it to 80 categories in 1966. Kikuchi and Kajikawa expanded it again, to 121 distinct types, in work published in 2013. Each expansion was a response to the same complaint: real crystals kept turning up that did not fit.

Adding categories does not fix the problem, because the problem is not that there are too few boxes. Some things really do come in boxes. Biological species mostly cannot interbreed, so they form genuinely separate groups. Chemical compounds have formulas. Snow crystals have neither. The monograph puts it flatly:

Key idea

“There is no absolute classification system for snow crystals, and there never will be, because there is no clear way to divide snow crystals into distinct, non-overlapping categories.” — Libbrecht, Snow Crystals, printed p. 384

The reason is the whole thesis of the previous chapter. “The shape of a snow crystal depends on its entire growth history, which is somewhat analogous to saying that the breed of a dog depends on its entire ancestry.” A crystal that started out near −5 °C, drifted up through −12 °C, and finished in dry air at −18 °C is not a member of a category. It is a record of that trip. Give a crystal an unusual enough trip and it will fit nowhere, no matter how many categories you have defined.

You can see the seams inside the classification chart itself. Sectored plates “can be considered a sub-class of stellar plates, and there is no sharp dividing line between the two categories” — if a stellar plate has especially prominent ridging, we tend to call it a sectored plate (printed p. 403). Sheaths and cups “could be included in the hollow-column and capped-column categories”; they have their own names mainly because they look striking (printed p. 439). And hollow plates are admitted to be “more of a structural feature than a snow-crystal type” (printed p. 431). These are not failures of the chart. They are what an honest chart of a continuum looks like.

Careful

You will sometimes read that there are seven kinds of snowflake, or 35, or 121. All three numbers are real, and none of them is a fact about snow. They are the sizes of three different filing systems — Nakaya's seven primary categories, a 35-box working chart for field observing, and the 121-type research system. “Asking ‘what kind of snowflake is that?’ is not an especially good question, because there may not be a well-defined answer.” The scientifically honest reply to a genuinely ambiguous crystal is that it sits between two types.

What actually falls

Here is the other thing the charts do not tell you: the crystals drawn in them are not what is usually coming down. Photographers, including the ones who made every beautiful image on this site, stand in the cold for hours hunting for the striking, symmetric specimens. Unattractive snowflakes do not get published. The published record is a heavily biased sample of the sky.

The unbiased sample is free. Go outside during a light snowfall and look.

Three separate things can happen to a crystal that have nothing to do with the temperature and humidity that shaped it, and all three are extremely common.

Riming. Clouds are full of supercooled liquid droplets — water still liquid below 0 °C. A falling crystal runs into them, and each one freezes onto the ice on contact. A typical cloud droplet is “roughly 0.03 millimeters, which is half the diameter of a human hair” (printed p. 401), so rime shows up as a stubble of tiny beads. Large crystals are especially prone to it, because the high humidity they need in order to grow large is supplied by a dense population of droplets in the first place. Keep going and the crystal disappears under its own rime: that is graupel.

Travel wear. A crystal that passes through air that is drier than it needs starts losing molecules instead of gaining them. Sublimation attacks sharp features first, so the corners round off and the finest tips shorten. This is worst when the temperature is warm, which is exactly when most people are outside watching snow.

Clumping. Crystals collide with each other and stick. Recall the distinction from Chapter 1: a snow crystal is one lattice, while a snowflake can be a whole clump, and those big puffballs in warm snowfalls are made from hundreds or thousands of individual crystals. What matters here is that clumping belongs on this list at all. Like riming and like sublimation, it is a post-growth event. It happens to a finished crystal, in a part of the sky that had no say in the shape, and the only thing it records is how crowded and how sticky the fall was.

How we know

The standard way to see an unbiased sample is to let falling snow land on a few glass microscope slides for a couple of minutes, then scan the slides under a low-power microscope. It is how sheaths and cups were found in the first place — not by spotting them in the air, but by scanning over collections of small irregular crystals that had already landed on slides (printed p. 439). It is also the recommended way to hunt for arrowhead twins, which are uncommon but have an easily recognisable shape, so they can be found if you go searching for them (printed p. 68).

None of this appears on a morphology diagram, and it should not. Riming and sublimation and clumping are not records of the temperature a crystal grew at. They are records of something else — where the liquid droplets were, how dry the air got, how crowded the fall was. When you look at a real snowflake you are reading two histories layered on top of each other, and telling them apart is most of the skill.

The crystals that are not six-fold

Everything so far has been a six-armed crystal wearing damage. A lopsided stellar crystal still has six branches; what is uneven is their lengths, and that is a record of riming or drying or a bumpy ride, not of anything unusual in the crystal itself. Now for something different: crystals whose symmetry was never six-fold to begin with. Not a lopsided star, but three long edges, or a pointed blade, or a right-angled corner.

Six-fold symmetry comes from the hexagonal ice lattice, so a snow crystal that is not six-fold is telling you something about its lattice. The best-understood reason is twinning: “When two single crystals grow together with a specific orientation between their respective lattices, this is called crystal twinning” (printed p. 66). It is ordinary in minerals, and ice is no exception.

The easiest case to explain is the columnar twin, and you have already met one. Chapter 3 put two hexagonal columns end to end at a flat interface called the twin plane, where the two halves “fit perfectly together… by simply replacing the usual hexagonal bonds with a plane of cubic bonds” (printed p. 66) — the lattice does not break, it switches for one layer and switches back. The next two figures are the same two figures Chapter 3 used, and they are here to do a different job. There they were evidence for something you cannot see: that ice really does have a second way to stack, and that the choice occasionally goes wrong in the violent first instant of freezing. Here they are being read as a shape — what a twin looks like from the outside, and how you would recognise one on a glass slide.

That single mismatched layer is slightly weaker than the rest of the structure, which gives the crystal a tell. When the crystal sublimates, the twin plane gives up molecules faster than the ice on either side of it, and a groove opens around the column's waist.

Rarer twins are more dramatic. In an arrowhead twin, two crystals meet at a reflection plane and grow into a flat, pointed blade with an apex angle fixed by the lattice rather than by the hexagon — 78, 57 and 89 degrees for the three known variants, none of which a hexagon can make. And this is where the lattice earns its keep, because that angle is not a measurement. It is a prediction. If you know how the two lattices are joined, the angle follows from the two numbers that define the ice unit cell and from nothing else.

apex angle = 2 · tan−1(c0 / 2a0) = 78.3°

where
a0
the width, across the hexagon, of the ice unit cell — the smallest block of the lattice that repeats over and over to build the whole crystal. Measured near 0 °C: 0.452 nm (a nanometre is a millionth of a millimetre)
c0
the height of that same unit cell, along the axis perpendicular to the flat basal face: 0.736 nm
tan−1
the inverse tangent — it turns a ratio of two lengths back into the angle that produced it

Several distinct joins are possible, and each one predicts a different angle from the same two lattice constants — though not the same kind of angle. For the three arrowhead twins the prediction is the apex angle of the blade, the sharp point you can lay a protractor across. For the two crossed-plate twins there is no apex at all: two plate-like crystals pass through each other, and what the lattice predicts is the angle between them where they cross. How we know

The prediction and the measurement are independent. The prediction comes from measurements of the ice unit cell made near 0 °C, plus a proposal about how two lattices are joined. The measurement comes from someone putting a protractor on a photograph of a crystal that fell near the South Pole. 88.7 predicted, 88.5 ± 0.5 measured. That is what it looks like when a claim about invisible molecular stacking is tested against something you can see.

There is a second reward hidden in these odd crystals, and it is cashed out in three terms from Chapter 3: ice Ih, the ordinary hexagonal stacking; ice Ic, the cubic alternative; and stacking disordered, a crystal built from a mix of the two, layer by layer.

Now the argument. One family of crossed-plate twins may have started from a tiny seed of cubic ice, which was then buried by the ordinary hexagonal ice growing over it. The monograph says this model “nicely explains why such a high-order twinning should occur so readily” — that is, why so complicated a join happens so easily — “and it supports the hypothesis that stacking disordered crystals, containing a mix of Ih and Ic bonding, can play a significant role in snow-crystal nucleation” (printed p. 73). A rare shape in a photograph becomes evidence about the very first instant of a snowflake's life, which nobody has ever watched directly.

Twinning is not the only route to a crystal that is not six-fold. Triangular crystals — usually small, truncated triangular plates — are not twins at all. Their lattice is perfectly ordinary; it is the growth that went odd. There is a weak growth instability that can turn a hexagonal plate into a triangular one: once three of the six prism facets are running slightly ahead of the other three, diffusion-limited growth — the runaway of Chapter 6 — amplifies the difference, and once the change starts it is irreversible (printed p. 436).

That growth instability got its own dedicated paper decades after the monograph, and it sharpens the puzzle rather than solving it. The three prism facets that pull ahead and the three that fall behind start out identical: “the molecular structures of all three of these top prism terraces are identical” (Libbrecht, “Triangular Snowflakes: Growing Structures with Three-fold Symmetry using a Hexagonal Ice Crystal Lattice,” arXiv:2106.09809v1, printed p. 9). Explaining how two patches of the very same surface end up growing at noticeably different rates is, in the paper’s own words, “the crux of the trigonal-growth problem” — and the paper is candid that a molecular-level answer “will likely remain an unsolved problem for many decades” (printed p. 10). The animation below does not attempt that answer. It only shows the amplification step the monograph already described: give three alternating facets a small head start, let diffusion-limited growth do the rest, and watch the hexagon go triangular.

What the dedicated experiment measured — and what it inferred

A perfectly sharp 60-degree triangular tip supplies one unusually clean number. Its tiny tip facet must advance perpendicular to itself at exactly twice the speed of either neighbouring large facet just to preserve that geometry. The three top terraces are close enough to experience nearly the same local supersaturation, so the paper infers that the tip’s attachment coefficient alphaHK must exceed twice the neighbouring value. It proposes a three-dimensional extension of structure-dependent attachment kinetics: at the tip, the top terrace has shrunk from a narrow strip into a small island. That extension is a hypothesis, not a measured transition law (arXiv:2106.09809v1, pp. 9–10).

A less extreme trigonal plate in the same study gave a separate rate measurement: its short facets advanced about 60 % faster than its long facets. The author suggests slightly different top-terrace widths could produce that contrast, but says the photographs cannot establish those widths. These experiments used c-axis needle tips at −14 °C in air at one atmosphere; below 6 % far-field supersaturation, plates stopped appearing. They measure a narrow slice of conditions, not a universal triangular-crystal rule (pp. 6–7, 12).

And a twelve-branched snowflake is not a twelve-fold crystal. It is two ordinary six-branched crystals that collided while they were small and stuck together with close to a 30-degree rotation between them. The near-perfect examples are a selection effect: a badly aligned collision produces an inconspicuous mess that nobody photographs, while a well-aligned one produces something your eye picks out instantly (printed p. 441).

Still unsettled

Working out what a twin looks like is the easy half. Working out when one forms is not: “Estimating the probability that a particular twinned state will form under different circumstances is an extremely challenging task” (printed p. 66). And “no one has ever published examples of any arrowhead twins that were grown in the laboratory” (printed p. 68). For triangular crystals, exactly how and when the instability is triggered is “not yet well understood” (printed p. 436). A later, dedicated study of exactly this crystal (Libbrecht, arXiv:2106.09809v1) does not close that gap so much as sharpen it: the three facets involved are molecularly identical, so a first-principles account of why they still race apart “will likely remain an unsolved problem for many decades” (printed p. 10).

A third kind of flat face

Everything so far has been built out of two kinds of flat face: the basal facets that cap the ends of a hexagonal prism, and the six prism facets around its sides. That is the standard toolkit, and almost every crystal in the field guide is assembled from it.

Almost. Chapter 3 showed you the third: the pyramidal facet, which slices across the corner between a prism face and a basal face and turns the end of a column into something like a stubby pencil point. Chapter 3 also settled the one number that pins it down. The lattice leaves that face no freedom at all about its angle — tan−1(√3 a0/2c0) = 28.0 degrees, out of the two lattice constants and nothing else — and it explained why the photographs below are annotated 31.5: that is the same face seen along a line of sight which is not parallel to it, and an angle read off a picture is a measurement of the picture (printed p. 52).

So the angle is settled, and settled without a crystal. What Chapter 3 could not tell you is why you almost never see one. The evidence suggests pyramidal facets “form only rarely, at quite low temperatures (below −20 °C) and perhaps only in air” (printed p. 51). The lattice plainly permits the face. Something else is removing it.

The answer on offer is Chapter 5's rule again, applied to a third face: the slowest faces are the ones you see. A face that grows quickly runs outward until it collides with its neighbours and disappears from the outline, so what you end up looking at is whatever was left behind. Fast faces are not forbidden. They eliminate themselves. The animation below applies that rule to a corner face cut at 28.0 degrees and asks how fast it has to grow before it is gone.

Now the rest of the argument. Whether a face grows slowly comes down to how hard it is to get a new molecular layer started on it, and that in turn depends on the energy cost of the step at the edge of a new layer. Faces with shallow, indistinct steps have almost no barrier, grow fast, and never appear as facets — which is exactly the case for one family of ice surfaces that never facets at all. The basal and prism faces have bigger steps, a real barrier, and strong faceting.

The proposal is that the pyramidal face sits in between: its step energy is small at ordinary snowfall temperatures, so those layers start easily, the face races ahead, and it grows itself out of existence before anyone can photograph it. Only in extreme cold does the barrier become large enough for the face to survive. That would explain the whole pattern — the rarity, and the −20 °C threshold — from one number.

Still unsettled

That number has never been measured. The monograph flags its own argument: “the pyramidal step energy likely becomes substantial at low temperatures, as pyramidal facets apparently only form in quite cold conditions. These are both speculative statements, however, as only the basal and prism step energies have been measured to date” (printed p. 54). The explanation is currently a reasonable story that fits the observations, and it will stay that way until somebody grows pyramidal facets under controlled conditions and measures them.

Key idea

Faceting is a competition, not a list of allowed faces. Which faces you see on a snow crystal is decided by which ones happen to be growing slowest at that temperature — and the rare pyramidal facet is the clearest evidence that the list can change when the conditions do.

If you carry one thing out of this chapter, carry that — and notice that what makes it worth carrying is that it is a comparison rather than an assertion.

One rule, many shapes

Step back from the individual entries and the menagerie asks an obvious question: is there anything under all these names besides a list? Chapter 6 already pointed at an answer that does not come from a photograph at all. Janko Gravner and David Griffeath built a cellular automaton — a grid of cells updated by one fixed rule, nothing photographed, nothing fitted to a particular crystal — out of just the ingredients that chapter called order and chaos: vapor diffusing across a hexagonal lattice, and an anisotropic attachment rule that freezes a cell once it has accumulated enough mass from its already-frozen neighbors (Gravner & Griffeath, Physical Review E 79, 011601 (2009)). Turn the same few numbers and the same rule produces a hexagonal plate in one setting and a branching dendrite in another — not different rules for different crystals, one rule that happens to have several distinct regimes.

The interactive below is a small, original, self-contained reimplementation of that published rule — not this project’s own production solver, which is a much larger, differently parameterized instrument built for this project’s own validation work. It keeps the paper’s two mass fields and its published attachment logic, but it drops the paper’s vertical axis and runs on a single flat hexagonal layer, so it can show you a faceted hexagon, a plump stellar plate, thin unbranched needle-like blades, and a branching dendrite — but not a true hollow column, which needs the missing third dimension to hollow out at all. The four numbers you can drag are the model’s own tunable knobs — a background vapor density and three attachment thresholds, one for each of 1, 2, and 3-or-more already-frozen neighbors — not measured supersaturations or temperatures.

The omitted dimension matters for more than the silhouette. Gravner and Griffeath’s full T×Z simulations produced plates with ridges or ribs, true axial needles, and columns whose hollow cores began developing early. They also demonstrated abrupt environmental changes that produced capped columns and plates with dendritic extensions (their §7 and Figure 4; §§11–12 and Figures 29–30). This one-layer teaching model cannot test any of those three-dimensional results; it demonstrates only how neighbour-count thresholds can reorganize a flat growth front. It also omits the paper’s vertical diffusion, drift, melting, and proposed random slowdown, so resemblance here is a qualitative regime illustration, not a reproduction of a published three-dimensional case.

Every number in this chapter was taken by somebody: 0.452 nanometres, 88.5 ± 0.5 degrees. And one of them has not been taken by anybody — the pyramidal step energy — which is why the last section had to end in a guess. Both of those are facts about measurement. So is the answer to the puzzle Chapter 7 left hanging: it is a curve fitted to measurements. Before we cash that answer in, you should see how measurements like these are actually made.
