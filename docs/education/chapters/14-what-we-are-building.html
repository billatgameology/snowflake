Chapter 14

What We Are Building

Somebody built a machine to grow a snow crystal from measured physics, and then set out to prove it wrong. What is the machine?

~14 min read 2 original interactives, 2 diagrams Part VI · What we tried to build

By the end you will be able to

  • Say in one sentence what the instrument is for, and why what you feed it is a designed weather history rather than a shape you drew.
  • Explain why showing a model's hidden state is a claim about knowledge, not an item on a feature list.
  • Name things this project wrote down that it refuses to be — and say why writing refusals down before any code exists is a design technique, not modesty.
  • Tell a success criterion a machine can settle from one that people can argue about forever.

Chapter 13 left you holding a working hypothesis. Two curves, one for the flat top and bottom faces of an ice crystal and one for the six sides, crossing as the air gets colder, the crossings deciding whether the crystal grows tall or wide. Real measurements underneath it. Ill-defined curves on top. Nobody had put that explanation into a full three-dimensional crystal and checked.

Somebody did. This section of the site is about the machine they built, why almost all of the work went into making the machine distrustable, and what happened when it was finally pointed at Nakaya's diagram.

The ending, first

You should know how it comes out before you read another word, because the engineering chapters read completely differently depending on whether you already know the ending. Not knowing, you would read them as boasting. Knowing, you read them as forensics.

The completed solver was run 204 times across a grid of temperatures and humidities, and its results were scored against the Nakaya diagram by a rule written down before the run. It agreed with the diagram at 5 of the 90 points that counted. Ninety, not 204, because that same rule set two groups aside before the run. Thirty-six points sit in a band of ±1 °C either side of each of the three boundaries, where the diagram itself is ambiguous about which habit belongs there. Seventy-eight sit in the very cold regime, where the diagram accepts either pure habit — so producing one of them cannot be scored wrong, and the regime is “reported but kept out of the headline because it accepts both pure classes”. Those 78 are worth having anyway: 26 agreed, and 52 did not, every one of the 52 a run that came out neither plate nor column (research/phase6-sweep-report.md, lines 60–63 and 72). Ninety plus 78 plus 36 is 204: nothing was quietly dropped, and you can do the subtraction yourself.

Now the 90. In the band where the diagram says columns, it produced columns 0 times out of 24. In the cold band where the diagram says plates, 0 out of 60. And across all 204 runs, the number that failed a numerical check was zero (research/phase6-sweep-report.md, lines 65–72 and 91–93).

Even the 5 are not really agreement. All five sit at a single temperature, −2 °C, which the scoring rule had already registered before the sweep ran as carrying essentially no weight — precisely so that it could not later be waved about as a result. Outside that one column, in the report's own words, “the model agrees with the reference nowhere else inside the headline scope” (research/phase6-sweep-report.md, lines 77–80).

Read those numbers in that order, or you will read them wrong. “5 out of 90” on its own sounds like broken code. “5 out of 90 with zero invalid runs” is a different kind of statement: the machinery did everything it was asked to do, and the physics it was given still did not produce the diagram.

Be exact about which physics that was. The machine is Libbrecht's measured stickiness curves installed in a three-dimensional lattice — a later section quotes the sentence it was built from — and these 204 runs carried the broad-facet curves alone. The extra mechanism chapter 13 ended on, the SDAK dips on narrow facets, was deliberately left switched off. That is the design, not an oversight. The dips are the part of the theory whose positions were chosen rather than measured, so the charter reports runs with them and runs without them separately: “wherever P3 inputs are active, matching Nakaya is in-sample reproduction”, P3 being its label for “Nakaya-informed SDAK hypotheses (dip locations chosen to impose agreement with the diagram)” (project charter.md, line 183). Switching them on and then matching the diagram would prove nothing. What ran instead is what the charter calls “the no-SDAK probe … a first-class result in its own right” (line 313). Chapter 28 works through why the other half could not have been a fair test.

Inside that scope, one thing did match. The same sweep's −5 °C points land inside the range of aspect ratios — height divided by width — that Libbrecht measured on his own bench, with columns absent exactly as he reported. The project's findings ledger, a running file of results it thinks are worth sharing outside the repository, states the catch in the same breath: the model “agrees with the lab data its inputs came from” (docs/findings-ledger.md, entry A1). Agreeing with the data you were built from is a check that the wiring is right, not independent validation. The disagreement with the diagram is the half nobody could have arranged.

Key idea

The interesting result is not that a model failed. It is that a model failed while the safeguards did their job. Every numerical check held inside all 204 runs — and where something did slip past, a mismatch between the written protocol and what the runs actually did, a different safeguard caught it afterwards and made it expensive. That combination is only worth anything if the safeguards were built first, in public, and hard enough to be trusted — which is what the next fifteen chapters are about.

Because here is the twist. Those numbers are already invalidated. The way the model's parameters were supposed to be worked out between measured temperatures was written into the frozen protocol before the sweep; the way the 204 runs actually worked them out was different, and nobody noticed until afterwards. The project's own rule is that any change to a frozen protocol voids the sweep that ran under it, so all 204 points are being run again: “the 204-point sweep of 6995868 is invalidated and re-runs” (docs/decisions/0031-phase6-paramset-freeze-row.md).

That is not a technicality being observed politely. The decision record considers the cheap alternative — keep the result, write the mismatch down as a limitation — and rejects it by name: “The mismatch is not a limitation of the evidence, it is a contradiction between the evidence and the protocol that certifies it.” And it doubts the published numbers explicitly, in writing: the class matrix and the per-regime counts “are all per-point quantities, and none of them is currently correct” (same file, “Alternatives considered”). What the re-run is expected to produce is written down in advance — a headline of about 2 of 90, with the structure of the failure unchanged (docs/findings-ledger.md, entry B1). Chapter 29 is entirely about what that cost. Everything between here and chapter 28 is the work that has to exist before any of these numbers are allowed to mean anything at all.

The instrument

The thing being built has a working title — The Virtual Cloud Chamber — and its own document describes it in one sentence:

“A desktop-browser application in which a user designs the environmental history of a snow crystal — the sequence of temperatures, humidities, and conditions it passes through as it ‘falls’ — and watches a three-dimensional crystal grow in response. The crystal that emerges is a readable record of its journey: arms sprouted during a cold, humid phase; a column capped by plates when conditions shifted; a hollow core formed where vapor could not reach.”
project charter.md, §1.1, line 31

Notice what you are not given: a shape tool. You cannot drag a branch where you want one. The only thing you can edit is the weather, and the crystal is whatever that weather writes. That follows from chapter 8: a snow crystal is a record of the conditions it passed through, so the honest input to a snow-crystal instrument is a journey, not a drawing. Give the reader a shape tool and you have built an ice-sculpture app with hexagons in it.

Three steps, and the third is the point

The application is described as a loop with three steps (project charter.md, §1.1, lines 36–38). One: you edit an environmental timeline. Two: a field solver — a program that tracks a quantity spread through space, here the water vapour in the air — grows the crystal through those conditions. Three:

“Diagnostic views expose the normally invisible state driving the growth — the vapor field around the crystal, the local growth propensity on its surface — so the user can see not just what grew, but why.”
project charter.md, §1.1, line 38

Step three is the whole difference. The document is blunt about it: “Plenty of generative-art snowflake toys exist. This project is an explanatory instrument: it makes the model's hidden state legible, so a ‘snowflake designer’ can reason about cause and effect rather than pull slot-machine levers” (line 41).

A slot machine and an instrument can produce the same picture. What separates them is whether you can see the mechanism in between. A program that asks for trust and shows you nothing has taught you nothing, however pretty its output. That is why so much of this project's budget goes to looking at things rather than to resolution — how fine the grid of cells is. One of its standing rules reads “Model validity outranks compute. Spend surplus GPU budget on diffusion iterations, observability, determinism, and sweeps before resolution” (project charter.md, line 335).

There is a happy accident buried in this. The views that let a user see why a branch appeared are the same views the developers need to find out why the solver is wrong: “These views are debugging instruments first and product features second — that they are the same artifact is the project's luckiest property” (line 281).

How we know

Nothing in that demo was told that corners grow faster. The corners win because the vapour field says so, and the field is a consequence of one equation plus the shape the crystal happens to have. Watching the reason is the point; the shape alone would let you believe any story you liked.

The gap between two papers

The project's actual technical move fits in one breath, and it is a move between two existing bodies of work that each have exactly what the other lacks.

On one side is a computing result: a three-dimensional simulation on a honeycomb of cells where each tick runs four simple rules — diffuse, freeze, attach, melt — and plates, dendrites and hollow columns all emerge from them (docs/video-explainer.md, lines 197–201). It is beautiful and it works. It also has one disqualifying limitation for our purposes: “there is no temperature anywhere in it. Its attachment thresholds are abstract knobs. Some knob settings make plates and some make columns, but no knob is labeled in degrees. You can't ask it ‘what grows at −15 °C?’ — the question isn't just unanswered, it's un-askable” (lines 203–206).

On the other side is the measurement programme you met in chapters 10 to 12: real stickiness curves with real temperatures attached — but published simulations that mostly run in simplified geometry rather than free-form three dimensions. “The gap between them is where this project lives” (line 215). The build strategy is one sentence: “take Gravner–Griffeath's honeycomb machinery, rip out its temperature-blind attachment thresholds, and install Libbrecht's measured stickiness curves in their place” (lines 242–244).

Putting degrees on the dial sounds cosmetic. It is the opposite. Once temperature enters the equations rather than the label on a knob, the model has an opinion its builders did not choose — and an opinion you did not choose can be wrong. The explainer states the consequence plainly: “A model that cannot fail can't teach you anything; this one can fail, and that is deliberately the point” (docs/video-explainer.md, lines 250–251).

Careful

Installing measured physics in a model is not the same as showing that the model reproduces reality. Those are two different claims about two different things — what went in, and what came out. The project treats them as different sentences and never lets one stand in for the other.

Four things it refuses to be

Before any code existed, the charter wrote down a section titled “What this is explicitly not” (§1.3, lines 51–57). Four denials. The first is about scale:

“Not a molecular simulation. A real snow crystal contains ~10^18 water molecules. This is a mesoscopic model: each lattice cell stands in for trillions of molecules. That is legitimate because the physics being modeled (vapor diffusion, boundary attachment rates) is already continuum-scale — not because of any hand-wavy ‘fractal scaling’ argument.”
project charter.md, §1.3, line 54

Mesoscopic means “in between”: bigger than a molecule, smaller than the crystal. It is worth doing the division yourself, because the ratio is startling.

Nmolecules per cell ≈ 1018 ÷ Ncells ≈ 1018 ÷ 106 = 1012

where
1018
water molecules in one real snow crystal — a billion billion (project charter.md, line 54)
Ncells
lattice cells the solver actually tracks. The development budget is about 1 million cells; the interactive preview budget about 8 million, and an offline high-fidelity run about 130 million (project charter.md, line 299)
1012
roughly a trillion real molecules standing behind each cell the program moves

Arithmetic from project charter.md lines 54 and 299

A trillion-to-one approximation would be indefensible if the physics being modelled lived at the molecular scale. It does not. Vapour diffusion and surface attachment rates are already written as continuum quantities — densities and rates over patches of surface — so coarsening to cells is the scale those equations were stated at in the first place. Note the second half of the charter's sentence, which refuses a bad justification as firmly as it accepts the good one.

The other three denials: not a validated physical simulator — “The solver is a phenomenological model inspired by real physics. The product's identity includes being honest about this” (line 55); not mobile — desktop browser, WebGPU required (line 56); and not (initially) a social product — “Galleries, sharing, and export come after the science works” (line 57).

Phenomenological is the load-bearing word there. It means the model copies the behaviour without claiming to have derived it: it reproduces what the measurements do, and does not claim to know why at the level of molecules. Such a model can be extremely useful and still be wrong about the mechanism, and it has no right to be believed about a case nobody measured.

Read the validation denial again. It is a product whose identity includes a disclaimer — a strange thing to write on day one, and the most useful sentence in the document. When somebody eventually wants to say “this shows what snowflakes do at −15 °C”, there is already a line in the founding document saying they may not. That is what stops a project drifting into a claim it cannot support, months later, when everybody has forgotten the original scope and the demo looks amazing.

Eight criteria, and the one no machine can settle

Version 1 of this project is called a success on eight counts. Seven of them are things one solver, on one hexagonal-prism lattice, has to be able to do (project charter.md §1.4, lines 62–71). Quoted exactly:

Seven of the eight v1 success criteria, verbatim from project charter.md §1.4, lines 65–71. Note what is not among them.
#“One solver, on a hexagonal-prism lattice, that…”
1grows a sixfold-symmetric hexagonal plate;
2grows a solid column from the same code by changing only model parameters;
3transitions continuously between plate-like and column-like habits;
4shows facet-center vapor depletion in its diagnostic views;
5produces hollow columns as emergent behavior, with no hard-coded “make hollow” rule;
6supports conditions changing mid-growth (the timeline, in embryo);
7exposes its vapor field and surface growth propensity visually the whole time.

“Looks like a real snowflake” is not on the list. It could not be: two people can look at the same crystal and disagree about that forever, and neither can be shown to be wrong. Every one of those seven ends in a number somebody can recompute — how far from sixfold, how tall against how wide, how much lower the vapour is at a facet centre than at its rim. The project states the rule for itself in one line: “Every scientific milestone is an automated metric, not a screenshot” (project charter.md, line 342). Its working rules put the same thing less politely: “‘Looks right’ is not a result. If you eyeballed it, write that you eyeballed it” (AGENTS.md, line 335).

Then there is the eighth, which the list is careful to keep separate from the other seven: “Plus one independent finding from a cheap 2D prototype: that ‘designing a cloud journey’ is understandable and enjoyable as an interaction” (project charter.md, line 74). Nothing in that ends in a number. Whether an interaction is enjoyable is a judgement a person makes, and no metric settles it. That is not a hole in the argument; it is the argument's edge, drawn on purpose. The rule is not “never accept a human judgement” — it is that the science is never allowed to rest on one. A criterion about how a person feels using a tool has to be judged by people; a criterion about whether a crystal grew hollow does not, so it is not allowed to be. Notice also where the eighth is meant to be settled: on a cheap two-dimensional prototype, off to the side, deliberately not on the solver whose output is the evidence.

Criterion 5 is the one to stare at. Hollow columns are a real thing that real crystals do — the middle of a wide face starves while its rim keeps advancing, and a pit opens. The criterion does not ask for hollow columns. It asks for hollow columns with no hard-coded “make hollow” rule. The difference between those two requests is the difference between a model that has learned something and a model that has been told the answer, and it is very easy to lose.

Key idea

A shortcut that guarantees the result destroys the evidence rather than producing it. You just watched the hollowing test read PASS with the rule switched on — and passing it no longer tells you anything, because the metric is now measuring the rule instead of the physics. A criterion that cannot fail is not a criterion.

Where it actually stands

It would be easy, after several pages of design documents, to leave you with the impression that the application described in §1.1 exists. It does not, and being clear about that is the same discipline as everything else on this page.

What exists is the engine and its evidence: a solver that runs on a graphics card in a browser, a headless runner that executes hundreds of runs without a human watching, the metrics, the gates — automatic pass/fail checks that block a phase from starting until the previous one has met them — and the 204-run sweep. The validation work — the project calls it Phase 6 — is in flight, not finished: the sweep ran, and the re-run described above is pending (docs/findings-ledger.md, entry B1). The product layer — Phase 7, the four view profiles (four ways of presenting the same engine, changing what the screen shows and nothing about the physics), the polished timeline editor a person would actually enjoy using — has not started. Its scope was written down in July 2026; it still begins after Phase 6 (docs/PROGRESS.md, “Next step”). The charter's own long-term sketch — two engines, one interactive and one slow and high-fidelity — is labelled “post-v1, non-binding” in its heading (§1.6, lines 89–94), which is the same honesty device applied to the future.

Still unsettled

One limitation applies to every review this project has ever had, including the two-reviewer process that checked this page. This repository is worked on by “multiple different LLMs across sessions” (AGENTS.md, line 3), and its own publication checklist records the consequence in writing: “At least one human domain scientist reads any manuscript before submission. All reviews to date, internal and external-facing, are LLM reviews; for publication that is a disclosed limitation, not a substitute” (docs/stretch-sharing-and-investigation.md, §4, lines 217–219). No human domain scientist has read this work. That is a real gap, it is stated rather than buried, and you should hold everything in this section a little more loosely because of it.

You now know what the machine is for and what it refuses to claim. What you do not yet have is the vocabulary that makes those refusals precise — because “we gave the model real physics” and “the model reproduces reality” sound like the same sentence, and treating them as one is the fastest way to fool yourself. The project's answer is not a confidence score. Every quantity it puts on the screen carries two labels, held apart on purpose: one for what the number is, and one for what it has earned (project charter.md, §1.5, line 78). The next chapter is about why one label was never enough.
