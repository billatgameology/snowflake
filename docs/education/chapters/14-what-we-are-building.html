Chapter 14

What We Are Building

Somebody built a machine to grow a snow crystal from mixed-provenance, experiment-constrained physics, and then set out to prove it wrong. What is the machine?

~14 min read 3 original interactives, 2 diagrams Part VI · What we tried to build

By the end you will be able to

  • Say in one sentence what the instrument is for, and why what you feed it is a designed weather history rather than a shape you drew.
  • Explain why showing a model's hidden state is a claim about knowledge, not an item on a feature list.
  • Name things this project wrote down that it refuses to be — and say why writing refusals down before any code exists is a design technique, not modesty.
  • Tell a success criterion a machine can settle from one that people can argue about forever.

Chapter 13 left you holding a working hypothesis. Two curves, one for the flat top and bottom faces of an ice crystal and one for the six sides, crossing as the air gets colder, the crossings deciding whether the crystal grows tall or wide. Real measurements underneath it. Ill-defined curves on top. Nobody had put that explanation into a full three-dimensional crystal and checked.

Somebody did. This section of the site is about the machine they built, why almost all of the work went into making the machine distrustable, and what happened when it was finally pointed at Nakaya's diagram.

How the citations work

Every quotation in Part Two comes with something like project charter.md, line 185, or research/phase6-sweep-report.md, “Scored result”. This project keeps its records as plain text files — a founding document, decision records, a report for each experiment — all of them stored together in what programmers call a repository, a folder whose entire history is kept, so you can see not only what a file says now but every version it has ever said. A reference like that names the file and then the place inside it, the way a page number names a place in a book. Line numbers are exact but they move when a file is edited; section headings do not, so headings are used wherever a file has them. You can ignore all of it and lose nothing: the citations are there so that somebody who doubts a sentence can go and check it, which is a different job from reading.

The ending, first

You should know how it comes out before you read another word, because the engineering chapters read completely differently depending on whether you already know the ending. Not knowing, you would read them as boasting. Knowing, you read them as forensics.

The historical broad-facet CAK arm was run 204 times across a grid of temperatures and humidities, and its results were scored against the Nakaya diagram by a rule written down before the run. It agreed with the diagram at 3 of the 90 points that counted (research/phase6-sweep-report.md, “Scored result”). Ninety, not 204, because the scoring rule had already set two groups aside — before anybody knew what would land on them. Here is the whole grid with those groups marked, so that you can do the subtraction yourself instead of taking it from me.

So: 30 points sit in a band of ±1 °C either side of each of the three regime boundaries, where the diagram itself is ambiguous about which habit belongs there, and the rule excludes them from counting. Another 84 sit in the very cold regime, where the diagram accepts either pure habit — so producing one of them cannot be scored wrong, and the regime is “reported but kept out of the headline because it accepts both pure classes”. Those 84 are worth having anyway: 27 came out in agreement with the diagram and 57 came out neither plate nor column. Ninety plus 84 plus 30 is 204. Nothing was quietly dropped.

Now the 90, regime by regime. In the warmest band, where the diagram says plates, the model produced plates 3 times out of 6 — those three are the whole of the agreement, and chapter 28 explains why even they do not amount to agreeing with the diagram. In the band where the diagram says columns, it produced columns 0 times out of 24. In the cold band where the diagram says plates again, 0 out of 60. Over all 204 runs it committed to a plate 6 times and to a column 30 times and declined to commit either way 168 times. And the number of runs that failed a numerical check was zero.

Read those numbers in that order, or you will read them wrong. “3 out of 90” on its own sounds like broken code. “3 out of 90 with zero invalid runs” is a different kind of statement: the recorded run completed its per-row checks, and at this executed setup its parameterization still did not produce the diagram. The open R15 numerical-adequacy work means this is not a resolution-independent conclusion.

Be exact about which physics that was. The machine uses source-tabulated, fitted and model-inverted attachment inputs installed in a three-dimensional lattice, and these 204 runs carried the broad-facet CAK parameterization alone. The extra mechanism chapter 13 ended on, the SDAK dips on narrow facets, was deliberately left switched off. That is the design, not an oversight: the dips are the part of the theory whose exact shapes, widths, depths and placements are P3 Nakaya-informed choices, so the project reports runs with them and runs without them separately, and what ran here is the half it calls “the no-SDAK probe … a first-class result in its own right” (project charter.md, §3.2 line 315). Why that separation has to exist, and why the other half could not have been a fair test on its own, is chapter 28's argument. For now it is only a label on which physics was in the box. The source-inferred narrow-facet reduction regions are observational constraints, but they do not make the exact M1 prescription measured.

The separate historical M1 arm has now completed the same 204-row grid. It matched 54 of 90 on the common scope (54 of 78 on its arm-specific scope), with 75 plate, 119 neutral, 10 column and zero invalid rows. That larger score does not isolate an SDAK-dip effect: CAK to M1 changes the broad σ0 functions and A prefactors along with the dip factors. Both arms are measured-only historical records, not the replacement gate.

Historical report snapshot

When the CAK report was written, it said: “These numbers are not yet gate evidence. WP5 has not run: no independent verifier has re-derived report.json from points.json, and the six negative controls have not executed.” That quotation is a preserved dated snapshot, not the repository’s current state. Its caution was correct; its inventory is now obsolete.

Updated

Both historical arms have completed artifacts and independent re-derivations. Phase 6 later added a matched no-dip arm at 5/90 and a numerical ladder whose 64 comparisons split 36 pass and 28 attached-count failures. Decision 0045 accepted that negative record; gate6 verified 13/13 closure criteria. The preview-resolution GPU cohort and held-out validation moved to the independently eligible, not-started Phase 7 scope. The historical one-directional extent-fragility rule was also refuted by its own aspect-ratio ladder; a later symmetric audit found more threshold-vulnerable rows. Chapters 27 through 29 carry the evidence and its current limits.

There is one more thing to know about that 3, and it is the reason chapter 29 exists: it is the second number this sweep produced. The first time all 204 points ran, they scored 5 of 90. Then somebody compared what the runs had actually done against the protocol that was supposed to govern them, and found a mismatch — the way the model's parameters were worked out between measured temperatures was not the way the frozen protocol said. The project's own rule is that any change to a frozen protocol voids the sweep that ran under it, so the 5 of 90 was not softened or annotated. It was withdrawn: “the 204-point sweep of 6995868 is invalidated and re-runs” (docs/decisions/0031-phase6-paramset-freeze-row.md). All 204 points ran again, about twenty hours of machine time, and returned the 3.

That is not a technicality being observed politely. The decision record considers the cheap alternative — keep the result, write the mismatch down as a limitation — and rejects it by name: “The mismatch is not a limitation of the evidence, it is a contradiction between the evidence and the protocol that certifies it.” And it doubted its own published numbers explicitly, in writing, before the correction ran: the class matrix and the per-regime counts “are all per-point quantities, and none of them is currently correct” (same file, “Alternatives considered”). Chapter 28 reads the corrected result and chapter 29 itemises what the correction cost. Everything between here and chapter 28 is the work that has to exist before any of these numbers are allowed to mean anything at all.

Key idea

The interesting result is not that a model failed. It is that a model failed while the safeguards did their job. Every numerical check held inside all 204 runs, in both sweeps — and where something did slip past, a mismatch between the written protocol and what the runs actually did, a different safeguard caught it afterwards and made it expensive. That combination is only worth anything if the safeguards were built first, in public, and hard enough to be trusted — which is what the next fifteen chapters are about.

The instrument

The thing being built has a working title — The Virtual Cloud Chamber — and its own document describes it in one sentence:

“A desktop-browser application in which a user designs the environmental history of a snow crystal — the sequence of temperatures, humidities, and conditions it passes through as it ‘falls’ — and watches a three-dimensional crystal grow in response. The crystal that emerges is a readable record of its journey: arms sprouted during a cold, humid phase; a column capped by plates when conditions shifted; a hollow core formed where vapor could not reach.”
project charter.md, §1.1, line 33

That paragraph is the product goal, not a description of the screen that exists today. Phase 3 built a Three.js development instrument: an orbit camera, field overlays, a vapour slice and honest cell readouts for inspecting the solver. The charter calls those views “debugging instruments first and product features second” (project charter.md, §3.2, Phase 3). The polished application — Phase 7’s four view profiles, timeline editor and product shell — is a later layer over that engine. Sharing an engine does not make the development viewer the finished product.

Notice what you are not given: a shape tool. You cannot drag a branch where you want one. The only thing you can edit is the weather, and the crystal is whatever that weather writes. That follows from chapter 8: a snow crystal is a record of the conditions it passed through, so the honest input to a snow-crystal instrument is a journey, not a drawing. Give the reader a shape tool and you have built an ice-sculpture app with hexagons in it.

Three steps, and the third is the point

The application is described as a loop with three steps (project charter.md, §1.1, lines 38–40). One: you edit an environmental timeline. Two: a field solver — a program that tracks a quantity spread through space, here the water vapour in the air — grows the crystal through those conditions. Three:

“Diagnostic views expose the normally invisible state driving the growth — the vapor field around the crystal, the local growth propensity on its surface — so the user can see not just what grew, but why.”
project charter.md, §1.1, line 40

Step three is the whole difference. The document is blunt about it: “Plenty of generative-art snowflake toys exist. This project is an explanatory instrument: it makes the model's hidden state legible, so a ‘snowflake designer’ can reason about cause and effect rather than pull slot-machine levers” (line 41).

A slot machine and an instrument can produce the same picture. What separates them is whether you can see the mechanism in between. A program that asks for trust and shows you nothing has taught you nothing, however pretty its output. That is why so much of this project's budget goes to looking at things rather than to resolution — how fine the grid of cells is. One of its standing rules reads “Model validity outranks compute. Spend surplus GPU budget on diffusion iterations, observability, determinism, and sweeps before resolution” (project charter.md, line 337).

There is a happy accident buried in this. The views that let a user see why a branch appeared are the same views the developers need to find out why the solver is wrong: “These views are debugging instruments first and product features second — that they are the same artifact is the project's luckiest property” (line 281).

The next demo is the smallest honest version of a hidden state made visible, and it needs one piece of physics first. Water vapour travels through air by diffusion: it drifts from where there is more of it to where there is less. It does that far faster than ice grows, so at every instant we can treat the vapour as having already finished settling. That assumption has a name — quasi-static — and it collapses the whole moving air problem into one standing equation.

∇²σ ≈ 0

where
σ
the supersaturation: how much more water vapour the air is carrying than it can hold. This is the quantity the crystal eats and the distant air keeps supplying
∇²
read it as “how far this point sits below the average of its surroundings”. Setting that to zero says every point equals the average of its neighbours — which is exactly what you get by replacing each cell with the average of its neighbours, over and over, until it stops changing. That is all the demo does
≈
approximately, not exactly. A computer stops when the remaining mismatch is small enough, and what counts as small enough is a number this project had to write down and defend. Chapter 22 is about what happens when it is set too loosely

The “quasi-static diffusion (Laplace) equation, ∇²σ ≈ 0, with the crystal surface as a sink” — project charter.md §2.4, line 132

Two edges close the problem off. The ice is a sink: it swallows vapour on contact, so σ is held at zero along its surface. The rim of the box is the far field, a stand-in for undisturbed air a long way off, held at a fixed value. Everything interesting happens in between, and ice is added wherever the vapour at a surface cell rises past a fixed threshold — the attach step of the four-rule cycle named in the next section (docs/video-explainer.md, lines 199–201).

Two things that demo does deliberately badly are worth naming, because both come back as real problems later. The first is that it is under-converged. The smoothing is halted after a couple of dozen passes where honest convergence would need thousands, because the number of passes needed “scale[s] like (L/Δx)²” — halve the cell size and you quadruple the work, so a browser-sized budget buys a picture that is qualitatively right and quantitatively short (project charter.md §2.4, line 141). Chapter 22 is entirely about what stopping early costs when it matters.

The second is what happens when the crystal grows big enough to feel the walls of its box. A crystal near the wall is partly measuring the wall, so the real project flags it: “any run in which the crystal's bounding box exceeds ~65% of any domain extent is automatically flagged invalid” (line 231). Notice the shape of that rule — it is a verdict, not a brake. The run is allowed to finish and then its results are refused, which is what makes “zero invalid runs” a sentence worth saying rather than a tautology. The miniature above brakes instead, purely so that you can watch the moment it fires.

How we know

Nothing in that demo was told that corners grow faster. The corners win because the vapour field says so, and the field is a consequence of one equation plus the shape the crystal happens to have. Watching the reason is the point; the shape alone would let you believe any story you liked.

The gap between two papers

The project's actual technical move fits in one breath, and it is a move between two existing bodies of work that each have exactly what the other lacks.

On one side is a computing result: a three-dimensional simulation on a honeycomb of cells where each tick runs four simple rules — diffuse, freeze, attach, melt — and plates, dendrites and hollow columns all emerge from them (docs/video-explainer.md, lines 197–201). It is beautiful and it works. It also has one disqualifying limitation for our purposes: “there is no temperature anywhere in it. Its attachment thresholds are abstract knobs. Some knob settings make plates and some make columns, but no knob is labeled in degrees. You can't ask it ‘what grows at −15 °C?’ — the question isn't just unanswered, it's un-askable” (lines 203–206).

On the other side is the empirical programme you met in chapters 10 to 12: attachment parameterizations with real temperatures attached — but published simulations that mostly run in simplified geometry rather than free-form three dimensions. “The gap between them is where this project lives” (line 215). An earlier build summary called every input a measured stickiness curve; that provenance was too broad. More exactly, the project keeps Gravner–Griffeath’s lattice machinery, replaces its temperature-blind attachment thresholds, and installs Libbrecht’s source-tabulated, fitted and model-inverted attachment parameterizations.

Putting degrees on the dial sounds cosmetic. It is the opposite. Once temperature enters the equations rather than the label on a knob, the model has an opinion its builders did not choose — and an opinion you did not choose can be wrong. The explainer states the consequence plainly: “A model that cannot fail can't teach you anything; this one can fail, and that is deliberately the point” (docs/video-explainer.md, lines 250–251).

Careful

Installing experiment-constrained inputs — some direct empirical values, some fits, inversions and prescriptions — is not the same as showing that the model reproduces reality. Those are two different claims about two different things — what went in, and what came out. The project treats them as different sentences and never lets one stand in for the other.

Four things it refuses to be

Before any code existed, the charter wrote down a section titled “What this is explicitly not” (§1.3, lines 51–57). Four denials. The first is about scale:

“Not a molecular simulation. A real snow crystal contains ~10^18 water molecules. This is a mesoscopic model: each lattice cell stands in for trillions of molecules. That is legitimate because the physics being modeled (vapor diffusion, boundary attachment rates) is already continuum-scale — not because of any hand-wavy ‘fractal scaling’ argument.”
project charter.md, §1.3, line 56

Mesoscopic means “in between”: bigger than a molecule, smaller than the crystal. It is worth doing the division yourself, because the ratio is startling.

Nmolecules per cell ≈ 1018 ÷ Ncells ≈ 1018 ÷ 106 = 1012

where
1018
water molecules in one real snow crystal — a billion billion (project charter.md, line 56)
Ncells
lattice cells in a solver run. The charter’s design budgets are about 1 million for development, 8 million for preview and 130 million for a bake-quality target. They are planning targets, not a claim that every adapter has demonstrated all three capacities; the charter makes the detailed and bake targets adapter-dependent (project charter.md, §3.2, Phase 5)
1012
roughly a trillion real molecules standing behind each cell the program moves

Arithmetic from project charter.md lines 56 and 301

A trillion-to-one approximation would be indefensible if the physics being modelled lived at the molecular scale. It does not. Vapour diffusion and surface attachment rates are already written as continuum quantities — densities and rates over patches of surface — so coarsening to cells is the scale those equations were stated at in the first place. Note the second half of the charter's sentence, which refuses a bad justification as firmly as it accepts the good one.

The other three denials: not a validated physical simulator — “The solver is a phenomenological model inspired by real physics. The product's identity includes being honest about this” (line 55); not mobile — desktop browser, WebGPU required (line 56); and not (initially) a social product — “Galleries, sharing, and export come after the science works” (line 57).

Phenomenological is the load-bearing word there. It means the model copies the behaviour without claiming to have derived it: it is designed to reproduce selected measured behaviour, but whether it succeeds must be tested, and it does not claim to know why at the level of molecules. Such a model can be extremely useful and still be wrong about the mechanism, and it has no right to be believed about a case nobody measured.

Read the validation denial again. It is a product whose identity includes a disclaimer — a strange thing to write on day one, and the most useful sentence in the document. When somebody eventually wants to say “this shows what snowflakes do at −15 °C”, there is already a line in the founding document saying they may not. That is what stops a project drifting into a claim it cannot support, months later, when everybody has forgotten the original scope and the demo looks amazing.

Eight criteria, and the one no machine can settle

Version 1 of this project is called a success on eight counts. Seven of them are things one solver, on one hexagonal-prism lattice, has to be able to do (project charter.md §1.4, lines 64–73). Quoted exactly:

Seven of the eight v1 success criteria, verbatim from project charter.md §1.4, lines 67–73. Note what is not among them.
#“One solver, on a hexagonal-prism lattice, that…”
1grows a sixfold-symmetric hexagonal plate;
2grows a solid column from the same code by changing only model parameters;
3transitions continuously between plate-like and column-like habits;
4shows facet-center vapor depletion in its diagnostic views;
5produces hollow columns as emergent behavior, with no hard-coded “make hollow” rule;
6supports conditions changing mid-growth (the timeline, in embryo);
7exposes its vapor field and surface growth propensity visually the whole time.

“Looks like a real snowflake” is not on the list. It could not be: two people can look at the same crystal and disagree about that forever, and neither can be shown to be wrong. Every one of those seven ends in a number somebody can recompute — how far from sixfold, how tall against how wide, how much lower the vapour is at a facet centre than at its rim. The project states the rule for itself in one line: “Every scientific milestone is an automated metric, not a screenshot” (project charter.md, line 344). Its working rules put the same thing less politely: “‘Looks right’ is not a result. If you eyeballed it, write that you eyeballed it” (AGENTS.md, line 335).

Then there is the eighth, which the list is careful to keep separate from the other seven: “Plus one independent finding from a cheap 2D prototype: that ‘designing a cloud journey’ is understandable and enjoyable as an interaction” (project charter.md, line 76). Nothing in that ends in a number. Whether an interaction is enjoyable is a judgement a person makes, and no metric settles it. That is not a hole in the argument; it is the argument's edge, drawn on purpose. The rule is not “never accept a human judgement” — it is that the science is never allowed to rest on one. A criterion about how a person feels using a tool has to be judged by people; a criterion about whether a crystal grew hollow does not, so it is not allowed to be. Notice also where the eighth is meant to be settled: on a cheap two-dimensional prototype, off to the side, deliberately not on the solver whose output is the evidence.

Criterion 5 is the one to stare at. Hollow columns are a real thing that real crystals do — the middle of a wide face starves while its rim keeps advancing, and a pit opens. The criterion does not ask for hollow columns. It asks for hollow columns with no hard-coded “make hollow” rule. The difference between those two requests is the difference between a model that has learned something and a model that has been told the answer, and it is very easy to lose.

Key idea

A shortcut that guarantees the result destroys the evidence rather than producing it. You just watched the hollowing test read PASS with the rule switched on — and passing it no longer tells you anything, because the metric is now measuring the rule instead of the physics. A criterion that cannot fail is not a criterion.

Where it actually stands

It would be easy, after several pages of design documents, to leave you with the impression that the application described in §1.1 is the same artifact as the Phase 3 viewer. It is not. The development instrument exists to inspect a running solver; the Phase 7 product is the later, human-facing composition of that engine. Being clear about the boundary is the same discipline as everything else on this page.

The sweep account at the start of this chapter is a dated evidence snapshot: it describes the corrected no-SDAK arm and the repository state recorded with that report, not a permanently live status panel. This page deliberately does not copy a mutable “Phase 6 is in progress” sentence. For what is complete now, what is in flight and the next concrete action, use the dated Next step entry in docs/PROGRESS.md; that file is the project’s designated live-state authority (AGENTS.md, “When sources disagree”). This audit found that its entry at 8c781b1 lags three commits it should have recorded: it still says to freeze Arm 2 after 483f7ee froze the value content, 0cb52bf recorded that freeze commit and the final gated values hash, and 8c781b1 repaired the arm-specific combined revision hash. The disagreement is explicit; Git establishes the landed implementation while the progress file’s next action needs correction. The stable architectural distinction is simpler: Phase 3 is the development instrument, while Phase 7 is the product layer described by the charter.

And one limitation applies to all of it, including the recorded two-reviewer process that checked this page: every recorded non-maker reviewer-agent round is an LLM review. Maker audits are recorded separately, the maker is not identified as a domain scientist, and the repository records contain no human domain-expert review — in the project's own words a “disclosed limitation, not a substitute” (docs/stretch-sharing-and-investigation.md, §4). Chapter 29 gives that its own section, because by then you will be able to see what it costs.

You now know what the machine is for and what it refuses to claim. What you do not yet have is the vocabulary that makes those refusals precise — because “we gave the model experiment-constrained physical inputs” and “the model reproduces reality” sound like the same sentence, and treating them as one is the fastest way to fool yourself. The project's answer is not a confidence score. Every quantity it puts on the screen carries two labels, held apart on purpose: one for what the number is, and one for what it has earned (project charter.md, §1.5, line 80). The next chapter is about why one label was never enough.
