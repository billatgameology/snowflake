What It Cost, and What Is Still Open

Then they found the runs had disobeyed their own protocol, threw the whole sweep away, and wrote down in advance that the corrected score would be worse. Why would anyone do that — and what happened when the corrected score came back?

~18 min read 6 figures 5 interactives

By the end you will be able to

  • Explain why a pre-registration only proves itself the first time obeying it costs something.
  • Read a prediction against its own outcome — and say what “nearly right” earns and what it does not.
  • Read a limitations list written by the claimant as the most informative thing on the page — and write one.
  • Tell what a result licenses apart from what it merely suggests.
  • Say which of Part One’s open questions now carries a number, and which do not.

The number has been withdrawn

The first time this sweep ran it scored 5 of 90, with columns 0 of 24, cold plates 0 of 60, and zero invalid runs in 204. That headline no longer stands. It was withdrawn by the project itself, on the same date the finding was first written into the ledger; all 204 points were then run again; and the corrected run came in at 3 of 90, which is the number that stands now. Before the re-run existed, somebody wrote down what it would return. The withdrawal and that written-down prediction are the two most useful things in this section, and they arrive in that order.

A review compared the 204 runs against the protocol that was supposed to govern them, row by row. The frozen protocol records how each measured curve is filled in between the temperatures where it was actually measured. Two of those rows sit next to each other:

export const PHASE6_INTERPOLATION = {
  sigma0: “piecewise-log-log-linear”,
  aPrism: “piecewise-linear-in-(Tm-T)”,
  aBasal: “constant-1”,
  ...
} as const;

runner/src/phase6-protocol.ts, quoted in docs/decisions/0031-phase6-paramset-freeze-row.md

Read the strings as recipes. Tm is the melting temperature, so (Tm − T) is just how many degrees below freezing; “piecewise-linear” means join the measured points with straight lines (the σ0 row does the same joining on logarithmic axes); and “constant-1” means do not interpolate at all — the answer is always 1. The basal row says one thing, the prism row says another, and that difference is the whole story.

A_prism is the ceiling on how sticky the six side faces are allowed to get. In the model’s attachment rule — Libbrecht’s attachment coefficient, written alphaHK in the source to keep it apart from the lattice rule’s unrelated threshold — the value is alphaHK = A·exp(−σ0/σsurf), which climbs toward A as the air gets wetter. Two of those symbols were digitized off the same printed figure and they are not the same quantity: A is the prefactor out front, and σ0 is the term inside the exponent. Hold on to that difference — later in this chapter the project checks both against printed sources, and one of those two verdicts gets reversed against an earlier version of itself. The pair is easy to confuse because both go by “the digitized prism anchors”. The registration says A_basal is pinned at 1 and A_prism is not: it is interpolated through nine measured anchors. The decision record is blunt about it: “There is no reading of that row on which A_prism ≡ 1.”

And then the next line: “Every one of the 204 runs used A_prism ≡ 1.”

The mechanism is mundane, which is what makes it worth learning. The sweep harness never sets the parameter set at all — “the string does not appear in the file”. It launches one child process per point, and that child had a default buried in its command-line handling: paramSet: "CAK_A1", the parameter set in which the prism prefactor returns 1 at every temperature. So:

“an unregistered CLI default silently overrode a registered freeze row, through a path the freeze did not cover because paramSet was not itself a registered row.”

ADR 0031, The mechanism, named exactly

Key idea

This is worse than a gap in the rules, and the record says why: “The scheme was registered. The runs violated it. That is a materially worse failure than an unregistered degree of freedom, because the artifact and the evidence disagree while both look complete.” Two documents, each internally tidy, describing different experiments.

The charter had priced this in advance, in a clause written before anything had run: “Any post-freeze edit to parameters or protocol requires a logged ADR and invalidates prior sweep results — the full sweep re-runs.” Earlier amendments had triggered that clause and cost nothing, because no sweep had happened yet. This one was different: “Unlike ADRs 0025, 0026 and 0028, a sweep HAS now run, so the price is not zero: the 204-point sweep of 6995868 is invalidated and re-runs. That clause is the mechanism working exactly as designed, and the cost is the reason it was written.”

The structural repair took one sentence: paramSet itself becomes a registered row, and the harness must pass it explicitly to every child. “A freeze row that a CLI default can override is not frozen.” The protocol’s fingerprint moved from 9aa2e7c1… to 8aeb2b80… as the list went from 24 rows to 25 — and the record notes, drily, that “this is the first amendment that is not free”.

The free way out

There was another option, and it was a good one. The runs all used a simpler value for A_prism. Libbrecht himself uses that simplification in one of his own models, and writes down why. Nothing stopped the project from editing the registration to say constant-1, matching what had actually executed. The sweep would then have been in perfect conformance with its protocol. The headline would have stayed at 5 of 90. The cost would have been zero hours of compute.

You are about to be put in that seat. Both buttons below are real options that were on the table.

The decision record names the free option first, on purpose, and then refuses it:

“Rejected, and it is the alternative worth naming most explicitly because it is free: it costs no compute, keeps a better headline, and can be argued from Libbrecht’s own simplification. It is still wrong. The registered scheme predates the runs; changing it to match them after seeing the results converts a pre-registration into a post-registration, and every subsequent claim of pre-registered validation in this project would be worth less.”

ADR 0031, Alternatives considered

A softer version was refused too — keep the result and list the mismatch as a stated limitation. The answer to that one is worth memorising, because it is the difference between an honest caveat and a cover story: “The mismatch is not a limitation of the evidence, it is a contradiction between the evidence and the protocol that certifies it. A reader who checks the protocol against the runs finds a discrepancy the report knew about and shipped anyway.”

Worse, on purpose, in writing

Now the part that makes this an experiment rather than a tidy-up.

All five agreements in the withdrawn headline sat at a single temperature, −2 °C. Restoring the registered A_prism there throttles the prism prefactor down by a factor of 3.6 — the measured value at that temperature is 0.28, not 1. Less sticky side faces means the crystal spreads sideways less, which makes it thicker relative to its width. And the plate class has a ceiling. A crystal whose thickness-to-width ratio climbs past 0.6667 stops being scored as a plate, and the scoring rule frozen before the sweep counts “neither plate nor column” as a disagreement rather than an abstention.

So the correction was going to hurt, and the project worked out roughly how much before running it. Drag the slider and watch the wins go — then press the last button, which loads what the re-run actually measured at all five points.

How we know

The estimate has exactly two measured points behind it, and the record says so: at −8 °C a 1.75× throttle on the prefactor raised the aspect ratio by 1.36× (0.5789 → 0.7895), and at −5 °C a 5.6× throttle raised it by 2.62× (0.3821 → 1.0000). Interpolating between them puts −2 °C near a 1.9–2.2× rise. The record labels this honestly: “This is an ESTIMATE from two probe points, not a measurement; only the re-sweep settles it.” All five have since been settled. The measured rises came in at 1.35–1.61×, against the 1.9–2.2× predicted — the two-point interpolation ran hot, “which is why three agreements survived instead of two”. And the rise is not monotone in f, so a single throttle-to-rise factor was the wrong model for it in the first place; the two probes happened to sample the two ends. Read that as the estimate working: it got the direction right, the order of magnitude right, and the mechanism right, while getting the size wrong in a way its own record now names.

And then, in a block quote of its own, dated 2026-07-28, before the re-run existed:

“Registered prediction: the headline falls from 5/90 to approximately 2/90.”

ADR 0031

Why write that down? Because of what it takes off the table. If the number is predicted in advance, nobody can later look at two parameter sets, notice that one scores better, and choose it for that reason while telling a story about provenance. “It is written down now so that the parameterization cannot later be chosen by its score.” And the consequence section states the general rule in one line worth carrying out of this site:

“The corrected result is expected to be worse, and that is the point. A validation whose headline improves when you fix a defect is pleasant; one whose headline degrades is the case that tests whether the pre-registration is real. Recording the direction in advance is what makes the re-sweep an experiment rather than a search.”

ADR 0031, Consequences

Keep those two numbers where you can see them: 5 of 90 measured and withdrawn, about 2 of 90 forecast in writing. One more rule had to be nailed down before the re-run was allowed to start, and then the re-run reported.

What the re-run is forbidden to be

A re-run is a dangerous thing to have in your hands after a bad result. It would be very easy to present it as a second attempt — a chance to go back and get the columns band right this time. The decision record closes that door before the sweep starts.

Two facts make the closing credible. First, the change is confined. A figure caption in one of Libbrecht’s own papers (arXiv:2009.08404v2) states that between −10 and −30 °C the two prefactors are both about 1 — so in the cold-plates band “the two parameter sets are the same model”, and “the violation can only have affected roughly −2 to −9 °C”. Second, the reason the diagram cannot be reproduced has nothing to do with which parameter set is used: it is the counting argument from the last chapter, and CAK is a broad-facet parameterization exactly as CAK_A1 was.

“The re-sweep is expected to reproduce the warm plate regime less well and the other two regimes exactly as badly, and must not be presented as an attempt to fix the columns or cold-plates bands.”

ADR 0031, Consequences

With that fixed in advance, the re-run was allowed to start.

The re-run came in

All 204 points ran again under the registered parameter set, and the report of them carries the provenance a reader is meant to check: protocol 8aeb2b80… with its 25 freeze rows, parameter set CAK, execution commit 390fe35, the freeze commit “verified an ancestor of the execution commit”, exit 0, 204 of 204 points, about 20 hours of wall time on six workers, and a sha256 printed for each of the three output files. The raw output still lives in an evidence tree the repository does not track; the hashes of it are the tracked record, which is the project’s standing convention for research artifacts.

“Headline: 3 of 90 headline-scope points agree.”

“Outcome: 3/90. Direction correct, magnitude one point pessimistic.”

Careful

Three is not a small success. It is three points at one temperature. All three agreements are at −2 °C, the single counting temperature in the warmest regime — and the scoring rule registered that temperature pre-sweep as carrying essentially no statistical weight, “precisely so this could not be presented as a result”. The report says the rest plainly: “The model agrees with the reference nowhere else inside the headline scope.” So the honest reading of 3 of 90 is not “the model got a few right”. It is a negative result with a rounding error attached to it, and the project built the rule that says so before it knew which way the rounding would go.

The shape of the change is the shape ADR 0031 said to expect, which is its own kind of check. Correcting the prism prefactor shrank the plate region and widened the neutral band at the warm end: plate now ends at −4, −3 and −2 °C in the three driest rows and vanishes entirely in the wetter ones, while column onset stays exactly where it was, at −19 and −23 °C. The cold end did not move, because in that band the two parameter sets are the same model. Nothing was fixed. The warm end got worse and the rest stayed as bad as it was, on the record, in advance.

And one finding got stronger for having survived both runs. Wherever the model commits to a habit at all, the sequence is plate, then neutral, then column, once, with no return — one transition where the reference diagram has three. That now holds under both parameter sets, “which is stronger than holding under one”. The counting argument behind it was registered before either sweep, and it is indifferent to which broad-facet parameterization you feed it.

Still unsettled

Two things stop 3 of 90 from being a finished result, and the report puts both in its own opening. First, “these numbers are not yet gate evidence”: the verification work package has not run, no independent verifier has re-derived the summary from the raw per-point file, and the six negative controls — the tests designed to fail on purpose, to prove the checker can detect a fault — have not executed. “Until then this is a measured result with verified provenance, which is not the same thing.” Second, this is one arm of a two-arm comparison. On maker direction the phase reports the model without the missing mechanism as the control and the model with it as the treatment, together, rather than publishing this arm alone. The numbers above are final for this arm; what they mean for the phase waits.

There is a third thing, and it is the most uncomfortable entry in the whole report, because it is about whether the provenance block above says what it appears to say.

“A verification hazard found while this sweep ran, and recorded because it is not yet fixed. The harness captures provenance once at preflight, but each of the 204 children spawns runner/src/main.ts from the working tree. An edit to runner/src/ or core/src/ mid-run would change the physics for later points while the report still named the launch commit.”

research/phase6-sweep-report.md

A commit hash pins the code at the moment the run started. It does not pin the files the run keeps reading for the next twenty hours. And this was not hypothetical: nine commits from a parallel session landed while the sweep was running. The project then checked the one thing that mattered — whether any of them touched the physics directories — found the difference empty, and said so, along with the sentence that makes it a finding rather than a reassurance: “That was luck, not design.” The fix — a preflight assertion that the spawned command line carries every frozen parameter, plus a re-check when the run finishes — is written down as outstanding. Until it lands, the provenance of this sweep rests on a diff somebody remembered to run.

Turn on the caveats

The Phase 6 sweep report ends with a section headed “What this does NOT establish”. It is eight bullets long, it was written by the people who produced the result, and it is sharper than anything a hostile reader would have thought of. When you are learning to judge a claim, a limitations list written by the claimant is the single most informative artifact available — both for what it contains and for whether it exists at all.

Two of those eight bullets now begin with the word CORRECTED, and they move in opposite directions, which is the detail worth stopping on. One withdraws a limitation: an earlier entry said the project’s digitized σ0 curve for the prism faces was too low by 1.6–3.2× exactly where the agreements sit, and that claim is now marked “REVERSED” — the dedicated measurement papers give 0.03% at −2 °C and about 0.2% at −5 °C, the project’s curve matches them to roughly 7%, and the closed form it had been judging itself against turned out to be the outlier. The other bullet sharpens a limitation, and that one is discussed below. A limitations list that only ever grows is a ritual; one that corrects itself in both directions is being read.

Below, five claims from that result are printed as rows, and each stated limit is a switch. Turn them on one at a time and watch the counter.

Careful

With every switch on, nothing is left unqualified — and that is not the same as nothing being known. A result is a number plus the domain over which it holds. The switches are not doubts about whether the sweep happened; they are the boundaries of the sentence you are allowed to build from it. Notice also which way the limits point. Both zeroes have now been arrived at twice, under two different parameter sets, and neither moved: the 60 counted cold-plates points are bit-identical under either set, and every counted point in the columns band sits inside the window the violation touched, so those 24 were genuinely re-measured rather than inherited. Nothing on the list would make either band agree — and the one limit that used to point at the warm end, the suspect σ0 curve, has since been withdrawn by the project against itself.

Limits no re-run removes

Separately from the sweep’s caveats, the model itself has a list of things it simply does not contain. The Phase 6 plan carries it under the heading “Permanent model limits”: no latent-heat transport, no Gibbs–Thomson effect, no surface diffusion of admolecules, no sublimation, and a seed that is placed rather than nucleated. To that it adds an intrinsic anisotropy of about 10% that more grid resolution does not remove, and a growth-rate bias that comes from the convergence tolerance itself.

Two more entries, a few lines up the same page, deserve to be read slowly, because they bite hardest in exactly the experiment that was run. Here is the first, in full:

“No D(T) law exists. The solver, like the monograph’s working table, treats diffusivity as temperature-independent at 1 atm. This bites hardest in exactly this phase’s temperature sweeps.”

docs/plans/phase-6-nakaya-validation.md

How fast water vapour spreads through air really does depend on temperature. There is no published law for it in the sources this model was built from, so the model holds it fixed — in a study whose only swept axis is temperature. The second entry is shorter and no gentler: “Latent heating is unmodelled. Ignoring it overestimates diffusion-limited growth by 40–80% on the warm side.” Both were written into the plan before the sweep, not discovered afterwards.

Still unsettled

The obvious next experiment — switch the missing narrow-facet mechanism on and see whether the diagram appears — is the second arm of this phase, not yet run, and its ceiling is already fixed. Because the mechanism’s parameters were chosen against the Nakaya diagram in the first place, matching it can only ever be labelled in-sample reproduction, never independent validation. There is a harder problem underneath: the width scale the mechanism depends on is about 50 nm, and the registered grid cell is 350 nm. The thing the theory needs is seven times smaller than the smallest thing this model can see, so it has to be represented rather than resolved — and how it is represented has to be written down and frozen before that run, not after.

Who has actually checked this

Every safeguard in Part Two is a written rule, a test, or a hash. None of them answers the question a scientist would ask first: who read it?

The working rules open by answering it, and the answer is unusual. Their first paragraph reads: “This project is worked on by multiple different LLMs across sessions, with no shared memory between them. Any model may pick up work another left mid-flight. The markdown files described here are the authoritative handoff index.” There is no continuous mind on this project. There is a relay, and the baton is a set of text files.

The countermeasures are real and they are structural rather than aspirational. Reviews record the reviewing model, what it independently re-executed, and — the unusual part — what it did not check, stated as a limit of the evidence rather than left implicit. Gate-bearing reviews prefer a different model than the one that wrote the code. No component may supply both sides of a comparison it takes part in. All of that is better than nothing, and none of it is the thing that is missing.

All reviews to date, internal and external-facing, are LLM reviews; for publication that is a disclosed limitation, not a substitute.”

Key idea

That rule binds this page too. Part Two was written by a language model and checked by two more, and no human snow-crystal scientist has read a word of it. A chain of machines checking each other is not the same thing as one qualified person checking it once. The project treats that as a disclosed limitation rather than a substitute for expert review, and so should you — including when you are reading this sentence.

What the carefulness cost

The project turns its scepticism on its own method in several places — three of the working rules carry a line headed Paid for twice, naming the exact work that had to be done again. But the one place it puts a number on what its own carefulness cost is a single bullet in a section called Anti-rules — a list of things not to do, written by people who had done them.

The instruction attached to those numbers is “Don’t build new adversarial evidence machinery per work package. Reuse the existing verifier seams; a new seam is justified only by a new attack surface.” Roughly one commit in seven went on ceremony rather than on the thing itself, and almost half of all the redoing was hardening evidence rather than fixing physics — with, in the project’s own words, visibly declining yield after a certain point.

That does not undo Part Two. It qualifies it. Rigour is a design problem with trade-offs, not a virtue you can max out, and the same record shows the cost of skimping: one work package paid twice for the same lesson, because a study run at one crystal size was used to justify a decision at another, and its conclusion reversed when re-run at the registered size. Knowing where the knee of that curve is, is itself a skill, and this project would tell you it has not found it.

The thing being built

None of this was ever only about the diagram. The point of the machinery is an instrument, and the instrument does not die when a physics test comes back negative.

A decision record dated 2026-07-28 pins the product’s shape: four view profiles — Realistic, Scientific, Designer, Developer — that are “shells over one engine and one artifact”. The rule that makes them trustworthy is a single sentence: “A profile switch never changes solver behavior, schedules, checkpoints, or evidence semantics.” The beautiful mode cannot become a second, unreviewed physics path, because it has no pipe into the engine. And the Designer profile, which turns a user’s intent into a growth journey, maps that intent through the model’s own morphology diagram — the artifact the sweep produces — and the record spells out the label it must carry: “labeled as the model’s diagram, never nature’s; if Phase 6 lands negative in a regime, intents grounded there must say so.”

The capability that idea rests on has been demonstrated, and it is Part One’s central metaphor made into software. Phase 4’s gate closed on 2026-07-18, and among its criteria was a crystal grown through a two-segment history: column conditions, then plate conditions, producing a capped column — a column wearing plate hats, a shape you can find in real snow photographs. A snowflake is a record of its journey; here is a program that wrote one on purpose. Even the direction is on the record as a correction: the charter originally said plate→column, and a numbered decision fixed it to column→plate.

And here the chapter has to be careful with itself, because Phase 4 ran its whole gauntlet twice. Pass A used the borrowed threshold rule and was blocking: its job was to prove the lattice, the diffusion and the measuring instruments could express and detect each target shape at all. That is the pass the capped column belongs to. Pass B re-ran the same list under the measured physics and was diagnostic — allowed to miss — and it did miss: three morphology diagnostics passed and five honestly missed, the capped history among them. So the diary-of-a-journey capability is demonstrated on the machinery, and it was already an early warning about the physics, months before the sweep. Both halves of that sentence are in the same gate record.

Careful

None of those four profiles exists as software. Phases 0 through 5 are complete, Phase 6 is in flight — first sweep withdrawn, corrected sweep reported at 3 of 90, not yet gated, second arm not run — and Phase 7 — the whole product layer — is marked not started; there is no Phase 7 plan file. What exists is one accepted decision record describing what it will be. Read a confident specification as a promise that is binding on its authors, not as a description of a thing you could open.

What the result licenses

Here, finally, is the skill this whole site has been building toward. A result does not hand you a sentence. It licenses a small number of sentences and forbids the rest, and telling those apart is usually a matter of one verb.

Three sentences are supported, in ascending order of strength, and each one is pinned to a different artifact.

One. The model implements measured broad-facet attachment physics, and it reproduces the −5 °C laboratory measurements those inputs came from. Libbrecht’s substrate experiments at that temperature found aspect ratios between 0.1 and 1, with columnar crystals absent; the ledger records the sweep’s −5 °C points at 0.3821 to 1.0000 — inside that range, columns absent. Those two endpoints happen to bracket both parameter sets: 0.3821 is what the withdrawn run measured at −5 °C, and 1.0000 is what the registered one measures — ADR 0031 records the move, at the largest throttle in the whole table. So this claim survives the re-run in a way the headline does not. And the ledger attaches the limit in the same entry: that lab data is upstream of the model’s own inputs, so this is a strong consistency check, not independent validation.

Two. It does not reproduce the Nakaya diagram, and the shape of that failure is now measured rather than argued: one habit transition where the reference needs three, and a band 14 to 19 °C wide in which the model declines to commit to either shape — the single largest feature of the measured diagram. That is the sweep report’s own finding, it now holds under both parameter sets, and the counting argument behind it was registered before either run.

Three. Therefore the narrow-facet mechanism — the one Chapter 12 offered and Chapter 13 left you holding as a working hypothesis — is not optional decoration for this diagram, and this is the first time anyone has run the test that shows it. Through July 2026 a literature sweep found that mechanism had never been independently tested by anybody. The framing the project permits itself is exact: first quantitative, pre-registered, full-3D falsification test of a claim that existed only in prose. Not a discovery — the claim was Libbrecht’s, published in words. Nobody had run it.

And a fourth rung, drawn dashed and grey and not claimed: that this model predicts what nature does. The charter forbids that sentence in advance. What would license it is a test on data the model was not built from, and the project’s own register lists four candidates — free-fall growth data, facet-rate curves, size-dependent habit, a pressure ladder. None has been run.

Back to the frontier

Chapter 13 left you holding something uncomfortable. The explanation for why snow crystals flip between plates and columns — two curves, crossing — is, in the words of the people who built it, a working hypothesis with ill-defined curves. You were asked to accept it anyway, provisionally, the way scientists do.

Part Two is what happened when somebody built a machine to test it. The curves are still ill-defined: the location of the crossing carries no evidential weight at all, because the uncertainty already registered on the digitized curves slides it across a 16 °C span. The question on Chapter 13’s clock — why does the habit flip, three times over? — is still open, ninety years after Nakaya started drawing it. Its bar on that chart has not closed.

But it has changed, in one specific way. Before this, “broad-facet kinetics alone cannot explain the diagram” was a sentence in a paper. Now it has a number attached: 3 of 90, with all three of those agreements sitting at the single temperature the scoring rule had already declared weightless before anyone ran anything; columns 0 of 24; cold plates 0 of 60; zero invalid runs in 204, twice over — and a structural argument that says no amount of tuning the grid, the seed, the box or the diffusion can fix it, because none of those things reorders the two curves. The missing mechanism is now measurably not optional. That is one open question carrying a measurement it did not carry before. The other questions on that clock carry exactly what they carried.

And the honest close is a list of things nobody knows. The 3 of 90 is not yet gate evidence: no independent verifier has re-derived it from the raw per-point file, and the six negative controls have not run. It is one arm of two, and the arm with the missing mechanism switched on has not been run at all — when it is, the best it can earn is in-sample reproduction, because its parameters were chosen against the very diagram it would be reproducing, and the width scale it depends on is seven times smaller than this grid can see. The provenance hazard is recorded and unfixed, so the commit hash on that report is trustworthy because of a diff somebody ran, not because of a mechanism that would have caught it. And no human domain scientist has read any of this: not the sweep, not the code, not the charter, and not this page.

Key idea

The machinery in Part Two was not built to make the model right. It was built so that when the model came out wrong, you could tell whether the fault was in the theory or in the code — and the answer, in the findings ledger’s own phrase, is the signature of a correct implementation of an insufficient theory. That separation is the achievement. The score is just where it happened to land.

And if you keep one thing from this whole section, keep the sequence, in the order the dates run. A sweep measured 5 of 90. A review found the runs had violated a row of their own frozen protocol, so the project threw the result away under a rule it had written before there was anything to lose by it. Then, in a dated file, before the corrected sweep existed, it wrote down where the corrected number would land — about 2 of 90, worse than the one it was giving up — and explained why it was writing it down: so that “the parameterization cannot later be chosen by its score”. About twenty hours of machine time later, the corrected sweep returned 3 of 90. Direction correct. Magnitude one point pessimistic.

The impressive part is not that the forecast was nearly right. Forecasts are nearly right by accident all the time. The impressive part is that being nearly right was checkable — because the forecast was on the record, with a date on it, before the number existed, and it forecast a loss. That is the whole difference between an experiment and a search, and it is the one thing on this site you can apply tomorrow to a claim that has nothing to do with snow: ask what the claimant wrote down before they looked, and whether it cost them anything when they did.

That is the end of the story so far, which is a strange place to stop and exactly the right one: the interesting things in science are the sentences somebody has not been able to write yet.
