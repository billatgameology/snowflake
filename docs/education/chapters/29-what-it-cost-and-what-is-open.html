What It Cost, and What Is Still Open

Then they found the runs had disobeyed their own protocol, threw the whole sweep away, and wrote down in advance that the corrected score would be worse. Why would anyone do that — and what happened when the corrected score came back?

~18 min read 6 figures 5 interactives

By the end you will be able to

  • Explain why a pre-registration becomes most informative when obeying it costs something.
  • Read a prediction against its own outcome — and say what “nearly right” earns and what it does not.
  • Read a limitations list written by the claimant as the most informative thing on the page — and write one.
  • Tell a retracted interpretation from a retracted measurement, and say why only one of the two costs you your numbers.
  • Tell what a result licenses apart from what it merely suggests.
  • Say which of Part One’s open questions now carries a number, and which do not.

Current Phase 6 status — corrected 2026-08-01

Both historical 204-row arms are complete at measured-only scope: CAK 3/90 and M1 54/90 on the common scope, 54/78 on M1's arm scope. M1 is in-sample for Nakaya. Neither number is ADR 0026's registered conservative-intersection headline, and Phase 6 remains active and incomplete. Only ADR 0026’s conservative-intersection scoring rule is registered. R15 and M1_NO_DIP_ABLATION are planned but unfrozen, unimplemented and unexecuted; composed numerical adequacy, the preview-budget GPU cohort and all four held-out families remain open. The 408 historical CPU-oracle rows do not substitute for the charter’s hundreds of preview-resolution GPU-harness runs, and no ADR amended that obligation.

CAK→M1 is not a clean intervention: broad σ0 functions, prefactors, and dip factors all change. M1 is an everywhere-narrow approximation; it substitutes temperature-only curves for the unresolved width query. The width-dependent project M2 closure is still unimplemented.

Keep two uses of invalidated separate. The earlier CAK_A1 run contradicted its own frozen protocol and was invalid as that registered experiment. The accepted ADR 0040 correction does not erase the executed CAK or M1 bytes: it records them as historical measurements and their provenance does not satisfy the replacement gate. R15 remains planned, unfrozen, unimplemented and unexecuted.

The number has been withdrawn

The first time this sweep ran it scored 5 of 90, with columns 0 of 24, cold plates 0 of 60, and zero invalid runs in 204. That headline no longer stands. It was withdrawn by the project itself, on the same date the finding was first written into the ledger; all 204 points were then run again; and the corrected run came in at 3 of 90, which is the number that stands now. Before the re-run existed, somebody wrote down what it would return. Chapter 28 read that 3 as a result; this chapter is the invoice behind it, and the withdrawal and that written-down prediction are the first two lines on it. They arrive in that order.

A review compared the 204 runs against the protocol that was supposed to govern them, row by row. The frozen protocol records how each adopted, fitted, or digitized curve is filled in between its tabulated anchors. Two of those rows sit next to each other:

export const PHASE6_INTERPOLATION = {
  sigma0: “piecewise-log-log-linear”,
  aPrism: “piecewise-linear-in-(Tm-T)”,
  aBasal: “constant-1”,
  ...
} as const;

runner/src/phase6-protocol.ts, quoted in docs/decisions/0031-phase6-paramset-freeze-row.md

Read the strings as recipes. Tm is the melting temperature, so (Tm − T) is just how many degrees below freezing; “piecewise-linear” means join the recorded anchors with straight lines (the σ0 row does the same joining on logarithmic axes); and “constant-1” means do not interpolate at all — the answer is always 1. The basal row says one thing, the prism row says another, and that difference is the whole story.

A_prism is the ceiling on how sticky the six side faces are allowed to get. In the model’s attachment rule — Libbrecht’s attachment coefficient, written alphaHK in the source to keep it apart from the lattice rule’s unrelated threshold — the value is alphaHK = A·exp(−σ0/σsurf), which climbs toward A as the air gets wetter. Two of those symbols were digitized off the same printed figure and they are not the same quantity: A is the prefactor out front, and σ0 is the term inside the exponent. Hold on to that difference — later in this chapter the project checks both against printed sources, and one of those two verdicts gets reversed against an earlier version of itself. The pair is easy to confuse because both go by “the digitized prism anchors”. The registration says A_basal is pinned at 1 and A_prism is not: it is interpolated through nine figure-digitized P2 anchors. The decision record is blunt about it: “There is no reading of that row on which A_prism ≡ 1.”

And then the next line: “Every one of the 204 runs used A_prism ≡ 1.”

The mechanism is mundane, which is what makes it worth learning. The sweep harness never sets the parameter set at all — “the string does not appear in the file”. It launches one child process per point, and that child had a default buried in its command-line handling: paramSet: "CAK_A1", the parameter set in which the prism prefactor returns 1 at every temperature. So:

“an unregistered CLI default silently overrode a registered freeze row, through a path the freeze did not cover because paramSet was not itself a registered row.”

ADR 0031, The mechanism, named exactly

Key idea

This is worse than a gap in the rules, and the record says why: “The scheme was registered. The runs violated it. That is a materially worse failure than an unregistered degree of freedom, because the artifact and the evidence disagree while both look complete.” Two documents, each internally tidy, describing different experiments.

The charter had priced this in advance, in a clause written before anything had run: “Any post-freeze edit to parameters or protocol requires a logged ADR and invalidates prior sweep results — the full sweep re-runs.” Earlier amendments had triggered that clause and cost nothing, because no sweep had happened yet. This one was different: “Unlike ADRs 0025, 0026 and 0028, a sweep HAS now run, so the price is not zero: the 204-point sweep of 6995868 is invalidated and re-runs. That clause is the mechanism working exactly as designed, and the cost is the reason it was written.”

The structural repair took one sentence: paramSet itself becomes a registered row, and the harness must pass it explicitly to every child. “A freeze row that a CLI default can override is not frozen.” The protocol’s fingerprint moved from 9aa2e7c1… to 8aeb2b80… as the list went from 24 rows to 25 — and the record notes, drily, that “this is the first amendment that is not free”.

The free way out

There was another option, and it was a good one. The runs all used a simpler value for A_prism. Libbrecht himself uses that simplification in one of his own models, and writes down why. Nothing stopped the project from editing the registration to say constant-1, matching what had actually executed. The sweep would then have been in perfect conformance with its protocol. The headline would have stayed at 5 of 90. The cost would have been zero hours of compute.

You are about to be put in that seat. Both buttons below are real options that were on the table.

The decision record names the free option first, on purpose, and then refuses it:

“Rejected, and it is the alternative worth naming most explicitly because it is free: it costs no compute, keeps a better headline, and can be argued from Libbrecht’s own simplification. It is still wrong. The registered scheme predates the runs; changing it to match them after seeing the results converts a pre-registration into a post-registration, and every subsequent claim of pre-registered validation in this project would be worth less.”

ADR 0031, Alternatives considered

A softer version was refused too — keep the result and list the mismatch as a stated limitation. The answer to that one is worth memorising, because it is the difference between an honest caveat and a cover story: “The mismatch is not a limitation of the evidence, it is a contradiction between the evidence and the protocol that certifies it. A reader who checks the protocol against the runs finds a discrepancy the report knew about and shipped anyway.”

Worse, on purpose, in writing

Now the part that makes this an experiment rather than a tidy-up.

All five agreements in the withdrawn headline sat at a single temperature, −2 °C. Restoring the registered A_prism there throttles the prism prefactor down by a factor of 3.6 — the registered figure-digitized P2 value at that temperature is 0.28, not 1. Less sticky side faces means the crystal spreads sideways less, which makes it thicker relative to its width. And the plate class has a ceiling. A crystal whose thickness-to-width ratio climbs past 0.6667 stops being scored as a plate, and the scoring rule frozen before the sweep counts “neither plate nor column” as a disagreement rather than an abstention.

So the correction was going to hurt, and the project worked out roughly how much before running it. Drag the slider and watch the wins go — then press the last button, which loads what the re-run actually measured at all five points.

How we know

The estimate has exactly two measured points behind it, and the record says so: at −8 °C a 1.75× throttle on the prefactor raised the aspect ratio by 1.36× (0.5789 → 0.7895), and at −5 °C a 5.6× throttle raised it by 2.62× (0.3821 → 1.0000). Interpolating between them puts −2 °C near a 1.9–2.2× rise. The record labels this honestly: “This is an ESTIMATE from two probe points, not a measurement; only the re-sweep settles it.” All five have since been settled. The measured rises came in at 1.35–1.61×, against the 1.9–2.2× predicted — the two-point interpolation ran hot, “which is why three agreements survived instead of two”. And the rise is not monotone in f, so a single throttle-to-rise factor was the wrong model for it in the first place; the two probes happened to sample the two ends. Read that as a limited directional forecast: it got the sign and rough scale of the response right, but the non-monotone result does not establish a mechanism and the magnitude was wrong in a way its own record now names.

And then, in a block quote of its own, dated 2026-07-28, before the re-run existed:

“Registered prediction: the headline falls from 5/90 to approximately 2/90.”

ADR 0031

Why write that down? Because it makes a later score-driven switch detectable when the correct history and verifier are inspected. It does not make confusion or misconduct impossible. The historical record put it more strongly: “It is written down now so that the parameterization cannot later be chosen by its score.” Read that as a procedural rule with an auditable trail, not a theorem about human behaviour. And the consequence section states the general rule in one line worth carrying out of this site:

“The corrected result is expected to be worse, and that is the point. A validation whose headline improves when you fix a defect is pleasant; one whose headline degrades is the case that tests whether the pre-registration is real. Recording the direction in advance is what makes the re-sweep an experiment rather than a search.”

ADR 0031, Consequences

Keep those two numbers where you can see them: 5 of 90 measured and withdrawn, about 2 of 90 forecast in writing. One more rule had to be nailed down before the re-run was allowed to start, and then the re-run reported.

What the re-run is forbidden to be

A re-run is a dangerous thing to have in your hands after a bad result. It would be very easy to present it as a second attempt — a chance to go back and get the columns band right this time. The decision record makes that reinterpretation detectable if the registered history is actually checked.

Two arguments were offered at the time for why that door could be closed honestly. First, that the change was confined: a figure caption in one of Libbrecht’s own papers (arXiv:2009.08404v2) says that between −10 and −30 °C the two prefactors are both about 1, so in the cold-plates band “the two parameter sets are the same model” and the violation “can only have affected roughly −2 to −9 °C”. Second, that the reason the diagram cannot be reproduced has nothing to do with which parameter set is used, because it is a counting argument about two curves — and CAK is a broad-facet parameterization exactly as CAK_A1 was.

“The re-sweep is expected to reproduce the warm plate regime less well, and must not be presented as an attempt to fix the columns or cold-plates bands.”

ADR 0031, Consequences

Hold on to both of those arguments, because neither of them lasted. An audit dated 2026-07-29 — the same day the corrected sweep was scored, and so well after both arguments had been written down to license it — weakened the first and retracted the second outright. Both strikings are now printed inside the decision record itself, immediately above the sentences they cancel; you will meet each of them shortly, at the point in this chapter where it does its damage. The prohibition they were supporting survived both. That is the part worth keeping: a rule can be right for reasons that turn out to be wrong, and writing the reasons somewhere checkable is what made that correction possible here.

With that fixed in advance, the re-run was allowed to start.

The re-run came in

All 204 points ran again under the registered parameter set CAK, against the 25-row protocol 8aeb2b80…, and finished clean: exit 0, 204 of 204 points, about twenty hours of wall time on six workers. Chapter 28 reads that provenance table as part of the result; the row this chapter needs off it is the last one, a sha256 printed for each of the three output files. The exact points and report bytes are now tracked under evidence/phase6-sweep/ and independently pinned by evidence/MANIFEST.json. The hashes remain provenance checks over those published bytes; they are not a substitute for the bytes.

“Headline: 3 of 90 headline-scope points agree.”

research/phase6-sweep-report.md

Predicted in writing, before the run: about 2 of 90. Measured: 3 of 90. The report’s verdict on its own forecast is one line, and it marks itself down rather than up:

“Outcome: 3/90. Direction correct, magnitude one point pessimistic.”

research/phase6-sweep-report.md, The registered prediction, and how it did

How that 3 is distributed, and how the whole measured picture moved with it, is in chapter 28’s per-regime table rather than repeated here — that is the corrected sweep read as a result, regime by regime and class by class. Two lines off it are all the bill needs. Warm plates scored 3 of 6, columns 0 of 24, cold plates 0 of 60. And all three agreements sit at −2 °C, the single counting temperature in the warmest regime, which the scoring rule had registered pre-sweep as carrying essentially no weight for a temperature-general conclusion. Nevertheless, −2 °C contributes six equally counted humidity cells to the 90-point measured headline, and three agree. Three is not a small success or a temperature-general result. What this chapter adds is the price of being allowed to print it.

Two things the twenty hours bought that never appear on the scoreboard. The first is that the change arrived in the shape ADR 0031 said to expect, which is a check on the forecast that survives the count being one point off: the warm end got worse, the cold end kept every habit class it already had — “No plates-cold point changed class”, which is what the 0 of 60 rests on — and nothing anywhere was fixed. The confinement argument itself came back a notch weaker than it had been written. The prism prefactor is 0.830 at −10 °C and only reaches 1 at −15, so the two parameter sets do not actually coincide until about −15 °C; the violation’s reach was roughly −2 to −14 °C rather than −2 to −9; and 11 of the 72 points between −10 and −21 °C did move, by up to 0.1092 in aspect ratio — never far enough to cross a class line. “The same model” was an overstatement of a conclusion that happened to be true.

The second is a measured claim that got stronger for having been made twice. Wherever the model commits to a habit at all, the sequence is plate, then neutral, then column, once, with no return — one transition where the reference diagram has three — and that now holds under both parameter sets, “which is stronger than holding under one”. A score can move when you change the inputs; a measurement that does not move is worth more than the score. What did not survive is the explanation the project had attached to that measurement, and losing it is the largest correction anywhere in Part Two.

Careful

The pre-registered reason for “one transition, never three” was a counting argument that promoted crossings of the two critical-supersaturation functions into habit boundaries. That promotion is retracted. Even the full attachment coefficients can be ordered analytically only at the same positive surface supersaturation; the result is then basal-higher, prism-higher, or a tie. It is an input-function diagnostic, not a plate/column label. The 3-D solver has facet-dependent fields, geometry, size, and seed history, and only the frozen evaluator may assign morphology from published forward-run bytes. What survives is measured and narrower: historical CAK, at its executed configuration, scores 3/90 and does not reproduce the diagram. That is not a necessity theorem about every broad-facet model. The retraction is printed at the head of the sweep report, inside ADR 0031 and inside the findings ledger, and it is careful to say what it does not touch: “the 3/90 headline, the per-regime table, the class totals, the diagram, the artifact hashes — are unaffected. What is retracted is the interpretation.”

Still unsettled

At the report’s historical audit-cutoff snapshot, two things stopped 3 of 90 from being a finished result, and the report put both in its own opening. First, “these numbers are not yet gate evidence”. Its historical inventory says the verification work package had not run, no independent verifier had re-derived the summary from the raw per-point file, and the original six negative controls — the tests designed to fail on purpose, to prove the checker can detect a fault — had not executed. “Until then this is a measured result with verified provenance, which is not the same thing.” Second, this is one arm of a two-arm comparison. On maker direction the phase reports the model without the missing mechanism as the control and the model with it as the treatment, together, rather than publishing this arm alone. The numbers above were final for that arm. Both snapshot items have since advanced: the next callout records the hardened verifier, and Arm 2 later completed. Phase 6 still waits on the different replacement obligations named at the top of this chapter.

Updated

That is the report’s wording as written, and the work behind it did not stop there. As of 2026-07-29, seven negative controls have executed (not six) — app/scripts/phase6-wp5-negative-controls.mjs — and an independent verifier — app/scripts/phase6-wp5-independent.mjs — re-derives all 204 points and reports PASS. The verifier itself did not start out earning that: a follow-up audit found it printed the three artifact hashes without asserting them, checked one of report.json’s nine fields, and trusted a published pass/fail flag it should have recomputed — and under those gaps it had certified two real forgeries, a substituted superseded-arm artifact and a domain-contact mutation that would have read the headline as 3 of 3 instead of 3 of 90, both as “VERIFIED…exit 0”. Commit 990840a hardened it: all three registered artifact digests are asserted, the exact 204-point grid is checked for duplicate, missing, and extra rows, every report field is re-derived, and ambiguity and domain-contact status are recomputed. It now correctly fails both. The seven controls’ own two real gaps — an undetectable splice from a different parameter set, and a scored run whose measurement size was never checked against the registered one — are addressed at b701285 by decision record 0035: the child command and output are checked against every pinned input, a size-target stop is required, and measured extent must be at least 21. Arm 1 has no per-row configuration or stop-reason field, so later code cannot retroactively give it one; re-checking all 204 recorded extents found exactly 21 and therefore moved no class or headline. This is one more entry for the budget this chapter is about: real, executed, adversarial work after publication, closing real holes. It hardened the historical artifact but did not create the replacement scientific gate. The current label for 3/90 is measured-only; R15 and its numerical, GPU, ablation, and held-out obligations remain open.

There is a third thing, and it is the most uncomfortable entry in the whole report, because it is about whether the provenance block above says what it appears to say.

“A verification hazard found while this sweep ran, and recorded because it is not yet fixed. The harness captures provenance once at preflight, but each of the 204 children spawns runner/src/main.ts from the working tree. An edit to runner/src/ or core/src/ mid-run would change the physics for later points while the report still named the launch commit.”

research/phase6-sweep-report.md

A commit hash pins the code at the moment the run started. It does not pin the files the run keeps reading for the next twenty hours. And this was not hypothetical: nine commits from a parallel session landed while the sweep was running. The project then checked the one thing that mattered — whether any of them touched the physics directories — found the difference empty, and said so, along with the sentence that makes it a finding rather than a reassurance: “That was luck, not design.” The fix — a preflight assertion that the spawned command line carries every frozen parameter, plus a re-check when the run finishes — is written down there as outstanding. For historical Arm 1, the provenance still rests on a diff somebody remembered to run; the forward fix below does not rewrite that artifact.

Updated at commit 154359d. The completion-time re-check named as outstanding above has since landed: the sweep dynamically enumerates every TypeScript source file under core/src, solver-cpu/src and runner/src and fingerprints the set and bytes before the first child launches and again after the last one finishes. There were 39 files at that commit; additions are included rather than hidden behind a fixed count. The sweep throws rather than publishing if the two endpoint fingerprints disagree. This catches a commit that remains landed or an edit present at an endpoint; it does not exclude a transient reverted edit, inherited NODE_OPTIONS, or a shadow module. R15 still owes an immutable source snapshot, an environment allowlist and verified child source identity. The endpoint check cannot reach backward and verify Arm 1, whose provenance is exactly what this section already described: a diff somebody remembered to run, and luck rather than design that it came back empty.

The second arm moved through execution as well. Its M1 value content was frozen at 483f7ee; 0cb52bf then recorded that freeze ancestry and repinned the final gated values hash. Commit 8c781b1 records an arm-specific combined protocol revision hash that moves with prose and is therefore revision-pinned rather than frozen as a gate. That repair was caught by launching the sweep: its first line exposed Arm 1’s combined hash, so that run was stopped about a minute in. The repaired canonical 204-row M1 sweep later completed at 54/90 on Arm 1's common measured-only denominator, or 54/78 under Arm 2's own exclusions; its classes are 75 plate, 119 neutral, and 10 column. It is in-sample for Nakaya and remains separate from CAK's 3/90. The change is also confounded because broad curves, prefactors, and dip factors all differ.

Turn on the caveats

The Phase 6 sweep report ends with a section headed “What this does NOT establish”. It is eight bullets long, it was written by the people who produced the result. When you are learning to judge a claim, a limitations list written by the claimant can be one of the most informative artifacts — both for what it contains and for whether it exists at all.

Two of those eight bullets now begin with the word CORRECTED, and they move in opposite directions, which is the detail worth stopping on. One withdraws a limitation: an earlier entry said the project’s P2 digitized σ0 curve for the prism faces was too low by 1.6–3.2× exactly where the agreements sit, and that claim is now marked “REVERSED” — dedicated source papers report fitted or model-inferred P2 values of 0.03% at −2 °C and about 0.2% at −5 °C, the project’s curve matches those reported source values to roughly 7%, and the closed form it had been judging itself against turned out to be the outlier. The other bullet sharpens a limitation, and that one is discussed below. A limitations list that only ever grows is a ritual; one that corrects itself in both directions is being read.

Below, five claims from that result are printed as rows, and each stated limit is a switch. One of the ten switches is the retraction from earlier in this chapter, wired to the one claim it actually touches. Turn them on one at a time and watch the counter.

Careful

With every switch on, nothing is left unqualified — and that is not the same as nothing being known. A result is a number plus the domain over which it holds. The switches are not doubts about whether the sweep happened; they are the boundaries of the sentence you are allowed to build from it. Notice also which way the limits point. Both executions reported the same two zeroes, but the earlier CAK_A1 execution violated its frozen protocol and is not an admissible registered replication. In the standing historical CAK artifact, not one of the 60 counted cold-plates points agrees, and all 24 counted columns points were rerun under the corrected parameter set rather than inherited. The one limit that used to point at the warm end, the suspect σ0 curve, has since been withdrawn by the project against itself.

Limits no re-run removes

Separately from the sweep’s caveats, the model itself has a list of things it simply does not contain. The Phase 6 plan carries it under the heading “Permanent model limits”: no latent-heat transport, no Gibbs–Thomson effect, no explicit surface diffusion of admolecules, no sublimation, and a seed that is placed rather than nucleated. Premelting enters only insofar as the recorded parameterizations encode it; the project has not shown that they quantitatively absorb all such effects. Latent heat and explicit surface diffusion are therefore systematics to quantify or carry, not omissions presumed harmless for validation.

Earlier reconnaissance also measured about 10% lattice anisotropy and a tolerance-dependent growth-rate bias at named configurations. It varied size, domain, grid, and timestep in separate compositions, so those observations do not establish that refinement leaves the anisotropy unchanged or that the errors compose benignly. R15 still owes the composed numerical-adequacy campaign.

Two more entries, a few lines up the same page, deserve to be read slowly, because they bite hardest in exactly the experiment that was run. Here is the first, in full:

“No D(T) law exists. The solver, like the monograph’s working table, treats diffusivity as temperature-independent at 1 atm. This bites hardest in exactly this phase’s temperature sweeps.”

docs/plans/phase-6-nakaya-validation.md

How fast water vapour spreads through air really does depend on temperature. There is no published D(T) law in the registered source set, so the model follows the monograph’s temperature-independent working table at 1 atm — in a study where temperature is a central swept axis. The second entry is shorter and no gentler: “Latent heating is unmodelled. Ignoring it overestimates diffusion-limited growth by 40–80% on the warm side.” That is a source-side scale carried as a systematic, not a measured correction to each historical row. Both gaps were written into the plan before the sweep, not discovered afterwards.

Still unsettled

The historical narrow-facet M1 arm has now completed: 54/90 common measured-only scope, or 54/78 on its own exclusions. Because its source-prescribed exact dip forms, depths, denominator constants, and placement were chosen against the Nakaya diagram and remain P3, matching it can only ever be labelled in-sample reproduction, never independent validation. Published model-dependent inversions of narrow-facet growth observations support approximate barrier-reduction regions, but do not directly verify σsurf or the exact M1 form. The source leaves log base-unspecified; the project’s Figure-1-width-supported base-10 transcription is P4. There is a harder problem underneath: the width scale the mechanism depends on is about 50 nm, and the registered grid cell is 350 nm. M1 substitutes an everywhere-narrow approximation rather than solving the closure: accepted decision 0036 uses temperature-only forms with A = 1 on both facets and performs no facet-width query. The width-dependent project M2 model, which needs a sub-grid rule and an unprinted strength parameter, remains unimplemented. The M1 outcome is in-sample and the CAK→M1 difference is confounded. The planned but unfrozen, unimplemented and unexecuted M1_NO_DIP_ABLATION could isolate only the implemented intervention effect under one frozen sampled configuration, not physical SDAK causality or necessity.

Who has actually checked this

Every safeguard in Part Two is a written rule, a test, or a hash. None of them answers the question a scientist would ask first: who read it?

The working rules open by answering it, and the answer is unusual. Their first paragraph reads: “This project is worked on by multiple different LLMs across sessions, with no shared memory between them. Any model may pick up work another left mid-flight. The markdown files described here are the authoritative handoff index.” There is no continuous mind on this project. There is a relay, and the baton is a set of text files.

The countermeasures are real and they are structural rather than aspirational. Reviews record the reviewing model, what it independently re-executed, and — the unusual part — what it did not check, stated as a limit of the evidence rather than left implicit. Gate-bearing reviews prefer a different model than the one that wrote the code. No component may supply both sides of a comparison it takes part in. All of that is better than nothing, and none of it is the thing that is missing.

The project states this itself, in the process requirements that gate anything leaving the repository: at least one human domain scientist must read any manuscript before submission. Repository records presently contain no such review. Every recorded non-maker reviewer-agent round is an LLM review; maker audits are recorded separately, and the maker is not identified as a domain scientist. That is a disclosed limitation rather than a substitute.

Key idea

That rule binds this page too. Part Two was written by a language model and its two recorded reviewers are language models; no human snow-crystal expert review is recorded. A chain of machines checking each other is not the same thing as one qualified person checking it once. The project treats that as a disclosed limitation rather than a substitute for expert review, and so should you — including when you are reading this sentence.

What the carefulness cost

You have met this number already. Chapter 18 read it off the end of the rulebook, out of the anti-rules — a list of things not to do, written by people who had done them: “Integrity has a budget: ceremony ran ~15% of all commits and evidence-hardening ~45% of all rework through Phase 5, with visibly declining yield after WP5.” On the ladder it was a percentage with nothing to weigh against it. Here there is something to weigh: three bills have since come in, and all three are itemised on this page.

The instruction attached to those numbers is “Don’t build new adversarial evidence machinery per work package. Reuse the existing verifier seams; a new seam is justified only by a new attack surface.” Now price it against something. The first bill is the one this chapter opened with. An entire 204-point sweep, already run, scored and written up, was invalidated by a clause the project had written against itself; about twenty hours of machine time went on producing its replacement; and a better-looking headline was handed back at the same time. Every hour of that is ceremony in the anti-rule’s own sense — not one of it went into the physics, and the physics came out worse for it. That is what the 15% looks like on a single line item, with a real number of hours attached rather than a percentage.

The second bill is what the other side of the trade-off costs, and the record has an entry for that too: one work package paid twice for the same lesson, because a study run at one crystal size was used to justify a decision at another, and its conclusion reversed when it was re-run at the registered size. Three of the working rules now carry a line headed Paid for twice, naming the exact work that had to be done again. That is the same 45% seen from the other end — rework that only existed because the evidence had not been hardened the first time.

The third bill is the retraction, and it is the awkward one, because no machinery malfunctioned to produce it. Retracting the structural claim cost an eighteen-agent adversarial audit, a reproduction of that audit before anyone was allowed to record it, retraction notes in three documents, and the narrowing of the most quotable result the project had. What it bought is a single new line in the working rules, and that line is worth more than the claim was: “State the measured claim, not the strongest claim. ‘Cannot’, ‘every’, ‘always’, ‘independent of’, and ‘provably’ assert theorems: they require a stated derivation whose quantity and scope are both named — and the derivation must be about the quantity the claim governs.” That last clause is the failure here. The derivation counted σ0 equalities; the claim governed 3-D morphology. The rule is filed under Paid for twice in two days, which is what it cost.

None of that undoes Part Two. It qualifies it. Rigour is a design problem with a cost on both sides, not a virtue you can max out: skimp and you pay for the same lesson twice; spend and you buy twenty hours of arithmetic whose only product is a worse headline; spend as hard as this project did and you can still publish a theorem you had not proved. The project’s own record says the returns on the spending side are visibly falling. Knowing where the knee of that curve is, is itself a skill, and this project would tell you it has not found it.

The thing being built

None of this was ever only about the diagram. The point of the machinery is an instrument, and the instrument does not die when a physics test comes back negative.

A decision record dated 2026-07-28 pins the product’s shape: four view profiles — Realistic, Scientific, Designer, Developer — that are “shells over one engine and one artifact”. The rule that makes them trustworthy is a single sentence: “A profile switch never changes solver behavior, schedules, checkpoints, or evidence semantics.” The beautiful mode cannot become a second, unreviewed physics path, because it has no pipe into the engine. And the Designer profile, which turns a user’s intent into a growth journey, maps that intent through the model’s own morphology diagram — the artifact the sweep produces — and the record spells out the label it must carry: “labeled as the model’s diagram, never nature’s; if Phase 6 lands negative in a regime, intents grounded there must say so.”

There is already something a person can open: Phase 3 built a browser development instrument, with the CPU solver in a Web Worker, overlays, a vapour slice, picking, readouts, and a deterministic visual harness. That is not the polished product just specified. Phase 7 owns the audience profiles, storytelling shell, and product-level finish; keeping those two nouns separate prevents an implemented scientific instrument from being erased and a future product from being claimed early.

The capability that idea rests on has been demonstrated, and it is Part One’s central metaphor made into software. Phase 4’s gate closed on 2026-07-18, and among its criteria was a crystal grown through a two-segment history: column conditions, then plate conditions, producing a capped column — a column wearing plate hats, a shape you can find in real snow photographs. A snowflake is a record of its journey; here is a program that wrote one on purpose. Even the direction is on the record as a correction: the charter originally said plate→column, and a numbered decision fixed it to column→plate.

And here the chapter has to be careful with itself, because Phase 4 ran its whole gauntlet twice. Pass A used the borrowed threshold rule and was blocking: its job was to prove the lattice, the diffusion and the measuring instruments could express and detect each target shape at all. That is the pass the capped column belongs to. Pass B re-ran the same list under the physically parameterized P1/P2 attachment rule and was diagnostic — allowed to miss — and it did miss: three morphology diagnostics passed and five honestly missed, the capped history among them. So the diary-of-a-journey capability is demonstrated on the machinery, and it was already an early warning about the physics, months before the sweep. Both halves of that sentence are in the same gate record.

Careful

None of those four product profiles exists as software. The Phase 3 development instrument does. Phase 6 is active and incomplete — historical CAK 3/90 and M1 54/90 common (54/78 arm scope) are measured-only, while R15 and its numerical, GPU, matched-ablation, and held-out obligations remain open — and Phase 7, the product layer, is marked not started; there is no Phase 7 plan file. What exists for those profiles is one accepted decision record describing what they will be. Read a confident specification as a promise binding on its authors, not as a description of a shipped product.

What the result licenses

Here, finally, is the skill this whole site has been building toward. A result does not hand you a sentence. It licenses a small number of sentences and forbids the rest, and telling those apart is usually a matter of one verb.

Three sentences are supported, in ascending order of strength, and each one is pinned to a different artifact. Above them sits a rung nobody claims. And one more sentence was on the supported list until 2026-07-29 — watching that one come off is the most useful thing in the section.

One. The model implements a physically parameterized broad-facet attachment rule with recorded P1/P2 provenance. Measured or source-tabulated empirical inputs adopted directly are P1, including recorded textbook transport properties; source fits, model inversions, project derivations, and figure digitizations remain P2. The prism prefactor anchors were digitized before the later printed fit was found and reproduce it to 8.4% at worst and under 2% typically. That is an independent cross-check of one extraction, not proof that every input or the modeled surface state was directly measured.

Two. Historical CAK, at its executed extent-21 configuration, does not reproduce the registered Nakaya comparison: 3/90 measured-only, with 6 plate, 168 neutral, and 30 column classes. M1 likewise does not fully reproduce it: 54/90 common scope and 54/78 arm scope, in-sample, with 75/119/10 classes. These are forward-morphology results; no input-curve equality is promoted into a habit boundary. Neither historical count is the registered replacement headline, and neither establishes numerical adequacy.

Three. The historical CAK→M1 comparison does not isolate the narrow-facet dip factors. It changes broad σ0 functions, prefactors, and dips together, so its score difference is confounded. A scoped literature search supplies context, not a priority claim. A future matched M1/no-dip pair could isolate only the implemented dip-factor intervention effect under one frozen sampled configuration; it cannot establish physical SDAK causality or necessity, and it has not been frozen, implemented or run. M1 itself is an everywhere-narrow approximation, while the width-dependent project M2 closure is still unimplemented.

And a fourth rung, drawn dashed and grey and not claimed: that this model predicts what nature does. The charter forbids that sentence in advance. What would license it is a test on data the model was not built from. The charter requires all four families: growth rates versus temperature and supersaturation, size-dependent habit, pressure dependence, and growth-history responses. The tracked source lock reports passEligible=false and no audited apples-to-apples target; no held-out family has been frozen or run, and the available pressure comparison is confounded with no quantitative pass target.

And the one that came off the list. Until 2026-07-29 there was one more supported sentence: that the model also reproduces Libbrecht’s −5 °C laboratory measurements, the ones its own inputs came from. His substrate experiments there found aspect ratios between 0.1 and 1 with columnar crystals absent, and the ledger recorded the sweep’s −5 °C points as landing inside that range. Then somebody read the corrected sweep’s own −5 °C row: 0.7895, 1.0000, 1.0000, 1.1053, 1.2353, 1.3125. All six neutral; three of them above the ratio of 1 he reported as absent; not one of them a plate, which is what he actually found. The report withdrew the claim in its own text — at −5 °C the model produces no habit at all, disagreeing with the diagram and with the bench — and took a second clause down with it: he never says what radius his aspect ratio is measured against, so whether his number and the model’s are even the same quantity is unverified, and an equal-area radius would move his by about 10%. The withdrawn run had measured 0.3821 there and looked like a match. The correction that cost the headline two points cost this claim all of it.

Back to the frontier

Chapter 13 left you holding something uncomfortable. Narrow-facet feedback is, in the words of the people who built it, a working hypothesis with ill-defined curves. A restricted coefficient-order calculation can compare facets only at the same positive surface field; it does not explain a 3-D plate/column reversal by itself.

Part Two is what happened when somebody built a machine to test it. The curves are still uncertain. Under the project's registered, Figure-1-width-supported P4 base-10 transcription of the source's base-unspecified log, the values −3.08, −8.07, and −24.73 °C are restricted equal-A/shared-positive-field equality locations, not dip centres or habit boundaries. The dip centres −4.5 and −14.4 °C are invariant to logarithm base. The question on Chapter 13’s clock — why does the habit flip, three times over? — is still open, ninety years after Nakaya started drawing it. Its bar on that chart has not closed.

But it has changed, in one specific way. A source claimed that broad-facet kinetics alone cannot explain the diagram. Historical CAK now supplies a scoped forward-run number: 3 of 90, with all three agreements at one temperature that contributes six equally counted cells but essentially no support for a temperature-general conclusion; columns 0 of 24; cold plates 0 of 60; zero named invalidities in 204, twice over. A five-seed, two-temperature probe also moved both points in the same direction and rescued neither target. Then notice what that list does not contain: a proof that no broad-facet model could do better, or that a temperature-independent knob can never help under any other configuration. One historical parameterization was tested at a named configuration and missed. That is one open question carrying a scoped measurement it did not carry before. The other questions on that clock carry exactly what they carried — and one of them, whether the broad-facet picture could ever have been enough on its own, remains open.

And the honest close is a list of things still open. CAK 3/90 and M1 54/90 common (54/78 arm scope) are preserved as historical measured-only results. M1 is in-sample because its exact functions and placement are P3 Nakaya-informed; its base-10 reading of the source’s base-unspecified log is a project P4 transcription. Source model inversions support approximate narrow-facet reduction regions, not direct measurement of σsurf or verification of the exact M1 form. M1 also assumes every facet is narrow while the width-dependent M2 closure is unimplemented. The historical bytes stay checkable. Accepted ADR 0040 corrects their provenance without freezing R15; the old runs remain inadmissible to the replacement gate, whose full execution is still future work. Repository records contain no human domain-expert review of this corpus.

Current-status boundary. Later safeguards do not repair or rewrite old bytes. The tracked Tier 1 fixtures self-report win32/x64 and darwin/arm64 identities; those headers are not independent physical-host authentication. Their bytes differ in 9 of 448 entries, but omit the registered −31 through −35 °C tail. A historical Tier 2 table reports four matching CAK rows attributed to the latter fixture; its raw output and exit records are unavailable, so the table is not independently rederivable. Phase 6 remains open for the science-first work, not the old audit-cutoff list: a future reviewed R15 freeze and full replacement execution, composed size/domain/grid/timestep adequacy, 612 planned preview-budget GPU cases across CAK, M1, and matched no-dip, the matched software ablation, and all four charter held-out families. Current source review has not found an apples-to-apples held-out target, which is a scientific scope blocker rather than permission to promote a near match.

Part Two names repository sources beside its load-bearing quotations and claims, so those specific statements can be checked against the recorded files. That citation practice is not a machine-complete census and does not make every sentence independently source-verifiable; the papers and monograph behind Part One are listed on the references page.

Key idea

The machinery in Part Two was not built to make the model right. It was built so that when the model came out wrong, named implementation and evidence faults could be detected rather than silently absorbed. A historical findings-ledger phrase called CAK the signature of a correct implementation of an insufficient theory. Read that as a scoped interpretation of the checks and parameterization actually run, not a proof of implementation correctness or universal theoretical necessity. The score is just where the historical execution landed.

And if you keep one thing from this whole section, keep the sequence, in the order the dates run. A sweep measured 5 of 90. A review found the runs had violated a row of their own frozen protocol, so the project threw the result away under a rule it had written before there was anything to lose by it. Then, in a dated file, before the corrected sweep existed, it wrote down where the corrected number would land — about 2 of 90, worse than the one it was giving up — and explained why it was writing it down: so that “the parameterization cannot later be chosen by its score”. About twenty hours of machine time later, the corrected sweep returned 3 of 90. Direction correct. Magnitude one point pessimistic.

The impressive part is not that the forecast was nearly right. Forecasts are nearly right by accident all the time. The impressive part is that being nearly right was checkable — because the forecast was on the record, with a date on it, before the number existed, and it forecast a loss. That is the whole difference between an experiment and a search, and it is the one thing on this site you can apply tomorrow to a claim that has nothing to do with snow: ask what the claimant wrote down before they looked, and whether it cost them anything when they did.

That is the end of the story so far, which is a strange place to stop and exactly the right one: the interesting things in science are the sentences somebody has not been able to write yet. So take chapter 13’s last instruction, which has been waiting sixteen chapters for you. The next time it snows on a cold day — the big stars grow near −15 °C up in the cloud, so cold days are when they reach the ground intact — take a magnifier and a piece of dark card outside and look. It is a better invitation now than it was then, because you know what is landing on the card: a lattice choosing its own shape one molecule at a time, and choosing it in a way that the model in Part Two — built from recorded empirical inputs, source fits, model inversions, and project digitizations — matched at 3/90 historical CAK points and 54/90 historical M1 common-scope points. Those are measured-only; M1 is in-sample and the comparison is confounded. The replacement scientific gate is unfinished. The field is small enough that careful looking still counts.
