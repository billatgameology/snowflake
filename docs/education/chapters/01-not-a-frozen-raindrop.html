Chapter 1

Not a Frozen Raindrop

What is actually landing on your sleeve, and where did it come from?

~15 min read 12 figures 3 animations

By the end you will be able to

  • Tell a snow crystal from a snowflake, and say which of the two has six-fold symmetry.
  • Trace the route water takes — vapour to cloud droplet to vapour again to ice — and name the step the crystal skips.
  • Explain why a cloud droplet does not freeze at 0 °C, and say what actually decides when it does.
  • Give the real mechanism behind “too cold to snow.”
  • Say where the design of a snow crystal is stored — and why the honest answer is nowhere.

Two words, one of which means a crystal

Catch one on a dark coat sleeve and hold still. What have you caught? Sometimes it is the six-pointed star from the holiday card. More often it is a stubby splinter of ice, or a little pencil shape, or a grey clump the size of a fingernail that looks like nothing at all. All of that is snow. Only some of it is a snow crystal.

Start with the word that has a hard edge to it. A snow crystal is a single crystal of ice — one continuous, precisely repeating array of water molecules, with no seams and no joins anywhere in it. Kenneth Libbrecht, who has spent decades measuring how these things grow, puts it plainly: “The latter is a single crystal of ice, in which the water molecules are all lined up in a precise hexagonal array. Whenever you see that characteristic six-fold symmetry often associated with snowflakes, you are actually looking at a snow crystal.” The six-fold symmetry is not decoration laid on top of the object. It is the internal arrangement of the molecules showing through to the outside.

That accounts for the six-ness — why six arms and not five or seven. It does not account for why all six arms carry the same ornament as each other. Hold that question; the last section of this chapter answers it.

Six-fold symmetry does not only mean six arms. It can be written into the surface of a flat plate as a pattern of ridges and hollows that repeats six times around, with no arms involved at all.

And plenty of snow crystals are not flat and not star-shaped at all. Change the cloud temperature by ten degrees and ice grows into something you would never put on a card.

Careful

“All snowflakes are six-pointed stars.” They are not. Stars are one shape among many, and the published caption for Figure 1.1 pins them to a narrow temperature band near −15 °C. Near −5 °C the very same substance — the same water, the same lattice — grows into stubby hexagonal columns instead. Ten degrees changes the answer completely.

Now the second word. Snowflake is a weather word rather than a physics word, and it is much looser. It can mean a single snow crystal. It can mean a small cluster of them. It can also mean one of those grey puffballs that drift down in mild weather: “Those large puff-balls you see floating down in warmer snowfalls are called snowflakes, and each is made from hundreds or even thousands of individual snow crystals.”

That matters more than it sounds. A puffball has no symmetry of its own. Every one of the hundreds of crystals inside it may be a flawless hexagon, but the clump is just a clump — crystals that happened to bump into each other on the way down and stuck. If you try to explain six-fold symmetry by looking at a puffball you will get nowhere, because there is none there to explain. Calling a snow crystal a snowflake is fine, “like calling a tulip a flower.” Calling a puffball a crystal is not.

Not a frozen raindrop

The single most common idea about snow is that it is rain that froze on the way down. It is worth stating the correction as bluntly as the source does: “A snow crystal is not a frozen raindrop; that type of precipitation is called sleet. Rather, a snow crystal forms out of water vapor in the atmosphere, as water molecules transition directly from the gaseous to the solid state.”

Those are two genuinely different manufacturing routes, and they leave different objects. A raindrop that freezes is a bead of ice that was liquid first, and it looks like exactly that: a small lump. A snow crystal is assembled the other way round. Individual water molecules drift through cold air as a gas, arrive at a piece of ice, and lock into place on it, one at a time. There is no liquid stage in that process at all. Gas going straight to solid has a name — deposition — and it is the only way the crystal itself is built.

One honest complication, because it is the kind of detail that decides whether you actually understand something. The very first speck of a snow crystal is a frozen droplet: “Once a cloud droplet freezes, it becomes an embryonic snow crystal that commences growing by absorbing water vapor from the air around it.” So the seed was liquid. But the seed is a droplet ten to twenty microns across — a hundredth of a millimetre or two — and everything you admire in Figure 1.1, every arm, plate, ridge and rib, was deposited on it out of the air afterwards. The droplet count later in this chapter puts a figure on that: the seed is roughly one part in a hundred thousand of the finished object.

Key idea

A snow crystal is not frozen water. It is deposited water — built molecule by molecule out of vapour onto a solid surface. Only the seed at its centre was ever liquid. Everything you can see was laid down straight from the gas.

There is a third thing that falls, and it is worth being able to name it outdoors. Crystals that are still growing sometimes fly through a patch of cloud full of liquid droplets, and the droplets freeze onto them on contact: “growing snow crystals collide with cloud droplets, creating what is called rime – basically collections of minute, frozen droplets.” Rime looks like a coating of tiny beads sitting on top of a crystal, because that is precisely what it is.

Rime does not break the rule in the paragraph above; it sharpens it. Rime adds mass to a crystal, but not the way the crystal itself was added: each bead arrived as liquid and froze where it landed, on the outside, instead of being laid down molecule by molecule out of the gas. Rime is weight without pattern — a crystal wearing a coat, not a bigger crystal — which is exactly why a heavily rimed crystal stops looking like a crystal at all: “If the rime coating becomes so thick that the entire structure is mostly an agglomeration of frozen droplets, then it is called graupel, or soft hail.” (printed pp. 388–389)

Where the raw material comes from

If the crystal is built out of vapour, the obvious next question is where a cloud keeps its vapour, and why any of it should end up on a growing piece of ice. The story starts with air being lifted.

Warm, moist air pushed upwards expands and cools. Cool it far enough and it passes what meteorologists call the dew point: the air is now carrying more water vapour than it can hang on to at that temperature. In the source’s words, “the relative humidity rises above 100 percent and the air becomes supersaturated with water vapor.” That word — supersaturated — is going to matter enormously later on, so it is worth fixing now in plain terms: supersaturated air is air carrying spare water vapour, more than it can hold on to. The spare part is what condenses out.

One qualification travels with that sentence and is easy to skip: more than it can hold on to on liquid water’s terms. Relative humidity is measured against liquid water, and 100 percent means the air and a pool of water are exactly in balance. Ice sets a different bar, and a lower one. Keep that in your pocket for two sections’ time.

At ground level the spare water settles on the grass as dew, which is where the dew point gets its name. High up, with no grass to settle on, it condenses onto microscopic specks of dust instead — and dust is everywhere in the atmosphere. Each speck picks up a coat of liquid water and becomes a cloud droplet. That is what a cloud is: not vapour, but an enormous number of tiny liquid drops.

How tiny? “Cloud droplets are so small – about 10-20 microns in diameter – that they can remain suspended in the air almost indefinitely.” A micron is a thousandth of a millimetre. For scale, the exceptional stellar crystal the author photographed in Kiruna, Sweden, measures “about four millimeters from tip to tip” (printed p. 16) — a couple of hundred droplet-widths across. Droplets that small barely fall at all; air resistance holds them up. A cloud is not really floating so much as failing to sink.

Nothing freezes at zero

Here is the step most people skip straight past. A cloud made of liquid droplets cools below 0 °C. What happens? The intuitive answer — it turns to ice — is wrong, and the way in which it is wrong is the reason snow exists at all.

“Not all the droplets freeze at once, and none will freeze right at 0 C. Instead, the droplets become supercooled as their temperature drops, often remaining in a metastable liquid state for long periods of time.” Metastable means the droplet is not in the state it would settle into if it could — ice — but it has no way to get there, so it stays liquid and stays put. Supercooled means liquid, below its own freezing point, and staying that way. It is not a rare laboratory curiosity. It is the ordinary condition of a winter cloud.

The droplets do freeze eventually, but they do it one at a time and over an enormous temperature range: “Some droplets will freeze when the temperature drops below -5 C, and most will freeze somewhere around -10 C. A hearty few may survive unfrozen at -20 C or below, but all will become solid ice before the temperature reaches -40 C.”

Why the spread? Because freezing has to start somewhere. Ice does not appear everywhere in a droplet at once; a first tiny patch of it has to form, and that is much easier if there is a surface for the water molecules to organise themselves against. Which surface is on offer is decided by whatever speck the droplet condensed on in the first place. Getting that first patch started is called nucleation, and a surface that makes it easy is said to nucleate freezing. Water with nothing useful in it “can be cooled to nearly -40 C before freezing, while some materials (silver iodide in particular) will nucleate freezing as high as -4 C. Certain bacterial proteins can even promote freezing at temperatures as high as -2 C.” Those exotic materials are not much present in the atmosphere, so “your average speck of dust will nucleate freezing around -10 C.”

Careful

“Water freezes at 0 °C.” Ice melts at 0 °C, which is a different statement. Freezing is not the reverse of melting run backwards: it needs a starting point, and a clean droplet has nothing to offer one. That is why pure water can be taken to nearly −40 °C and still be liquid, and why the dust speck inside a droplet decides when it freezes. Cool it far enough, though, and the liquid eventually manages that first patch unaided, with no surface involved at all — the source measures the surface energy between ice and water from exactly that process, “the homogeneous nucleation of ice from supercooled water droplets”, down to temperatures near −40 °C (printed p. 59). So nearly −40 °C is a floor rather than a coincidence: it is where water stops needing help. The speck does not decide what the crystal will look like — the speck “usually has little effect on the final snow crystal shape, because it is microscopic in size and soon becomes buried within the ice.”

A hundred thousand droplets, one crystal

So one droplet in a cloud full of droplets finally freezes. It is now a speck of ice surrounded by neighbours that are still liquid. What happens next is a slow, one-way robbery, and the source lays it out in four sketches.

The shape in the second sketch is worth naming, because the rest of this site turns on it. Strip any snow crystal — star, plate or column — back to the simplest form ice can take and you get the same solid: a hexagonal prism. Two flat ends, six flat sides. Each kind of face has its own name.

The paragraph caught between the two panels of that strip made a claim in passing — that facets grow slower than rough surfaces do. It is worth seeing directly, because the same effect, carried one step further, is where the six branches actually come from.

Now the part that pays for all of it. Ice and liquid water do not hold on to their molecules equally hard. Liquid water lets them escape into the air more readily than ice does — “the vapor pressure of liquid water is higher than that of solid ice” — and the consequence is that a droplet and a piece of ice sitting in the same parcel of air are not in balance.

This is the qualification from two sections ago coming due. Air sitting in balance with the droplets around it — 100 percent relative humidity, nothing spare at all as far as the droplets are concerned — is already supersaturated as far as ice is concerned. Ice holds its molecules more tightly, so it is satisfied by less vapour, so there is spare vapour in that air the moment a piece of ice turns up in it. The droplet evaporates faster than it collects; the ice collects faster than it evaporates. Put the two side by side in a cold cloud and the droplet loses, every time. That is why supersaturation on the rest of this site is quoted against ice and not against water: it is the ice’s surplus that does the work. We will put real numbers on its size later on; for now the direction is what counts.

So the crystal grows by pulling vapour out of the air immediately around it, and the local air is then topped up by the neighbouring droplets evaporating. Water goes liquid, then gas, then solid, and after that first frozen seed the crystal never touches liquid again. The source calls it a roundabout route, and gives its price: “About 100,000 cloud droplets will evaporate away to provide enough material to make one good-sized snowflake. This roundabout route is how most of the liquid water in a cloud freezes into solid ice.”

Key idea

One good-sized snow crystal costs about 100,000 cloud droplets, and none of that water ever arrives at the crystal as liquid. It goes droplet → vapour → ice, every molecule of it. (“Snow crystal”, not “snowflake”: the printed sentence quoted above says snowflake, but Figure 1.5’s caption prices the same 100,000 droplets as “one large stellar snow crystal”, which is the thing actually being built.)

Too cold to snow

The relay in the previous section has a consequence you can check against your own winter. A snowfall is not fed by cold. It is fed by liquid water — specifically by supercooled droplets standing by to evaporate. Take the droplets away and the growth stops, no matter how much water the sky started with.

Follow a cloud down the thermometer. Above about −5 °C few droplets have frozen, so there is very little ice in the cloud for vapour to build on. As the cloud falls “substantially below -5 C, cloud droplets will freeze in large numbers, thus initiating a full-fledged snowfall.” This is the productive window: plenty of ice to grow on, plenty of liquid left to feed it. Keep cooling, though, and the supply burns through. “By the time the cloud has cooled to around -20 C, most of the liquid droplets will be gone, as some will have frozen and many will have evaporated away.” (Both quotations: printed p. 20.)

And that is the whole of it: “At temperatures below -20 C, it is often said to be “too cold to snow,” because nearly all the liquid cloud droplets will have already disappeared before the cloud cools to that temperature. And when no liquid water remains to feed growing snowflakes, there can be no snowfall.” (printed p. 20)

Careful

“Too cold to snow” is usually dismissed as an old wives’ tale. It is not, and it is also not what it sounds like. Cold air does not refuse to make snow. What happens is that the cloud has already spent its liquid droplets on the way down to −20 °C — some froze, many evaporated — and the relay that feeds growing crystals has nothing left to hand over. The saying is about running out of fuel, not about the cold itself.

Nobody is designing this

One thing remains, and it is the thing that makes the rest of this site necessary. You now know where the water comes from and how it arrives. You do not know why it lands in a pattern. And the temptation, faced with an object as ordered as Figure 1.1, is to assume that something somewhere is specifying it.

Nothing is. That is not a poetic flourish; it is a statement about where the information lives, and the answer is that it lives nowhere. There is no plan in the ice, none in the water, and none in the cloud. We are simply not used to that, because it is the opposite of the way we build things ourselves: “Humans tend to create intricate objects via a subtractive process, beginning with bulk material and carving it into a final desired form, following a preconceived design.” Carving requires knowing the answer in advance. Nature does not work that way. “At the opposite end of the fabrication spectrum, living things develop into amazingly sophisticated organisms quite spontaneously, using the additive process of growth.” (Both quotations: printed p. 33.)

A snow crystal is built by addition too, and the only thing that ever varies is the conditions it happens to be passing through at the time. Change those conditions and the growth changes, immediately and visibly. Some crystals record that change so plainly you can read it off with the naked eye.

That is suggestive, but a crystal found on the ground is only circumstantial evidence: you are inferring the history from the object, and you never saw the weather that made it. The decisive test runs the other way round — impose a history on purpose, in a laboratory, and watch what comes out.

How we know

The crystal in Figure 1.11 was grown in the author’s own laboratory — “a snow crystal I grew suspended in my lab” (printed p. 22) — where the temperature and the amount of spare vapour in the air could be changed at will and the response watched live. In the author’s account: “It took about 45 minutes to create this specimen, and I controlled the formation of its sidebranches and faceted features in real time by following the rules of snow-crystal growth.” That is the difference between a story about how snowflakes might work and a claim you can test: the environment was the only input, and the pattern came out.

That also settles the question left hanging in the first section. The lattice explains why a star has six arms. It does not explain why all six carry the same ornament — the same side branch in the same place, on every arm of Figure 1.11 and of Figure 1.1. Nothing joins the arms except the block of ice in the middle, and they are not comparing notes: “The six branches of a snow crystal develop in near synchrony simply because they all travel together through the cloud. Thus the six branches all experience essentially the same growth conditions at the same times, so all six develop into the same elaborate shape.” The source rules out the alternative in as many words: “the growth of the six branches is not synchronized by any communication between them, but rather by their common history.” (Both quotations: printed p. 21.) One crystal, one path through the cloud, one weather report — written out six times over by six arms that happened to be along for the same ride.

The question underneath all of this — how does complex structure arise with nobody arranging it? — is much older than snowflake research and much bigger than snow. D’Arcy Wentworth Thompson’s On Growth and Form put it at the centre of biology, “in which he pondered the physical, biological, and mathematical origins of complex structures in Nature” — while admitting that crystal growth was “somewhat outside the province of his book.” (Both quotations: printed p. 32.) The source draws the obvious moral: “An easier approach, therefore, might be to consider something like the snowflake, which exhibits an interesting degree of spontaneous structure formation, but in a far simpler physical system.” (printed p. 33)

That is the bargain this site is built on. A snow crystal is one substance, in one phase, with two dials on it — how cold it is, and how much spare vapour is around. If complex form can arise from nothing but those two dials being turned, we ought to be able to follow it all the way down.

Still unsettled

We are not there yet, and it is worth knowing that from the first chapter. Thin plates and stellar crystals appear near −15 °C; stubby columns appear near −5 °C. Of that plain, century-old observation the source says: “Why is this? … it is a long-standing puzzle” and “a complete understanding of even this straightforward observation remains elusive.” More generally, “several rather basic aspects of the surface structure and dynamics of ice at the molecular level remain quite mysterious.” (printed pp. 16–17) This site walks towards that puzzle on purpose.

Boundary of this course

Libbrecht’s 523-page monograph also ranges into ice dendrites growing from liquid water, a geographic “cartography” of natural snow-crystal populations, a broad survey of simulation methods, and the craft of field and studio photography. They are valuable adjacent subjects, but they are not expanded into parallel courses here. Part One follows the causal spine of a snow crystal growing from water vapour; it uses experiments and photographs as evidence, not as a photography manual. Part Two examines this repository’s two models rather than attempting a history of every numerical method. These are declared scope boundaries, not claims that the omitted chapters are unimportant.

Nothing is designing this — so the design has to come from the ice itself and from the sky it falls through, and you have now seen both halves at work: the lattice that fixes the six-ness, and the path through the cloud that writes everything else. Working out which is which has taken four hundred years and is not finished — “the quest for true scientific understanding continues to this day” (printed p. 23). The first person to ask the question properly could not answer it either. Next: what people saw when they finally started looking closely.
