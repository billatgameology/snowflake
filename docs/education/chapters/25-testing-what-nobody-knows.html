Chapter 25

Testing What Nobody Knows

You can test add(2, 2) because you already know the answer is 4. What do you test when the output is a snow crystal and nobody on Earth knows what it should look like?

~18 min read 7 interactives Part VIII · Proving the machine

By the end you will be able to

  • Write an assertion that is insensitive to what you do not know and sensitive to what would be a bug.
  • Ask of any test you meet: what deletion would this catch?
  • Explain why a green test suite can mean either “the code works” or “the checks are asleep” — and name the one thing that tells them apart.
  • Say why a program that refuses to answer can be more trustworthy than one that answers.

What is the 4?

Every test you have ever written has the same shape. You call something with a known input, you compare what comes back against a known correct answer, and you go green or red. The known correct answer has a name in software engineering: the oracle. In add(2, 2) the oracle is 4, and it costs nothing, because you knew it before you wrote the function.

Now call the previous chapter's program instead. grow(−15 °C, σ = 2 %). Out comes a crystal. What is the 4?

There isn't one. If anybody already knew the shape those equations produce at −15 °C, nobody would need to run them. This is the oracle problem, and it is the normal condition of simulation science. Worse, the output is a picture, and plenty of generative-art toys make gorgeous six-armed snowflakes out of rules with no physics in them at all. Beautiful is exactly what a wrong snowflake program looks like. The charter says the quiet part out loud:

“a designer tool can be honest about being a model; a fake physics readout is worse than none — and a real-looking number resting on an untested model is the most convincing fake of all.”

project charter.md §1.5, line 87

So the tests here never assert the answer. They assert what the answer must obey whatever it turns out to be. Water cannot appear from nowhere. A sixfold-symmetric setup cannot grow a lopsided crystal. Shrink the problem until algebra can solve it and the code has to hit the algebra. And — the part that does the real work — break the code deliberately and the test has to scream.

That last one matters, because those properties are not equally good and which is good is not obvious. Before reading on, go and find out for yourself.

Your own eye does better than the written-down version of it. Build C is visibly lopsided and you can see that for yourself. But your eye has no chance at all with build D, which draws exactly the same crystal as the correct build, cell for cell, while its books are out by 109 times the mass that started in the box. That is what “the picture proves nothing” means — not that pictures are always wrong, but that you cannot tell which kind of wrong you are looking at.

The bigger surprise is the assertion that feels most rigorous. attachedCount === 115 does two bad things at once: it goes red on the build whose only difference is a random seed — a false alarm — and green on build D, where the count is identical. An exact snapshot of an output you do not understand is not a strong test; it tests only whether anything changed. The project's own version of that comparison is deliberately looser: it requires the same habit class and attached counts within 0.5 %, and the measured drift was 5,161 to 5,159 (runner/test/phase6-sweep.test.ts, lines 178–204).

Key idea

A good assertion is insensitive to what you do not know — the shape, the count, the seed — and sensitive to what would be a bug. That is the whole design rule, and it is why the repository asserts conservation and symmetry rather than crystals.

Things the answer must obey

With the answer off the table, here is what is left. Five families, all of them in this repository, roughly in order of how much they cost to write.

Conservation. Water molecules do not appear or vanish. In a sealed box the total — vapour still drifting, vapour clinging to the surface, ice — must be the same after ten thousand ticks as before, checkable to about fifteen digits while knowing nothing about what grew. Every word of the test's title was fought for: “conserves total mass to < 1e-10 relative over 10 000 ticks GROWN, noise OFF, reflecting”. An earlier version ran ten thousand ticks with no crystal plus a separate 4,800-tick run with one, and the audit that caught it is recorded in the comment: “a 10k crystal-free control plus a 4800-tick grown run is not the declared 10k grown experiment” (solver-cpu/test/gg-solver.test.ts, lines 53–55). The crystal-free run survives as its own test, characterising the pure floating-point floor below 1e-13 — which is what tells you the 1e-10 above it is a budget rather than noise.

Symmetry, exactly. Most simulations settle for “approximately”. This one asserts toBe(0), not toBeCloseTo — possible only because the solver adds neighbouring cells in a fixed, symmetry-respecting order. Floating-point addition is not associative, and a cell and its rotated twin see the same six values in a different order. The solver's header states the consequence flatly: “Symmetric cells round identically, so the gate's ‘exactly 0’ is structural, not luck” (solver-cpu/src/gg-solver.ts, lines 33–34). Chapter 20 told that story; here the point is that an expected value of exactly zero leaves no tolerance to argue about.

An analytic reference. You cannot solve a branched 3-D crystal by hand. You can solve one sphere sitting in vapour, in one dimension, with a pencil. The steady field around it is

σ(r) = A + B / r

where
σ(r)
supersaturation at distance r from the sphere's centre — the spare vapour the air is carrying there
r
distance from the centre of the sphere
A, B
two unknown constants, fixed by two boundary conditions: the outer shell holds A + B/Rfar = σ∞, and the ice surface imposes X0 σ′(R) = a σ(R)
X0
the kinetic length — the distance scale at which attachment and diffusion trade places
a
the attachment coefficient at the surface (0.393 in this test problem)

solver-cpu/test/spherical-reference.test.ts, lines 26–30

The discretised solver must reproduce that to a relative error below 1e-9, at four grid spacings. This is the project's only absolute accuracy anchor: everywhere else the tests check consistency, here they check correctness. Notice what the test file refuses to do. Rather than transcribe the monograph's printed solution it re-solves the problem from scratch, and says why: “Nothing in this function comes from Eqs. 3.33-3.36, so agreeing with it is real evidence.” Copying a formula out of a book and confirming your code agrees with the book proves nothing.

Properties instead of examples. An example test says add(2, 2) === 4. A property test says for every input this rule holds, and checks the whole input space when it is small enough to enumerate. core/test/lattice.test.ts is almost entirely properties: index to coordinates and back is the identity, over every index; the neighbour relation is mutual in all eight directions; mirroring twice returns you to the start. The nicest is it("rot60 has order 6 exactly"), which turns the hexagon six times, demands you land back where you started, and demands you have not landed there after one, two, three, four or five turns. Without that second half, a function that did nothing would pass.

A second implementation. The slow, readable version on the processor is treated as the definition of correct; the fast one on the graphics card has to reproduce it inside a declared envelope. That is chapter 26's whole subject.

How we know

The repository has 69 test files holding roughly 1,040 individual cases, counted by matching it( and test( across every *.test.ts. A startling fraction of them exist for one reason only: to prove that other tests are capable of failing. The next four sections are about those.

Could this test have failed?

The most dangerous test in any codebase is not the one that fails. It is the one that passes for the wrong reason.

Here is a real example, from the project's own specification. The charter asked for a check phrased like this: a crystal-free run holds σ at the set value. That sounds like a genuine test of the boundary condition — the rule deciding whether the box's outer wall is sealed (vapour bounces back) or replenished (topped up from outside). Fill the box uniformly at the set value, run it, check the value is still there.

It is worthless. A uniform field is a fixed point of both boundary conditions, so the test passes whether the boundary code exists, works, is broken, or was deleted an hour ago. The repository files this under traps already paid for: “A uniform field initialized at the Dirichlet set value is a fixed point under reflecting and fixed-σ boundaries alike. Use the documented depleted-start differential test.” (AGENTS.md lines 266–267.) The falsifiable version starts at half the target and demands the two arms disagree: the replenished one climbs back to the set value everywhere, the sealed one conserves its mass and settles at the depleted mean. Only a test whose arms come out different proves the code paths are different.

Test 3 — “the source file still mentions dirichlet” — is a joke, but only just. Tests that check that code exists rather than that it does something are common, and they are exactly this useless.

Test 4 is the interesting one. It compares the boundary's injection meter against the mass the field actually gained. Delete the clamp and the meter reads zero — but so does the gain, because nothing was injected. Written as an absolute difference that would be 0 === 0 and would print PASS: measuring nothing scores the same as agreeing. The repository's version divides by the gain, so the deleted build fails closed. Push the fill slider to 100 % and the same collapse hits the good build, which is why the registered test starts at half.

One habit follows directly. Where a check runs inside a loop, it counts its own iterations and asserts the count. After comparing shell cells against their rotated images bit for bit, the far-field symmetry test's last line reads expect(compared).toBeGreaterThan(100); — “the check must actually have had work to do” (solver-cpu/test/monopole-far-field.test.ts, line 223). A loop that never executes passes every assertion inside it.

Careful

“More tests means more trustworthy” is false, and this project's own rule says so: “a green self-test is not sufficient evidence for a scientific gate” (AGENTS.md line 243). A thousand tests that cannot fail are worth less than one that can. The question to ask of any test is never “does it pass?” but “what deletion would this catch?”

Sixteen saboteurs

If the only way to know a test can fail is to make it fail, somebody has to break the code on purpose. Laboratory science has a name for this: the negative control — the same experiment with the crucial ingredient left out. If it still produces a result, your instrument is measuring something other than what you think.

The repository's rule for gates: “For a gate, derive every precondition from the charter/spec and make the process fail by name when one is violated. Pin each bypass with an adversarial negative-control test.” (AGENTS.md lines 249–250.) In Phase 5 that comes out unusually tidy. Sixteen pass/fail criteria; sixteen registered sabotages; each sabotage owned by exactly one criterion, with a test asserting the ownership list matches the criteria list, in order, with no duplicates. Then a test is generated per criterion, which applies that criterion's sabotage and asserts the set of things that failed is [that criterion] — not “includes”, not “at least”. Exactly it.

Two things about that grid. The diagonal is the assertion — a mark off it would mean a criterion failing for a reason that is not its own. And the only way to get an empty column is to disable a check: loosen the symmetry threshold and NC-SYMMETRY-BIT flips its attachment bit, the evaluator shrugs, everything stays green. That is what a broken safeguard looks like from outside. It looks like success.

Even the saboteur list is closed: ask the runner to apply a control nobody registered and it throws unregistered Phase 5 negative control rather than quietly doing nothing. Rule 9 adds the requirement that makes the apparatus mean anything — “Every negative control must execute its named mutation, verified by something other than its author” — and records why: a gate candidate was rejected because ten of its sixteen negative controls never executed the mutation they were named after. They reported that they had.

Key idea

Two dashboards, both entirely green. One means the code works. The other means the checks are asleep. Nothing on the screen distinguishes them. The only way to tell them apart is to break something on purpose and see whether anyone notices.

Grading your own homework

Suppose a solver keeps a ledger: a running total of the vapour it has turned into ice. How do you test the ledger? The tempting answer is to ask the solver what it thinks it did. That comparison always passes and means nothing, because the same code produced both sides of it.

The repository states this as a rule and then a slogan. Rule 9 is titled “A verdict is computed from the artifact, never inherited from its producer”, and its operative sentence is “No component may supply both sides of a comparison it participates in.” The slogan version is shorter: “a report agreeing with itself does not prove its ledger” (AGENTS.md lines 254–256).

So the strong tests compute the expected answer a second time, by a different route, using none of the production code's internals. One is titled in capitals: it("NON-TAUTOLOGICAL: the ledger delta equals independently recomputed kinetic demand"). It rebuilds the expected growth from public fields and raw physics functions, and draws the line explicitly: “Neither solver rate cache nor ledger internals enter the expected value.” It also insists on being run somewhere difficult — it fails if the run never hits a step where demand has to be clipped, because clipping is where the historical bug lived. In an early audit, recomputed demand exceeded the ledger by 35 % while the clipping happened silently (solver-cpu/test/lk-solver.test.ts, lines 780–817).

The peek switch is the point of the demo. Nothing about the test looks different afterwards. It still runs, it still compares two numbers, it is still green. It has simply stopped being able to be red, and there is no way to see that from the outside except by reading what it compares.

The harness that agreed with nothing

There is a failure mode one level up from a bad test: a bad reader of tests. A program that collects results and decides pass or fail can be wrong in a specific, quiet way, and the name for it is fail-open. When it meets something it does not understand, it lets it through.

Rule 9 states it — “A harness that silently ignores an unrecognized field in a status line is fail-open and invalid evidence” — and then names the incident that bought it. A calibration probe read the solver's status lines looking for symErr=. It found it, it was zero, the probe was satisfied. Sitting in the same line, unread, was deltaSymClean=false: a flag reporting a real solver defect. The probe was not lying. It had no opinion about a field it was never told to look for, and no opinion reads exactly like approval.

The fail-closed stance costs something. Refusing every unrecognised field means adding a new field breaks the harness until somebody registers it: a small, loud, immediate cost, in exchange for never having a check silently stop applying. Being friendly about unknown keys sounds harmless right up until a field is renamed, at which point the criterion that read it quietly stops being evaluated and everything stays green.

Writing your weaknesses into the alarm

Some things here are known to be wrong and cannot be fixed, because the wrongness lives in the source material rather than the code. The usual response is to leave them alone and hope nobody trips. This repository does the opposite: it writes a test asserting the broken thing stays exactly as broken as it currently is.

The clearest case is sigmaWater — how much supersaturation a supercooled water droplet presents to ice. The monograph gives it as the difference between two approximate curves, saturation over water minus saturation over ice, each fit good to about a percent. Near 0 °C the curves converge, so their difference shrinks toward nothing while their errors do not, and the percentage error blows up without bound. At −1 °C the formula returns −0.009146: a negative supersaturation, which is physically impossible. The published table says +0.010. The implementation's own comment records that it crosses zero at −1.969 °C and is negative everywhere warmer than that (core/src/libbrecht.ts, lines 47–53).

And so, in the test file, pinned to five decimal places:

expect(sigmaWater(-1)).toBeCloseTo(-0.009146, 5); // the near-melting breakdown, pinned
expect(relErr(sigmaWater(-10), 0.102)).toBeGreaterThan(0.15); // ~20% off at -10, pinned

core/test/libbrecht.test.ts, lines 73–74

Read the second line again. It asserts the code is at least 15 % wrong, so improving the formula would turn it red. That is deliberate: the limitation is pinned “so the limitation cannot silently un-happen”. The quantity is a plausibility reference against the source and is explicitly “not an enforced runtime ceiling or a dynamical input” — so the honest move is to keep it exactly as the source has it, and make any change to that an event somebody has to argue for out loud.

The same instinct produces a stranger habit: functions that refuse to answer. Ask for a parameter outside the range actually digitised from the source figure and the code throws rather than extrapolating — expect(() => sigma0Basal(-0.5)).toThrow(/outside the digitized domain/). There is a family of these: a protocol that will not hand out its frozen hash before every item is settled, and names the missing item; a solver that refuses to advance the surface on an unconverged field, even through its public interface; a grid-limit extrapolation published for the cold case and refused for the warm one, where two defensible assumptions give 0.84 and 0.49 — different habit classes — so that “Reporting either as ‘the extrapolated class’ would be inventing a result” (runner/test/phase6-protocol.test.ts, lines 349–360).

Still unsettled

Not every red test means “fix the code”. One control in this project pins a fingerprint of the machine's own exp and log only when it is running on the architecture the baseline was recorded on, and deliberately declines to assert anywhere else — because a different value on a different chip is a finding about that chip, and “turning a legitimate finding into a red test would train everyone to ignore it” (runner/test/phase6-crossplatform.test.ts, lines 3–9). Designing around alarm fatigue is a judgement call, and reasonable people would draw that line elsewhere.

Testing the enforcement, not the metric

Everything so far tests functions. There is one more layer, and it is the one people forget: a program can compute a perfect diagnostic and still be worthless, if nothing acts on it.

This project found that out by doing it. A run in the box-shaped domain — known to break symmetry, and known to break it at tick 270 — printed deltaCheckCleanAllTicks=false and exited with status 0. Success. The number was right there in the output. Nobody had to read it. The test file that fixed this opens with the sentence that ought to be on a poster:

“gate metrics nobody has to read are not a gate.”

runner/test/gate-enforce.test.ts, lines 1–4

So a set of tests now launches the real command-line program as a separate process and asserts on its exit code and its printed reason. One test title states the design goal: exit 0 must be the whole claim by itself. A slip of paper reading GATE PASSED has to mean something without the command line beside it, because the command line is what nobody keeps.

The best entry is the 18×18×12 box run. It grew from 19 sites to 37, never broke symmetry once, stopped for a legitimate reason and exited 0. It is rejected anyway, because the gate is defined on the hexagonal-prism domain and a box footprint is not a sixfold-symmetric environment — this run had simply stopped before the walls began to bite. The comment calls it what it is: “a lucky-short box run must not pass.” A gate that accepts runs which happen to look fine will accept the wrong thing on the day it matters.

Note how much of that file is about arguments rather than physics. A --noise value of NaN or -1 used to run as a silent noise-off while writing false metadata into the record; now the parser rejects it for every run, gate or not. A cadence of 0 is rejected “instead of silently disabling a check”. Half of enforcement is refusing inputs that would make the output a lie about itself.

What green proves, and who checked

Put it all together and it is worth being extremely precise about what has been established, because this is the sentence the rest of Part Two depends on.

The two columns of the claim. Left: what the test suite establishes. Right: what it cannot, by construction. Sources: AGENTS.md lines 242–256 and project charter.md §1.5.
What the tests establishWhat the tests do not establish
Mass is conserved to better than 1×10−10 over 10,000 ticks with a crystal growing That these equations are nature's equations
The symmetry error is exactly 0, structurally rather than by luck That a crystal grown here at −15 °C is what a cloud grows at −15 °C
The one case algebra can solve is reproduced to better than 1×10−9 That the physics the code was told to compute is sufficient
Every registered criterion has a saboteur that trips it, and only it Anything at all about the real world

The left column says one thing four ways: the code computes what it was told to compute. Whether that is what nature does is a different question, answered only by comparison with measurement, and no amount of green anywhere in this chapter contributes a word to it. Keeping the two apart is this project's stated reason for existing — “giving a model real physics is not the same as showing the model reproduces reality” (project charter.md §1.5) — and it is why chapter 28 reads as a result rather than a disappointment.

One more thing has to be said, because a chapter about verification that does not say who did the verifying is doing the exact thing it warns against. Every review this project has had — every gate review, every code review, and the two-reviewer process that checked this page — has been an LLM review. No human domain scientist has read any of it. The project's own standard says so: “At least one human domain scientist reads any manuscript before submission. All reviews to date, internal and external-facing, are LLM reviews; for publication that is a disclosed limitation, not a substitute.” (docs/stretch-sharing-and-investigation.md, lines 217–219.)

That is a real limit, and it is why the machinery in this chapter is shaped as it is. Rule 9 is a countermeasure for reviewers who cannot be assumed independent. Rule 10 requires every review to record which model did it, whether it shared context with the developer, what it independently re-ran, and what it did not check, as a stated limit rather than an implicit one; gate-bearing reviews prefer a different model from the one that wrote the code. None of that adds up to expert review. It adds up to a system where a stranger can recompute the verdict from the published bytes without trusting anybody in the loop, and it should be judged as exactly that.

And to be plain about status: Phase 6, the comparison with measurement, is in flight. Phase 7 has not started. Nothing in this chapter is a claim that the product exists.

Everything here compared a program against a property. The hardest case is comparing a program against another program: a fast engine on the graphics card, working in about seven decimal digits instead of sixteen and adding numbers up in whatever order its thousands of threads happen to finish, which must nonetheless be shown to agree with the slow, readable one. How wide should that envelope be — and who decides, and when?
