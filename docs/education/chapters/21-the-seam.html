Chapter 21

The Seam

The physics says the surface is moving at so many metres per second. The grid knows only “ice” and “not ice”. Which cell freezes, and when?

~18 min read 7 figures 5 interactives

By the end you will be able to

  • Describe what a computational scientist actually does: decide, defensibly, what a physical law means on a grid.
  • Explain why a timestep derived from the state is a stronger claim than a timestep somebody chose.
  • Say what a control group is for, having used one.
  • Tell a conservation law apart from an audit trail, and say why the second is sometimes the more honest promise.
  • State what this phase's gate demanded, what it got, and why the answer is recorded as a finding rather than as a bug.

Two languages, nothing in between

Chapter 19 built the lattice. Chapter 20 showed why its last bit had to be defended. Both of those were preparation. This chapter is where the physics finally arrives — and the hard part is not the physics.

The published cellular automaton this project started from attaches a cell in one move. A cell is not ice; then the rule fires; then the cell is ice — one whole cell, one tick. That is not the same as saying the rule has no memory. Chapter 19 showed you the number it keeps: every boundary cell carries a running quantity b, its boundary mass, and the cell flips the moment b passes an attachment threshold — “boundary mass needed to join the crystal”, written ggThreshBeta (docs/gg-machinery.md, line 123; the rule itself at line 237). The memory is real. What it is made of is a model-internal ledger of stuff, counting toward a threshold somebody chose. The physical law the project wants to install says something of a completely different shape. It gives a surface velocity: the ice front is advancing at so many metres per second, right here, right now, and it will keep doing that until something changes.

Those two statements do not translate into each other. A velocity is continuous and has units; a cell flip is discrete and has none. Somebody has to decide what the velocity means on the grid. The project's charter gives that job a name and refuses to file it under housekeeping:

“The seam, which is the real work of this phase: G–G's attachment is a binary cell flip; Libbrecht's v_n = alphaHK·v_kin·sigma_surf is a continuous velocity. Converting a surface velocity into lattice attachment events is the substance of LibbrechtKinetics, not an implementation detail of it.”
project charter.md, line 265 (§3.2, Phase 2b)

vn = alphaHK · vkin · σsurf

where
vn
the speed the ice surface advances, perpendicular to itself, in metres per second
alphaHK
the attachment coefficient — the fraction of molecules that hit the surface and actually stay (Part One, chapter 11)
vkin
the kinetic velocity — how fast the surface would advance if every molecule that hit it stuck
σsurf
the supersaturation right at the surface, which the diffusion field decides, not you

project charter.md, line 265 (§3.2, Phase 2b)

Notice what the charter is doing there. It is refusing an excuse in advance. If the join is “an implementation detail”, then a bad join is a coding slip and the physics stays innocent. By calling the join the substance, the charter makes it something that has to be specified, reviewed and defended like any other scientific choice. That is the whole job description of a computational scientist, in one sentence: not write down the physics, but decide, defensibly, what the physics means on a grid.

Key idea

The equation was copied from a book. The seam — how a continuous speed becomes a discrete cell flip — was invented here, and is therefore the part that can be wrong. So it gets written down first, before any code, and reviewed as physics.

How full is this cell?

Here is the answer, and it is almost embarrassingly simple once you see it. If the surface is moving at vn and you let it move for a time Δt, it has travelled vn·Δt. A cell is Δx across. So do not ask “does this cell freeze this step?”. Ask “how full is this cell now?”, keep the answer, and freeze the cell when it runs out of room.

“A surface advancing at v_n for time Δt moves forward v_n · Δt. A lattice cell of size Δx is filled when that accumulated advance reaches Δx. So accumulate a filled fraction on each boundary cell and attach when it saturates”
docs/attachment-kinetics.md, lines 134–136 (§4.2)

f(x)  +=  alphaHK · vkin · σb · Δt / (Hb · Δx)     — attach when f(x) ≥ 1

where
f(x)
the fill fraction of boundary cell x — 0 is empty, 1 is full, and full means ice
σb
the supersaturation the surface actually sees, solved so that the rate the surface demands and the rate the vapour can deliver agree
Δt
the length of this growth step, in seconds — and it is not a setting; see the next section
Δx
the lattice spacing (0.35 µm in this phase's runs)
Hb
a shape correction for the patch of surface one boundary cell stands for. For both kinds of flat face it is 1, cited straight from the monograph, so in these runs it changes nothing (docs/attachment-kinetics.md, lines 539–540). The two cases still need names, because which cell counts as which is exactly what goes wrong in the source audit at the end of this chapter; the repository writes them [01] and [20], and the figure just below draws what those labels are counting

docs/attachment-kinetics.md §4.2; forward policy in project charter.md, line 265

Those two labels are worth half a minute now, because the audit at the end of this chapter turns on them. Chapter 19 gave every cell eight neighbours: six in its own sheet, one directly above, one directly below. A boundary cell is labelled by how many of those eight are already ice — the in-plane count first, then the count above and below, the pair written together in square brackets. That is the whole notation. What makes it worth drawing is that the two flat faces of a crystal come out as counts that look nothing like each other.

The lower row is where the notation stops being arbitrary. In a triangular sheet a flat wall is a straight close-packed row, and a cell resting against that row sits in the notch between two of its cells — it touches both, never just one. Nothing is above or below it, because those two neighbours are out in the vapour alongside the wall. So a flat side face reads [20]. A flat top reads [01] for the mirror-image reason: the cell sits in the first empty layer over the plate, so nothing in its own sheet is ice yet, and its single ice neighbour is the plate surface directly beneath. Basal and prism — the two flat faces Part One kept returning to — are those two labels, and Hb is 1 for both of them, which is why it drops out of the arithmetic above.

That accumulator is the seam — and the seam is not that one rule keeps a number and the other does not. Both keep one, and the specification went out of its way to keep the two apart: f is “a separate dimensionless Float64 field” and “is NOT stored in b” (docs/attachment-kinetics.md, line 517). The reason is what the two numbers are. “b is a mass ledger with G–G dynamics (freeze/melt); f is a geometric fraction with Libbrecht dynamics” (same file, lines 519–520). b counts stuff up toward ggThreshBeta, a threshold whose value somebody picked. f counts vn·Δt — a distance in metres — against Hb·Δx, a cell width in metres. Being a ratio of two lengths, f is itself just a fraction, and that is the point: its threshold is not a chosen number at all. It is 1, because 1 is what “the surface has crossed one cell” comes to. Pouring one into the other would not be a shortcut but a physics error — adding v_n·Δt/Δx to a mass ledger “injects mass from nowhere” (same file, lines 155–156). What either accumulator buys is easiest to see against a rule that keeps nothing: with no accumulator, a surface creeping forward at three-tenths of a cell per step and one charging forward at nine-tenths produce exactly the same output — nothing — until suddenly they don't. The fraction is where the physics goes while it is too small to show up. Drag the speed slider below and watch the two rows disagree.

The bottom row is nobody's real rule. It is a rule with no memory, drawn that way to isolate one thing: something that keeps no number cannot carry a velocity. The published automaton is not that; it keeps b, and it is not wrong — it is doing what it was designed to do, and the project keeps it forever, which we will come back to. But a mass ledger counting toward a chosen threshold cannot carry a velocity either, and a model whose whole claim rests on temperature changing a velocity needs somewhere for that velocity to live, built out of the velocity itself, cell by cell. The fill fraction is that somewhere.

Who sets the clock?

The fill equation has a Δt in it. How long is one growth step allowed to be?

You can feel the danger immediately. Make Δt big and a fast cell will not just fill — it will fill several times over in one step, and the crystal will lurch forward in chunks that no physics chose. There is a standard idea for this in numerical work, usually written CFL after the three mathematicians who wrote it down: never let anything cross more than a fraction of a cell in one step. This project applies it to filling rather than to moving, and calls it the fill-CFL.

The obvious way to obey a rule like that is to pick a small Δt and hope. This project does the opposite. It computes the fill rate of every boundary cell, finds the largest one, and then solves for the timestep that makes that fastest cell fill by exactly the allowed fraction:

Δt = cfl / max(rate)

where
cfl
the fill-CFL bound — the most any cell is allowed to fill in one step. Default 0.1, meaning 10% of a cell
rate
a cell's fill rate, alphaHK·vkin·σb / (Hb·Δx), in cells per second
max(rate)
the largest of those, over every boundary cell, recomputed every step

docs/attachment-kinetics.md, lines 682–683; implemented at solver-cpu/src/lk-solver.ts:1690 — deltaTime = this.cflFill / maxRate;

Read what that buys. If the timestep were a setting, then “no cell filled by more than 10%” would be a claim you check afterwards and hope holds. Because the timestep is computed from the state, the claim is the definition. The specification says so in exactly those words — Δt is “a deterministic function of the state, so slow-kinetics regimes advance in wall-clock-feasible step counts while the bound holds exactly by construction” (docs/attachment-kinetics.md, lines 685–687). It is also the only place in the whole physics rule where physical time enters at all.

There is a detail here that is easy to miss and is worth more than it looks. The crystal also gains cells a second way: a purely geometric hygiene rule closes interior voids left by the discretization — it is “geometric hygiene, not physics” (docs/gg-machinery.md, lines 245–246) — and it snaps a fill fraction from wherever it was straight to 1. That is a jump far bigger than the CFL bound. It would have been very easy to fold those jumps into the maximum, or to quietly leave them out. The code does neither, on the record:

“hole-filling jumps f → 1 are geometric events OUTSIDE the CFL claim and must never be absorbed into — or censored out of — the kinetic maximum; they are counted and deficit-ledgered separately, and the gate reads both.”
docs/attachment-kinetics.md, lines 688–690 — recorded as a correction made the same day the adaptive timestep was implemented, in response to a review blocker

How we know

The bound is not measured after the run and reported. It is the definition of the timestep, so it cannot fail without the arithmetic failing. And the one number it depends on is itself defended: the solver's constructor rejects any fill-CFL outside (0, 1) before a single step executes — throw new Error("cflFill must be finite and in (0, 1)"), solver-cpu/src/lk-solver.ts:451–452. You cannot ask this program for a step that advances the surface by a whole cell. It will not start.

Where temperature stops being a label

Now the payoff, and it is the reason this chapter cannot come any later in the book.

Look back at the fill equation. alphaHK is the attachment coefficient, and Part One's chapter 11 already told you what it depends on: which facet you are on, how supersaturated the air is right there, and the temperature. So temperature is inside the rate. It is inside the rate of every single boundary cell, recomputed every step, from the local supersaturation the diffusion field happens to deliver to that cell. Nobody types in a shape.

“temperature is an input to the physics, not a label applied afterward. α_basal(T, σ_surf) and α_prism(T, σ_surf) are computed each step from the local supersaturation the diffusion field delivers, and the plate↔column habit is an output of their competition rather than a knob. This is what allows the model to be wrong — and therefore worth testing”
project charter.md, line 165 (§2.5)

Compare that with the rule it replaced. Under the borrowed threshold rule, whether a cell attaches depends on a set of numbers — the project writes two of them ggThreshBeta01 and ggThreshBeta20 — that you choose before the run. The project's own notes are blunt about what those numbers are: “β₀₁ / β₂₀ is the plate↔column axis under GGThreshold. High β₀₁ ⇒ basal faces attach reluctantly ⇒ growth goes sideways ⇒ plate” — and then, four lines later, “Under LibbrechtKinetics the habit is not a knob at all — it is an output of the α_basal/α_prism competition at a given temperature” (docs/gg-machinery.md, lines 143–149).

The two rules live behind one shared interface, and the project keeps both, permanently. Swap the head on the panel below and watch what happens to the controls.

That duplication is deliberate, and it costs real work: the two solvers share the lattice and the stencil but do not share their mutable machinery code. The interface file says why in one clause — “the control-group differential (charter §3.3) is worth the duplication — but they speak the same operator language” (solver-cpu/src/operator.ts:1–9).

A control group is the thing you did not change, kept around so you can tell what the change did. Here is what it is for, in the project's own words:

“When LibbrechtKinetics produces something strange, the first question is always: does GGThreshold still produce these four, on this same machinery, from this same seed? If yes, the bug is in the physics. If no, the bug is underneath it and the physics is innocent. That differential is the entire reason both rules are kept forever.”
docs/gg-machinery.md, lines 420–424

That is a question you can now put to the panel. Switch it to LibbrechtKinetics and press check the control: it reports the permanent GGThreshold run, and what matters is not its shape but that its checkpoint hash has not moved — the same one Phase 2a accepted, Phase 3 re-derived byte-identically, and Phase 2b's gate row records the control still returning (docs/PROGRESS.md, lines 610–612). Because the control did not move, anything odd under the physics rule is the physics' problem. That is the entire value of keeping a run you already know the answer to.

Change one thing

So what did this phase have to prove? One sentence, written before the work started: habit changes with temperature alone. Two temperatures, nothing else different, two different shapes. If temperature is genuinely inside the physics, that should be possible. If temperature is secretly still a label on a knob, it will not be.

“Nothing else different” is doing an enormous amount of work in that sentence, and it is where the discipline lives. The panel below is the comparison the gate demanded. Every control except one is locked. Try to break it.

The run happened once, from a commit with no uncommitted tracked changes, with no command-line flags that could steer it, and it finished with status 0 — the number a program returns when every check it was told to enforce passed. Both runs used the same 96×96×96 hexagonal-prism domain and the same extent target. Extent is the crystal's largest span in any direction — across the plate, or up the column, whichever is bigger — counted in cells. In code that is literally what it says: largestExtent: Math.max(tExtent, zExtent), where tExtent is the sideways span and zExtent the vertical one (core/src/metrics.ts:170–176). The run stops at the first growth step whose extent reaches the registered target of 60, which is why both runs record 61 rather than exactly 60. Same far field — the outer wall of the box, held at a fixed supersaturation — at the same σ∞ = 0.002; same 101325 Pa, same 0.35 µm lattice spacing, same CAK_A1 nucleation parameter set (one of the code's two named settings of the CAK curves, and the subject of the next paragraph), seed 1, noise 0, fill-CFL 0.1, and the same convergence tolerances (docs/plans/phase-2b-v5p-parallel-retry.md, lines 47–51). Temperature alone differed.

One item on that list is worth stopping on, because it comes back at the very end of the book. The code offers exactly two named settings of the CAK nucleation curves — the curves Part One's chapter 11 introduced and chapter 16 had you digitize — and no others: CAK, which uses the digitized prism prefactor Aprism, the dipping curve read off the plot; and CAK_A1, which holds Aprism at exactly 1 at every temperature. The second is a simplification, and the code says so where it is defined — “a documented SIMPLIFICATION of the CAK set” (core/src/libbrecht.ts, lines 90–102 and 142–143). Phase 2b registered CAK_A1 in advance, ran it, and reported it, which is the whole of what a registered choice has to do. Chapter 29 is about a later phase that registered the other one and then ran this one without noticing.

The definition of extent earns its keep in the table below, where the two runs record the same extent — 61 — and it is not the same measurement twice. At −5 °C the aspect ratio is far below 1, so that crystal is very much wider than it is tall, and its 61 is a sideways span. At −15 °C the aspect ratio is 12.2000, which is 61 divided by 5: there the 61 is the height, and 5 is the width it was measured against. One number, two completely different objects — which is about the clearest illustration available that the two runs made different shapes.

The Phase 2b result, transcribed from docs/PROGRESS.md, line 611. Aspect ratio is height divided by width, measured at that size; below 1 is a plate, above 1 is a column. Its width is measured on a different footing from extent — a Cartesian across-flats denominator, “deliberately distinct” from the integer lattice spans extent uses (core/src/metrics.ts, lines 146–148 and 276–280) — so the extent column and the width in the aspect ratio are not the same number.

Both runs hit the size target, both reported symmetry error 0, every relaxation converged — a relaxation is the repeated sweep over the grid that solves the vapour field, and chapter 22 is about how anyone decides when one has finished — and both checkpoints round-tripped identically. The gate closed on 2026-07-20.

Now read that table again, carefully, next to what Part One's chapter 7 told you about the real world. Around −5 °C nature makes columns and needles. Around −15 °C nature makes plates. The model produced a plate at −5 °C and a column at −15 °C. The habit changed with temperature alone — and it changed in the opposite sense from the published diagram.

Before deciding what that means, notice what was not in the model when it happened. The frozen protocol lists, among the things held fixed, “no events, SDAK, or ramps” (docs/plans/phase-2b-v5p-parallel-retry.md, line 51), and the project's own record calls this “Phase 2b's scoped no-SDAK deliverable” (docs/PROGRESS.md, line 523). SDAK — the narrow-facet mechanism chapter 12 offered and chapter 13 left standing as a working hypothesis rather than a settled result — is not in this model at all. So the flip is not evidence that attachment kinetics is simply backwards. It is evidence that broad-facet attachment kinetics on its own, with no narrow-facet term anywhere in the rule, flips the wrong way. Those are different claims, and later chapters need the second one.

Careful

It is tempting to call that either a triumph or a bug. It is neither, and the difference matters. This gate asked whether habit responds to temperature at all when nothing else moves, in a model carrying broad-facet attachment kinetics and nothing else. It did not ask whether it responds correctly, it had no authority to ask that, and the answer to the question it did ask is yes. What the run shows is that the model was given temperature as a real physical input. It does not show that the model reproduces reality — that is a different claim, it needs a different experiment, and later chapters are entirely about how that experiment was set up and marked. Hold the wrong-sense flip as an early warning about broad-facet kinetics on its own. What a narrow-facet term would have done to it is not tested here, because there was not one in the model.

Still unsettled

Two temperatures is not a diagram. All this phase established is that habit moves when temperature moves, at two points, with everything else nailed down. Whether the model reproduces the pattern nature shows across the whole temperature range is the question the validation phase exists to answer — and that phase is not finished. In the project's own gate table, Phase 6 is marked in progress and Phase 7, the product layer, is marked not started (docs/PROGRESS.md, lines 615–616). The chapters ahead report what the validation sweep found so far. They do not report a finished project.

A conservation law and an audit trail

The reference rule makes a beautiful promise. Vapour spreads by local averaging, walls reflect, nothing is created and nothing escapes, so the total of everything on the grid is an invariant — exact in arithmetic, and, as chapter 19 was careful to say, tested against a measured float floor rather than against zero. It is the cleanest kind of test there is: not “does the picture look right” but “does this number stay where arithmetic says it must”.

The physics rule cannot make that promise, and the honest move is to say so rather than to find a form of words that sounds like it. The field under this rule is not a stock of stuff at all. It is a potential — a description of the balance the vapour has already settled into, with a boundary far away that supplies as much as the crystal can take. Asking it to conserve mass is a category error, and the specification says so flatly: “Anyone expecting the Phase 2a mass invariant here has mixed up the rules: G–G's invariant is a reflecting-boundary property of a mass field; this rule's field is a quasi-static potential” (docs/attachment-kinetics.md, lines 605–606).

So it makes a different promise, and the promise is still exact. Not “nothing left the system”, but “everything that left was written down”:

“the bookkeeping identity is placed fill ledger + saturation-clipped fill = that demand, exact — when a cell saturates (f hits 1 mid-increment) the excess demand is recorded in saturationClippedFill, never silently dropped”
docs/attachment-kinetics.md, lines 561–564 (§4.4, component 4)

The situation it describes is ordinary. A cell has 0.04 of a cell of room left; this step's physics demands 0.09; the cell takes the 0.04 it can hold and becomes ice. Where does the other 0.05 go? The first version of the code let it go nowhere. It simply did not appear in the books. An audit round measured the size of that hole at 35% of the demand on an ordinary saturating step. (The audit's own probe script was not kept — the specification labels it a historical audit finding for exactly that reason — but the regression test that closed it is in the suite.) The repair is two lines, and the comment on the second one is the whole lesson:

this.fillLedger += room;
this.saturationClippedFill += raw - room; // round-3 blocker 2: never silently drop

solver-cpu/src/lk-solver.ts:1696–1697

Two things about that demo are load-bearing. First, the size of the hole depends on how often cells saturate, which depends on the timestep — so a numerical choice made for stability quietly changes how much a bookkeeping bug costs you. Second, the “recompute from outside” button is not decoration. The project's real test does exactly that: it “recomputes the integral outside the solver—from the converged public field, public neighbor topology, core's alphaHK, and the recorded aggregate geometry—across many steps including saturating ones, and asserts it equals ledger-delta + clipped-delta” (docs/attachment-kinetics.md, lines 570–573). A solver that grades its own homework has proved nothing.

The same treatment is given to the growth the hole-filling rule hands out for free, which no vapour paid for. It is not netted away against anything. It gets its own line: holeFillDeficit, documented in the interface as “fill granted by hole-filling without vapor withdrawal — reported, never hidden” (solver-cpu/src/operator.ts:83–84).

Both rules are required to state, in code, exactly what they are promising. The strings below are what each one returns when asked.

Key idea

A conservation law says nothing left the system. An audit trail says everything that left was written down. When you cannot honestly promise the first, the second is not a weaker substitute — it is the promise that happens to be true, and it can be tested just as exactly.

What the code refuses to do

There is a habit in this codebase that is worth naming, because it is the thing that makes a later negative result believable. Wherever a shortcut was taken, the shortcut is labelled in place, its direction of error is stated, and the reason it was not fixed is given.

Here is one, sitting in the middle of the growth loop. The solver needs the rate at which the crystal is gaining volume. What it actually computes is the volume the physics demanded, not the volume the crystal got. Those differ, and the comment does not leave the reader to discover it:

“REGISTERED APPROXIMATION: this is the Hertz-Knudsen KINETIC DEMAND summed over boundary pixels, which is not identical to the volume the crystal actually gains this step. Two known differences, both bounded and both in the same direction”
solver-cpu/src/lk-solver.ts:1662–1664 — the two differences are then listed: clipped fill, and hole-filling

Elsewhere a genuine edge case in the boundary rule is documented rather than repaired. The stated reason is not “it's fine”. It is a cost argument, made in public: any repair “would change the boundary operator and therefore every executed Phase 2b/4/5 checkpoint. That is ADR-level, and it would be spending a re-derivation of accepted evidence on a branch no valid run takes” (solver-cpu/src/lk-solver.ts:1080–1083). “ADR-level” means it would need a numbered Architecture Decision Record — the machinery chapter 18 described — rather than a code change. Repairing it would change results already recorded and accepted, in order to improve a path that a separate guard already stops any valid run from reaching.

And in several places the program's answer to a question is simply to stop. Ask the nucleation parameters for a temperature outside the range the published data covers and the module throws instead of extrapolating — “extrapolating a fit outside its range silently manufactures a fake result”, core/src/libbrecht.ts:80–81, with the error message naming the banned operation outright: extrapolation is banned (core/src/libbrecht.ts:106–108). The solver's own constructor carries the same wall: throw new Error("tempC must stay in the digitized domain [-50, -1]") (solver-cpu/src/lk-solver.ts:442–443).

The most useful way to read code like this is not line by line. It is to ask what each piece refuses to do. This one refuses to invent a number outside its evidence, refuses to start with a timestep that could jump a whole cell, refuses to drop a remainder it could not place, and refuses to improve itself in a way that would quietly rewrite results already published.

Three protocols on the record

None of this worked the first time. Three protocols were written, frozen and run for this one gate, and all three are still in the repository.

Protocol v3 ran, and failed. It “completed according to its registered controls and FAILED its habit gate: both registered temperatures reached the same one-layer plate at the measurement size” (docs/PROGRESS.md, lines 524–526). Both temperatures produced an aspect ratio of 0.01888163179957996 — identical to the last digit. The −5 °C plate criterion passed; the −15 °C column criterion, which needed at least 1.5, failed on the same number. Every other criterion passed: exact symmetry, all relaxations converged, maximum kinetic fill 0.1 exactly as the CFL says it must be.

A source audit afterwards found why the seam had been blunt. The monograph names one boundary configuration as basal and another as the prism facet; the executed rule had classified them differently — it read the flat side wall, [20] in the notation above, as a rough step site rather than a prism facet — so a temperature-independent path controlled twelve of the seed's own starting sites. The record does not treat that as a licence to delete the run: “This source contradiction is a seam defect discovered after the run, not a reason to rewrite or discard the negative result; it limits interpretation of the failure as a test of the intended physical model” (docs/PROGRESS.md, lines 1643–1646).

Protocol v4 did not fail. It terminated. The warm run finished normally. The cold run stopped at growth step 12. Solving the vapour field means sweeping over the grid again and again until the numbers settle; this attempt used all 200,000 sweeps it was allowed. Two separate tests have to pass before the field counts as solved. The first asks whether it has stopped moving — how much the numbers still change from one sweep to the next — and it read exactly zero. The second asks whether the answer it stopped at balances: does the vapour flowing in at the far wall match the vapour the crystal surface is taking out? That one stuck at 3.10e-7, against a registered limit of 1e-7. Nothing was changing and the books still did not balance. Exit status 1. And then this line, which is the whole discipline in one clause: its aspect ratio of 0.6 “is below the registered measurement size and has no habit meaning” (docs/plans/phase-2b-v4-convergence-failure-and-v5.md, lines 38–42). The run produced a number. The protocol refused to let the number mean anything.

Diagnosing it meant decoding the immutable checkpoint with a separate script and rebuilding the whole step from the raw arrays, independently of the solver. The answer had stopped changing while the books still did not balance because of floating-point roundoff in the smoothing stencil — chapter 20's problem again. “The plateau is therefore local float64 stencil roundoff, not naïve total accumulation, incomplete relaxation, or a physical imbalance” (same file, line 94). Because the governing identity had only two terms and this was a third, the fix could not be a patch — it needed a numbered decision record and a new protocol version. v4's bytes stay immutable.

Protocol v5 passed — run once, flagless, with the two temperatures executed as two concurrent processes rather than one after the other (the “v5p” retry, after the sequential attempt was interrupted by a host shutdown mid-run; docs/PROGRESS.md, lines 247–255) — and produced the table in “Change one thing”.

How we know — and who checked

This phase went through six audit rounds, and the repaired code was signed off by an independent adversarial review with zero unresolved findings before the final run was allowed to execute. One disclosure belongs here rather than in a footnote: every review in this project to date — those audit rounds, the adversarial review, and the two-reviewer process that checked this page — has been a review by a language model. The project treats that as a stated limitation of its evidence, not as a substitute for review by a human domain scientist, of which there has been none.

Three protocols, three outcomes: a recorded negative result, an execution-invalid termination, and a pass. All three are still readable, with their hashes, in the same repository. That is not tidiness. It is what makes the third one worth anything — anybody can check that the pass was not just the version whose question had been softened until the answer flattered it.

Notice what actually stopped protocol v4. Not a crash, and not a wrong-looking crystal: a vapour field whose numbers had completely stopped changing while its books still did not balance. Chapter 22 is about why those two tests are not the same question — how does anyone know when a computer has finished solving something? — and shows what happened the first time this project trusted the obvious answer.
