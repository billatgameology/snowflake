Chapter 7

Plates, Columns, Plates, Columns

Why does cooling the air flip a snow crystal between flat and tall — three times?

~30 min read 22 figures 5 animations

By the end you will be able to

  • Say what a crystal's habit is, and reduce it to a single number.
  • State the aspect-ratio rule, and use it to read the physics of an ice surface off a photograph.
  • Explain why diffusion on its own can never build a thin plate or a slender needle.
  • Read the morphology diagram in both directions — conditions to shape, and shape back to conditions.
  • Locate the four shape bands and the temperatures where the shape flips.
  • Say which parts of this map independent laboratories have confirmed, and which parts they have rewritten.
  • Explain a hollow column and a needle as the same branching instability, turned sideways.
  • State precisely what the diagram does not explain, and why that has stayed open since the 1930s.

So far this site has built one engine with two parts. Water vapour has to reach the crystal, and diffusion decides how much arrives where — that is the fuel. Molecules then have to attach, and the ice surface decides how readily they do — that is the throttle. Between them they gave us faceting, and then branching.

That engine is enough to explain a great deal. It is not enough to explain the single most obvious thing about snow crystals: some of them are flat as paper, and some of them are shaped like pencils. This chapter is where that becomes a problem.

One number for the whole shape

A crystal's habit is its overall shape, ignoring the decoration — plate, block, column, needle. Two snow crystals can carry wildly different ornament and still share a habit, and habit is what the rest of this chapter is about.

Habit sounds like a vague, hand-waving word. It is not. Every snow crystal is built on a hexagonal prism, and a hexagonal prism carries just the two families of facet you named back in Chapter 1: the two basal caps and the six prism sides. Chapter 3 already showed you the losing candidates — the {112̄0} secondary prism planes, on which ice is not observed to facet at all, and the pyramidal facets, which turn up only very rarely. For habit, these two families are the whole story. So the whole shape collapses to one number — how far the crystal reaches from cap to cap, divided by how far it reaches across.

aspect ratio = c ÷ a

where
c
the crystal's overall size along the c-axis — cap to cap, straight through the two basal facets
a
the crystal's overall size along an a-axis — straight across the hexagon, corner to corner

Definition as given in Libbrecht, Snow Crystals, printed p. 111

An aspect ratio below 1 means flatter than it is wide. Above 1 means taller than it is wide. And the range that real snow crystals cover is startling. A large thin plate can get down to about 0.01 — a hundred times wider than it is thick. Libbrecht's phrasing is hard to improve on: that extreme aspect ratio “is what puts the ‘flake’ in ‘snowflake.’” At the other end, an especially slender needle can reach 20 or more.

Key idea

Every snow crystal habit — plate, block, column, needle — is one number: how tall it is divided by how wide it is. Real crystals span that number from about 0.01 to about 20.

The aspect-ratio rule

Here is the step that turns a photograph into physics. A growing crystal ends up bounded by its slowest faces. A fast face runs outward until it meets its neighbours and vanishes from the outline; a slow face just sits there, getting wider. So a crystal that ended up thin must have had lazy caps, and a crystal that ended up tall must have had lazy sides.

Chapter 5 gave a name to how readily molecules stick to a given facet: the attachment coefficient, written alphaHK in this project (0 to 1). Small alphaHK means a stubborn facet. Put the two ideas together and you get a rule that Libbrecht says “applies throughout the menagerie of different snow-crystal types”:

thin plates ⇒ alphaHKbasal ≪ alphaHKprism
slender columns ⇒ alphaHKbasal ≫ alphaHKprism

where
alphaHKbasal
how readily a molecule sticks to the flat top or bottom cap
alphaHKprism
how readily a molecule sticks to one of the six sides
≪
“very much less than” — not merely smaller, but smaller by a lot
≫
“very much greater than”

Libbrecht, Snow Crystals, printed p. 111: “the formation of thin plates invariably requires αbasal ≪ αprism, while the formation of slender columns requires αbasal ≫ αprism.”

Read that backwards and it becomes a measuring instrument. You do not need a laboratory to find out that the caps of a stellar dendrite are far harder to grow on than its edges. You need a photograph of one. Libbrecht runs exactly that argument on a stellar dendrite: the rounded branch tips say alphaHKprism is close to 1 there, and the extreme flatness says alphaHKbasal is below 0.01. As he puts it, “just looking at a large stellar dendrite with rounded branch tips reveals that αprism ≈ 1 at the tips and αbasal < 0.01.”

One more thing the rule is careful about. It says alphaHKbasal ≪ alphaHKprism, with the “very much less than” symbol, not simply “less than”. A small imbalance gives you a slightly squat or slightly stretched prism. An aspect ratio of 0.01 needs a whopping imbalance. Extreme shapes are evidence of extreme differences between the two faces.

Why diffusion can never make a plate

You might reasonably object that we already have a mechanism for making dramatic shapes. Chapter 6 built the branching instability: a bump that sticks further into the moist air collects more vapour, so it grows faster, so it sticks out further. That runaway is what turns a plain hexagon into a six-armed star. Why can it not also flatten a crystal, or stretch it?

Because branching and stretching are different jobs, and diffusion is only good at one of them. Diffusion makes structures intricate. Left to itself it does not make them lopsided. Take a thin plate and remove the imbalance between its two faces — set alphaHKbasal and alphaHKprism equal — and the plate does not stay thin. Its edges thicken, and the whole thing drifts back toward being as tall as it is wide, even while it keeps sprouting elaborate branches.

How we know

This is not a hunch; it is what happens when you actually solve the growth equations with the two faces made equal. Libbrecht runs exactly that experiment on paper, on printed p. 112: “If, for example, one begins with a thin plate crystal and then changes parameters so that αbasal ≈ αprism, then the subsequent growth will not maintain the thin-plate structure. Instead the edges of the plate would thicken over time and the overall aspect ratio would tend toward unity.” Take away the imbalance and the plate un-flattens itself. The general statement is on p. 111: “diffusion effects alone cannot yield structures with extreme aspect ratios, like thin plates or slender columns.”

The other candidate explanation gets ruled out in the same breath. Surface energy — the tendency of a surface to minimise its own area — can favour some facets over others in principle. In ice it is far too even-handed to matter here. Highly lopsided surface energies, Libbrecht writes, “are mostly a theoretical fiction (except perhaps in exotic materials).” A more recent paper of his (arXiv:2306.13087v1, p. 4) puts both eliminations in a single sentence: “Neither diffusion-limited growth nor surface-energy effects produce growing snow crystals with high overall aspect ratios.”

So there is exactly one place left for an extreme shape to come from: a large difference between how easily molecules attach to the caps and how easily they attach to the sides. The habit of a snow crystal is a report on its own surface chemistry.

Which raises the obvious question: what does the cloud do to that surface? The answer, and it is the whole reason this chapter exists, is that a cloud has only two knobs to turn. The first is temperature.

The second axis: how wet the air is

The second knob is supersaturation — how much spare water vapour the air is carrying beyond what it can hold in balance with ice. The two knobs do different jobs, and keeping them separate is the key to reading everything that follows.

Temperature decides plate or column. Supersaturation decides simple or complicated. At low supersaturation, crystals are blocky and fully faceted — plain hexagonal columns, stubby plates, nothing dramatic. Turn the supply up and you cross into branching: first six primary branches with little decoration, and only at the highest levels, side branches sprouting off the branches. Libbrecht describes exactly that progression from his own laboratory series on printed p. 301: “As the supersaturation increases, branching often begins with six primary branches with little or no sidebranching. Sidebranching eventually develops at the highest supersaturations shown in Figure 8.16, although some temperatures are more prone to copious sidebranching than others.”

The Figure 8.16 named in that quotation is worth knowing about. It is the same two-knob map we are building, made entirely out of photographs of real laboratory crystals rather than drawings. No single page can hold it: its caption, on printed p. 301, opens by warning you that the figure is “(Following five pages) The snow crystal morphology diagram depicted by crystals growing on the ends of slender ice needles, as a function of temperature and supersaturation.” Read one of its columns upward and the crystals get more elaborate; read one of its rows across and they change kind. Those are the two sentences this chapter is arguing about.

Worth a beat of its own: every crystal in that grid grew seeded on the tip of a c-axis ice needle, not free-falling and not resting on an ordinary flat substrate. Libbrecht’s dedicated paper on the technique explains why that seed shape is deliberate rather than incidental. A small prism seed carries two basal faces, and growing a crystal from both at once risks the “double-plate” problem, where the two sides “interfere with one another’s growth, often yielding split plates and split stars”; a needle tip avoids this outright, because it “presents only a single basal facet” (arXiv:2109.00098v1, pp. 9–10). The same paper gives the practical reason no ordinary substrate could have supplied a grid this wide: an open substrate suffers “the rapid nucleation of water droplets” once the near-surface supersaturation climbs past the same ceiling this chapter names a few paragraphs below, and “water droplets strongly alter the supersaturation boundary conditions surrounding the crystal, making it quite difficult to examine ice growth behaviors” there (arXiv:2109.00098v1, p. 9). Grow the crystal on a needle tip instead and the limit disappears — which is exactly why this grid could be built at all.

The upward reading is the one you can watch happen. Below is a single crystal, photographed three times while the applied supersaturation was raised. You met it in Chapter 6, where it was evidence about branching. Here it is evidence about an axis.

Careful

“Colder weather makes fancier snowflakes” is a natural guess and it is wrong. Elaboration tracks the moisture supply, not the thermometer. In dry air you get plain blocky crystals — squat prisms, stubby plates or short columns depending on the temperature — however far below freezing you go; in a wet cloud near −15 °C you get ferns. Temperature mainly changes which shape you get; supersaturation is what decides how ornate it becomes. Not entirely, though: as Libbrecht says of his own laboratory series, “some temperatures are more prone to copious sidebranching than others.”

There is a ceiling on that second axis, and it is a real physical one rather than a drafting choice. Push the humidity up inside a cloud and eventually the air becomes saturated with respect to liquid water, and droplets start condensing out of it. From then on the droplets take up the surplus, so the supersaturation around the crystal does not climb much further. That ceiling is drawn on every version of the diagram as the water saturation line. Above it you are no longer describing weather; you are describing a laboratory.

The map from weather to shape

Two knobs, two axes. Put temperature along the bottom and spare water vapour up the side, and every kind of snow crystal turns out to occupy its own territory. That chart is the snow crystal morphology diagram, universally called the Nakaya diagram.

Ukichiro Nakaya built it in the 1930s in a walk-in freezer laboratory at Hokkaido, by growing snow crystals in a chamber and varying the conditions on purpose. It took years. The monograph's account is deadpan (printed p. 31): Nakaya “spent years examining how his synthetic snow crystals grew and developed as he varied the temperature and supersaturation within his growth chamber, soon combining all his observations into what is now called the snow crystal morphology diagram, or the Nakaya diagram.”

The diagram was recognised immediately for what it was. In Libbrecht's words (printed p. 31) it “was immediately recognized as being like a Rosetta Stone for snowflakes. With it, one can translate the shape of a falling snow crystal into a description of its growth history. Upon seeing a slender needle crystal, for example, one can deduce that it must have grown in high humidity at a temperature near -5 C.” Nakaya himself, on printed p. 32, liked to say that snowflakes are “hieroglyphs from the sky.”

How to read the matrices

TAX1 calls its 97 photographs qualitative snapshots. It does not print a time or physical scale for each cell. TAX2 adds those two labels to every occupied tile and reports temperature uncertainty typically ±0.2 °C and far-field supersaturation between 0.8 and 1.2 times the stated value; that supply is inferred from a chamber model, not read by a probe next to the crystal (arXiv:2306.13087v1, pp. 7–8).

Both surveys used a single basal facet on a slender c-axis needle. That makes a clean, reproducible seed, but the needle changes the diffusion field and often makes the upper and lower basal surfaces grow differently. TAX2 also says the displayed panels were subjectively selected, with preference for isolated, symmetric, well-formed examples. They are excellent named tests for a model; they are not a census of how often each form occurs, and each transmitted-light picture is a two-dimensional projection of a three-dimensional crystal. Both papers say only normal air, with no numerical pressure printed for these matrices.

Careful

The diagram's own fine print — the caption Libbrecht gives this same chart when it is reprinted later in the book, on printed p. 383 — is worth reading before anyone over-trusts it: “this chart provides just a rough approximation of the different morphologies, plus it applies only if the growth conditions are constant in time.” Real crystals fall through changing air. The map tells you what grows in a steady environment; a crystal that visited two environments will carry two answers at once.

Plates, columns, plates, columns

Now do the thing the animation asked you to do. Pick a moisture level, hold it, and cool the air steadily from freezing point downward. Here is what nature does.

Just below 0 °C: plates. Cool a couple of degrees further and the crystals flip to columns — pencils and needles. Keep cooling and around ten degrees below freezing they flip back to plates, and this is the band that produces the great flat stars of the holiday cards. Keep going, past twenty degrees below, and they turn back toward columns again — though this last band is the untidy one. Its printed label is Columns and Plates. Both habits turn up in it, and the published caption of the diagram those boundaries come from does not end the sequence at the boundary; it ends it at “predominantly columns (T < -30 C)”. Columns only clearly take over about nine degrees further down.

Flat, tall, flat, tall. Four bands, three switch points, from monotonously turning one knob in one direction.

How we know

The three temperatures in that table are not quoted from a sentence; they were read off a published figure. A 300 dpi render of arXiv:1211.5555v1 Figure 1 was measured, the three dashed boundary lines located, and the temperature axis calibrated tick by tick — which mattered, because that axis is drawn non-uniformly and shrinks by a factor of 1.77 from the warm end to the cold end. Calibrating it with a single straight fit misplaces every boundary. The stated read uncertainty is ±0.5 °C. That covers the width of the printed lines and the calibration; it does not cover the fact that the real transitions are fuzzy bands rather than lines. Other sources put the two warm boundaries a degree or two elsewhere; the coldest one is barely pinned at all, as you will see a few paragraphs below, and again when two other laboratories get their say.

The switches are sharp. Libbrecht, on printed p. 32: “Even a change of a few degrees can dramatically alter its growth behavior.” Two degrees can be the difference between a pencil and a wafer.

Two obvious worries. Did Nakaya simply mis-draw his own boundaries? And is this an artefact of one freezer in one building in Hokkaido?

That free-fall chamber is worth staying with, because a few years later the same idea — let go of the crystal — was turned on the band boundaries themselves. Takahashi, Endoh, Wakahama & Fukuta, “Vapor Diffusional Growth of Free-Falling Snow Crystals between −3 and −23 °C” (J. Meteor. Soc. Japan 69, 15–30, 1991), grew single crystals in free fall inside a supercooled cloud tunnel: no substrate under the crystal, no fibre through it, and only one crystal in the tunnel at a time, so nothing else nearby was drinking the vapour. Each one grew for between 3 and 30 minutes at water saturation, at ten temperatures from −3.7 to −22.0 °C, and reached the sizes you would recognise on your sleeve — 4 mm dendrites, 2 mm needles, after half an hour.

Stop and notice what that rules out. Every boundary number this chapter has printed so far traces back to a crystal that was being held: seeded on the tip of an ice needle, resting on a substrate, or strung on a filament. A held crystal shares its air with whatever is holding it. Takahashi et al. let go. Different apparatus, different laboratory, different decade — and here is what came out.

That is precisely the size of the wobble the “How we know” note under the table warned you about — other sources putting the warm boundaries “a degree or two elsewhere” — and here is one of those other sources, holding its own instrument.

Key idea

Crystals falling freely through a cloud tunnel, with nothing touching them, put the three switch points within about a degree of the ones this chapter reads off a drawn diagram. So plates–columns–plates–columns is not a habit of any one laboratory’s apparatus, and not an artefact of chamber walls or seed needles or substrates. It is a property of ice.

One caveat before you bank the cold end, though, because it is the sort of thing a careful reader should catch. The coldest crystal in that tunnel grew at −22.0 °C, and the boundary they report is −22.4 °C. The number sits just past the edge of their own sampled range. The two warm switches are bracketed from both sides; the cold one is a step off the last measured point. That is a large part of why this chapter keeps calling it barely pinned.

The same survey left behind two numbers that say something the band labels cannot. The first is how extreme the habits get. Takahashi et al. report the axial ratio a ÷ c — note the reciprocal of this chapter’s aspect ratio, so it runs large for a plate — after ten minutes of growth, and across their temperature range it spans a factor of about a thousand. The two extremes sit near −14.8 °C at the flat end and −5.8 °C at the tall end. Nine degrees of thermometer; three orders of magnitude of shape.

The second is where the crystals got big. Plot the mass of the crystal against the temperature it grew at and you do not get one smooth hill. You get two sharp maxima, one near −5.8 °C and one near −14.8 °C — the needles and the dendrites. Now compare those with the two temperatures in the paragraph above: they are the same pair. The places where the crystal is most lopsided are also the places where it grows fastest. Whatever the surface is doing at −5 and −15 °C, it is not merely choosing a direction. It is opening a throttle. That is a strong hint about mechanism, and it is worth carrying as far as Chapter 12.

And one last finding, which matters for how you read every boundary on this page. At the crossover temperatures the crystals were not plates or columns at all. Takahashi et al. find near-isometric forms — about as tall as they are wide, the blocky prisms — above −4.4 °C, between −6.4 and −11.3 °C, and below −18.4 °C. Lay those three windows over the table and each one straddles a switch point. The bands do not jump. They blend through blocky — which is the measured version of a caveat this chapter has so far only asserted in words: a boundary is the middle of a blur, not a line drawn inside it.

The later research literature states the sequence more precisely than the original chart does — and in doing so, admits how loose the cold end really is. Libbrecht, opening a 2020 paper: the diagram “illustrates a complex series of morphological transitions between thin platelike structures (appearing in air at temperatures near -14 C and again above -3 C) and slender columnar forms (near -4 C and again below -30 C).” Elsewhere he sets the sequence out as ranges rather than lines: growth in air is “platelike above -3 C, columnar from -4 C to -6 C, platelike again from -11 C to -18 C, and columnar again below -30 C” (arXiv:2012.12916v1, p. 1). Count what is missing there. The stretch from about −18 to −30 °C — twelve degrees, straddling the boundary our table prints at −21.5 — is simply left unassigned. That is a more honest picture of the coldest switch than any single number. And the cold end of the map has since been pushed much further out — Bailey and Hallett “extended these results with additional observations exploring temperatures down to -70 C.”

Other laboratories, other answers

That last clause deserves better than a clause. What Bailey and Hallett found down at the cold end does not extend the diagram; it contradicts it. And a third laboratory, working up in the warm half where everybody agrees, found something arguably more awkward still: two crystals in the same air, coming out different shapes. Take them in turn, because they break the map in two quite different places.

The cold end was substantially wrong

Bailey and Hallett, at the Desert Research Institute, published “Growth characteristics of laboratory grown ice crystals between −20 °C and −70 °C” as preprint 3.3 of a 2002 American Meteorological Society conference — freely readable at ams.confex.com, with the companion journal papers in press at the Quarterly Journal of the Royal Meteorological Society as they wrote. The apparatus was a static diffusion chamber, the crystals grown on glass filaments about 50 microns across, at ice supersaturations from 1 % up past water saturation. And one detail that no other source in this chapter supplies at all — recall that the taxonomy papers behind the photographic matrices report only “normal air”, with no number attached. Bailey and Hallett set the background pressure deliberately, from 500 mb down to 150 mb, to match what the standard atmosphere actually offers at the altitudes where air gets that cold.

Their finding, put as flatly as they put it: contrary to the classical habit diagrams, growth between −20 and −40 °C is predominantly plate-like, and it is dominated by polycrystalline forms. Genuine columnar growth appears predominantly below −40 °C, showing up as bullet rosettes at the higher supersaturations. The plate-to-column transition begins near −38 °C and completes just below −40.

Read that against the fourth row of the four-band table and the disagreement is not a detail of a degree or two. The table says Columns and Plates below −21.5 °C, with columns predominating by −30. Bailey and Hallett say plates all the way down to −38, and columns only after that. Measure the gap against either classic number and it is large: eight degrees colder than the point where the source caption has columns predominating, and sixteen and a half degrees colder than the boundary the table prints.

They also name the culprit, and that is the part which should change how you read the older charts rather than merely how you read their cold end. The columns drawn down there in the classical diagrams are attributed largely to nucleation-method artifacts — to how the experimenters started their crystals — together with a misinterpretation of “prism” forms in the older chamber work. The old cold-end data, in other words, were contaminated by the method that produced them.

Which hands this chapter something it did not have a page ago. Remember the twelve degrees that the section above found missing — the stretch from about −18 to −30 °C that Libbrecht simply leaves unassigned when he sets the sequence out as ranges. That gap was presented here as honest silence, and it is. But silence has a cause, and now there is a candidate for this one. The historical measurements in that stretch may not merely be sparse. They may be wrong, in a specific and diagnosable way.

The other half of their result is about how rare textbook crystals are down there at all. Pristine single crystals — the clean hexagonal plates and columns every diagram in this chapter draws — turn up at high frequency only when the ice supersaturation is very low, typically no more than about 2 %. Push the supply up and the population goes over to polycrystals. At −30 °C their habit statistics run from roughly 38 % polycrystals at 1 % supersaturation to about 95 % at 12 %. Real cold clouds are not full of tidy hexagons. They are full of crossed plates, side planes and bullet rosettes.

Careful

This is why good halo displays are rarer than the textbook diagrams of them suggest. A halo needs a sky full of crystals with genuinely flat, well-oriented faces, and by Bailey and Hallett’s numbers those want something like 30 to 90 minutes of stable air at −30 °C that is barely supersaturated at all — longer still if it is colder than that. Both of the obvious ways to get more ice work against you: more moisture means more polycrystals, and more cold means more patience required. The optics books draw the crystal the halo needs; the cloud usually supplies something else. Chapter 9 makes the same argument from the ground up — the published record of snow crystals is a heavily biased sample of the sky.

Two crystals, one set of conditions, two shapes

The third laboratory did not go cold. It went careful. Bacon, Baker & Swanson at the University of Washington (“Initial stages in the morphological evolution of vapour-grown ice crystals: A laboratory investigation”, Quart. J. Roy. Meteor. Soc. 129, 1903–1927, 2003, doi:10.1256/qj.02.04) held their crystals in an electrodynamic trap — levitated in an electric field, touching nothing whatsoever, no substrate and no fibre. Over a hundred particles, from −4 to −38 °C, at ice supersaturations from 0 to 10 %, at one atmosphere, each grown to 100–200 microns.

Then they varied one thing that no axis of the Nakaya diagram carries: how the crystal was started. Some grew from frozen droplets. Others grew from tiny sublimated “frost seeds”. Under near-identical temperature and humidity, the two populations came out looking like different substances. The droplet-frozen crystals were compact and isometric, aspect ratios bunched between 0.6 and 1.5, with rough faces. The frost-seeded crystals were pristine faceted prisms, and their aspect ratios spread all the way from 0.4 to 8 — thick plates and short columns, at the same temperature and the same humidity.

Their own summary, in close paraphrase, is the sentence to carry away: ice-particle morphology is not unique to particular temperature and humidity conditions, and seems also to depend on the ice initiation process. Two knobs are not enough.

The mechanism they propose is worth following, because it runs on machinery this site has already built. Suppose the way a crystal came into existence left it with dislocations — defects in the lattice — and suppose those defects happen to sit on only one family of facet. Chapter 5 supplies what that means: a defect of this kind is a permanent step, a place where an arriving molecule can join without anybody first having to nucleate a fresh terrace out of nothing. So the defective family grows freely while the clean family sits below its nucleation threshold and does nothing at all. And the arrangement defends itself: the growing facet acts as a vapour sink, drawing the local supersaturation down and holding it there, so the clean facets never reach the threshold that would let them start. A plate or a column, decided by where the defects happened to land, in air that is otherwise identical.

Now fit that to the chapter honestly, because it does not demolish anything. The Nakaya diagram is still real, and the free-fall survey in the last section is the proof: at water saturation, with crystals nucleated from frozen droplets and grown to millimetres, the boundaries come back within a degree or so. But notice that this is a protocol — a wet cloud, a droplet-frozen seed, a crystal allowed to get large. The diagram is a map of that protocol. Step off it, into low supersaturation and small sizes and a seed that began some other way, and the seed’s history can shout louder than the thermometer. That is the same history-dependence Chapter 12 meets again from the model side, where a spell of fast early growth can leave a crystal on a different branch for the rest of its life.

So hold all three results at once, and resist the urge to collapse them into a verdict. One independent laboratory, dropping crystals through a cloud tunnel, says the classic warm boundaries are right to within a degree or two. Another, running colder and drier than anyone had, says the classic cold end is wrong by well over ten degrees, and wrong for a reason you can name. A third says the shape was never a function of those two axes in the first place. Neither “the diagram is simply right” nor “the diagram is simply wrong” survives that. What survives is this: the Nakaya diagram is a two-dimensional slice through a space that has more directions in it than two — supersaturation regime, seed history, growth method, and, as Bailey and Hallett insisted by setting theirs on purpose, background pressure. The slice is real. It is just not the whole solid. Which is exactly why Chapter 13, when it comes to audit what this site has built, ends up calling this map an incomplete summary of what crystals do.

Still unsettled

Take the fourth row of the four-band table with considerably more salt than the other three. Everything below about −21 °C on this chapter’s chart rests on measurements that a later laboratory says were contaminated by their own nucleation method — and the replacement account, plates down to −38 °C and columns only after that, has not been folded back into the diagrams drawn here. The redrawn map in the animation above still prints the classic cold end, because that is what its source figure prints. Read that band as the least settled thing on the chart.

Hollow columns and needles: the same instability, sideways

Before the mystery, one loose end worth tying, because it shows that the machinery from the last chapter did not stop working when the crystal turned tall.

Take the column band near −5 °C and run the moisture axis upward. At low supersaturation you get exactly what you would expect: solid, fully faceted hexagonal prisms. Raise the supply to intermediate levels and something happens to the ends. The rim of each flat cap starts to outrun the middle of it, because the rim has better access to the incoming vapour — and once it is ahead, its access improves further. Libbrecht names the mechanism directly: “hollow columns form near -5 C as the supersaturation increases to intermediate values. The basic hollowing mechanism is a form of the Mullins-Sekerka instability.” That is the Mullins–Sekerka instability of Chapter 6, pointed at a different face. He draws the parallel to a picture you have already studied in this chapter — the three-panel hexagon of Figure 3.5, higher up the page. The hollowing “is analogous to the formation of branches shown in Figure 3.5, except now the edges of the basal surface remain faceted, or nearly so, as the hollows develop” (printed p. 114). Same runaway, same figure — only now the runaway edge stays flat while it wins.

The rims climb, the centres get left behind, and the crystal ends up with a conical pit sunk into each end.

Now the detail that makes this a piece of evidence rather than a curiosity: the two pits match — not perfectly, but very nearly, wiggle for wiggle. If the conditions wobbled while the crystal grew, both hollows record the same wobble at the same depth. Libbrecht spells out why: “Because both ends of the column experienced the same growth conditions as a function of time, the shape variations on the two ends of the column are nearly symmetrical.” The two ends are two independent recordings of one history.

Push the moisture higher still and the rims themselves give up. A rim can only stay flat and faceted for so long; eventually it succumbs to the same instability that hollowed the cap in the first place, and it splits. What sprouts from the corners are needles — which is why needle crystals live at the top of the column band, in the wettest air.

Be careful about what has and has not just happened, because it looks for a moment like a contradiction. This chapter spent a whole section arguing that diffusion on its own can never build a slender needle, and here is a purely diffusion-driven instability apparently building one. It is not. The instability decided where the ice would break out — at the corners of the basal edges, and nowhere else. It did not make the result slender. That is still the column band's own kinetic imbalance doing its work. Printed p. 114 states the requirement plainly — “an overall columnar shape indicates αprism ≪ 1 for moderate supersaturations near -5 C” — and the aspect-ratio rule from p. 111 supplies the comparison, that slender columns need alphaHKbasal ≫ alphaHKprism. Take that imbalance away and the corners would still break out; what came out of them would simply not be slender, because “diffusion effects alone cannot yield structures with extreme aspect ratios” (printed p. 111). Diffusion picks the place; the surface decides the shape.

What the map does not explain

Here is where the chapter has been going.

Everything the last few chapters built is still working. Diffusion explains why crystals get more elaborate as the air gets wetter — that whole vertical axis is accounted for. Attachment kinetics explains why facets stay flat. The branching instability explains stars, ferns, hollow ends and needles. All of it holds.

None of it explains the horizontal axis.

Work through what we now know. The alternation is real: three laboratories redrew it, and a fourth reproduced its switch points in free fall, with nothing touching the crystals. The aspect-ratio rule says an extreme shape can only come from a large imbalance between the two faces. Diffusion is ruled out as a source of that imbalance, and so is surface energy. So the flat–tall–flat–tall sequence can only mean one thing: as the air cools steadily in one direction, the basal and prism faces swap which of them is harder to grow on — and they do it three separate times.

Nothing in this site so far gives the slightest reason why a surface would behave like that. Cooling is one smooth change in one direction. Why should it produce three reversals rather than none, or one? Why at those particular temperatures? Sit with how strange that is: the two faces are made of the same substance, in the same lattice, in the same air, and yet the pecking order between them keeps flipping as the thermometer falls.

Still unsettled

This is not a gap in the teaching — it is a gap in the field. Opening a 2020 research paper, Libbrecht writes that the Nakaya diagram “has long presented an essential scientific puzzle in snow-crystal growth”, and that ever since it was “discovered empirically in the 1930s by Ukichiro Nakaya and collaborators” it has been hard to build a physical model that convincingly explains these changes in ice growth behaviour with temperature. In the monograph he is blunt about how the field got here: the observational side “was quickly well established. But the quest for a comprehensive physical model continues to this day.” Ninety years on, plates–columns–plates–columns is still the central open problem of the subject.

Quotations from K. Libbrecht, arXiv:2011.02353v1, p. 1, and Snow Crystals (arXiv:1910.06389v2), printed p. 157.

So be precise about what the diagram is and is not. It is a superb description. Handed a crystal, it tells you where the crystal grew; handed a cloud, it tells you what will fall out of it. That is a genuine scientific achievement and it is why the Rosetta Stone comparison stuck. But a Rosetta Stone lets you read the writing; it does not tell you why the language works the way it does. There is a candidate answer and this site will get to it, but it arrives as a curve fitted to real measurements, and it will only convince someone who has watched how those measurements are made. So do the better thing with the tension in the meantime: use it. A crystal that fell through changing air did not pick one square on this map. It visited several, and it wrote down the order.

Hold that question — and know in advance that the next two chapters will not answer it. They are not a prerequisite; they are the rest of Part III, which is about what actually comes out of the sky. The real prerequisites are Chapter 10, where you watch the rates being measured, and Chapter 11, where the surface finally gives up a number. Before we go and watch that happen: two chapters on what this map is already good for — reading a single crystal's journey off its own arms — and on what is really falling out there, most of which looks nothing like a holiday card.
