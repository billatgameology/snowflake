Chapter 7

Plates, Columns, Plates, Columns

Why does cooling the air flip a snow crystal between flat and tall — three times?

~15 min read 13 figures 4 animations

By the end you will be able to

  • Say what a crystal's habit is, and reduce it to a single number.
  • State the aspect-ratio rule, and use it to read the physics of an ice surface off a photograph.
  • Explain why diffusion on its own can never build a thin plate or a slender needle.
  • Read the morphology diagram in both directions — conditions to shape, and shape back to conditions.
  • Locate the four shape bands and the temperatures where the shape flips.
  • Explain a hollow column and a needle as the same branching instability, turned sideways.
  • State precisely what the diagram does not explain, and why that has stayed open since the 1930s.

So far this site has built one engine with two parts. Water vapour has to reach the crystal, and diffusion decides how much arrives where — that is the fuel. Molecules then have to attach, and the ice surface decides how readily they do — that is the throttle. Between them they gave us faceting, and then branching.

That engine is enough to explain a great deal. It is not enough to explain the single most obvious thing about snow crystals: some of them are flat as paper, and some of them are shaped like pencils. This chapter is where that becomes a problem.

One number for the whole shape

A crystal's habit is its overall shape, ignoring the decoration — plate, block, column, needle. Two snow crystals can carry wildly different ornament and still share a habit, and habit is what the rest of this chapter is about.

Habit sounds like a vague, hand-waving word. It is not. Every snow crystal is built on a hexagonal prism, and a hexagonal prism carries just the two families of facet you named back in Chapter 1: the two basal caps and the six prism sides. Chapter 3 already showed you the losing candidates — the {112̄0} secondary prism planes, on which ice is not observed to facet at all, and the pyramidal facets, which turn up only very rarely. For habit, these two families are the whole story. So the whole shape collapses to one number — how far the crystal reaches from cap to cap, divided by how far it reaches across.

aspect ratio = c ÷ a

where
c
the crystal's overall size along the c-axis — cap to cap, straight through the two basal facets
a
the crystal's overall size along an a-axis — straight across the hexagon, corner to corner

Definition as given in Libbrecht, Snow Crystals, printed p. 111

An aspect ratio below 1 means flatter than it is wide. Above 1 means taller than it is wide. And the range that real snow crystals cover is startling. A large thin plate can get down to about 0.01 — a hundred times wider than it is thick. Libbrecht's phrasing is hard to improve on: that extreme aspect ratio “is what puts the ‘flake’ in ‘snowflake.’” At the other end, an especially slender needle can reach 20 or more.

Key idea

Every snow crystal habit — plate, block, column, needle — is one number: how tall it is divided by how wide it is. Real crystals span that number from about 0.01 to about 20.

The aspect-ratio rule

Here is the step that turns a photograph into physics. A growing crystal ends up bounded by its slowest faces. A fast face runs outward until it meets its neighbours and vanishes from the outline; a slow face just sits there, getting wider. So a crystal that ended up thin must have had lazy caps, and a crystal that ended up tall must have had lazy sides.

Chapter 5 gave a name to how readily molecules stick to a given facet: the attachment coefficient, written α (0 to 1). Small α means a stubborn facet. Put the two ideas together and you get a rule that Libbrecht says “applies throughout the menagerie of different snow-crystal types”:

thin plates ⇒ αbasal ≪ αprism
slender columns ⇒ αbasal ≫ αprism

where
αbasal
how readily a molecule sticks to the flat top or bottom cap
αprism
how readily a molecule sticks to one of the six sides
≪
“very much less than” — not merely smaller, but smaller by a lot
≫
“very much greater than”

Libbrecht, Snow Crystals, printed p. 111: “the formation of thin plates invariably requires αbasal ≪ αprism, while the formation of slender columns requires αbasal ≫ αprism.”

Read that backwards and it becomes a measuring instrument. You do not need a laboratory to find out that the caps of a stellar dendrite are far harder to grow on than its edges. You need a photograph of one. Libbrecht runs exactly that argument on a stellar dendrite: the rounded branch tips say αprism is close to 1 there, and the extreme flatness says αbasal is below 0.01. As he puts it, “just looking at a large stellar dendrite with rounded branch tips reveals that αprism ≈ 1 at the tips and αbasal < 0.01.”

One more thing the rule is careful about. It says αbasal ≪ αprism, with the “very much less than” symbol, not simply “less than”. A small imbalance gives you a slightly squat or slightly stretched prism. An aspect ratio of 0.01 needs a whopping imbalance. Extreme shapes are evidence of extreme differences between the two faces.

Why diffusion can never make a plate

You might reasonably object that we already have a mechanism for making dramatic shapes. Chapter 6 built the branching instability: a bump that sticks further into the moist air collects more vapour, so it grows faster, so it sticks out further. That runaway is what turns a plain hexagon into a six-armed star. Why can it not also flatten a crystal, or stretch it?

Because branching and stretching are different jobs, and diffusion is only good at one of them. Diffusion makes structures intricate. Left to itself it does not make them lopsided. Take a thin plate and remove the imbalance between its two faces — set αbasal and αprism equal — and the plate does not stay thin. Its edges thicken, and the whole thing drifts back toward being as tall as it is wide, even while it keeps sprouting elaborate branches.

How we know

This is not a hunch; it is what happens when you actually solve the growth equations with the two faces made equal. Libbrecht runs exactly that experiment on paper, on printed p. 112: “If, for example, one begins with a thin plate crystal and then changes parameters so that αbasal ≈ αprism, then the subsequent growth will not maintain the thin-plate structure. Instead the edges of the plate would thicken over time and the overall aspect ratio would tend toward unity.” Take away the imbalance and the plate un-flattens itself. The general statement is on p. 111: “diffusion effects alone cannot yield structures with extreme aspect ratios, like thin plates or slender columns.”

The other candidate explanation gets ruled out in the same breath. Surface energy — the tendency of a surface to minimise its own area — can favour some facets over others in principle. In ice it is far too even-handed to matter here. Highly lopsided surface energies, Libbrecht writes, “are mostly a theoretical fiction (except perhaps in exotic materials).” A more recent paper of his (arXiv:2306.13087v1, p. 4) puts both eliminations in a single sentence: “Neither diffusion-limited growth nor surface-energy effects produce growing snow crystals with high overall aspect ratios.”

So there is exactly one place left for an extreme shape to come from: a large difference between how easily molecules attach to the caps and how easily they attach to the sides. The habit of a snow crystal is a report on its own surface chemistry.

Which raises the obvious question: what does the cloud do to that surface? The answer, and it is the whole reason this chapter exists, is that a cloud has only two knobs to turn. The first is temperature.

The second axis: how wet the air is

The second knob is supersaturation — how much spare water vapour the air is carrying beyond what it can hold in balance with ice. The two knobs do different jobs, and keeping them separate is the key to reading everything that follows.

Temperature decides plate or column. Supersaturation decides simple or complicated. At low supersaturation, crystals are blocky and fully faceted — plain hexagonal columns, stubby plates, nothing dramatic. Turn the supply up and you cross into branching: first six primary branches with little decoration, and only at the highest levels, side branches sprouting off the branches. Libbrecht describes exactly that progression from his own laboratory series on printed p. 301: “As the supersaturation increases, branching often begins with six primary branches with little or no sidebranching. Sidebranching eventually develops at the highest supersaturations shown in Figure 8.16, although some temperatures are more prone to copious sidebranching than others.”

The Figure 8.16 named in that quotation is worth knowing about. It is the same two-knob map we are building, made entirely out of photographs of real laboratory crystals rather than drawings. No single page can hold it: its caption, on printed p. 301, opens by warning you that the figure is “(Following five pages) The snow crystal morphology diagram depicted by crystals growing on the ends of slender ice needles, as a function of temperature and supersaturation.” Read one of its columns upward and the crystals get more elaborate; read one of its rows across and they change kind. Those are the two sentences this chapter is arguing about.

The upward reading is the one you can watch happen. Below is a single crystal, photographed three times while the applied supersaturation was raised. You met it in Chapter 6, where it was evidence about branching. Here it is evidence about an axis.

Careful

“Colder weather makes fancier snowflakes” is a natural guess and it is wrong. Elaboration tracks the moisture supply, not the thermometer. In dry air you get plain blocky crystals — squat prisms, stubby plates or short columns depending on the temperature — however far below freezing you go; in a wet cloud near −15 °C you get ferns. Temperature mainly changes which shape you get; supersaturation is what decides how ornate it becomes. Not entirely, though: as Libbrecht says of his own laboratory series, “some temperatures are more prone to copious sidebranching than others.”

There is a ceiling on that second axis, and it is a real physical one rather than a drafting choice. Push the humidity up inside a cloud and eventually the air becomes saturated with respect to liquid water, and droplets start condensing out of it. From then on the droplets take up the surplus, so the supersaturation around the crystal does not climb much further. That ceiling is drawn on every version of the diagram as the water saturation line. Above it you are no longer describing weather; you are describing a laboratory.

The map from weather to shape

Two knobs, two axes. Put temperature along the bottom and spare water vapour up the side, and every kind of snow crystal turns out to occupy its own territory. That chart is the snow crystal morphology diagram, universally called the Nakaya diagram.

Ukichiro Nakaya built it in the 1930s in a walk-in freezer laboratory at Hokkaido, by growing snow crystals in a chamber and varying the conditions on purpose. It took years. The monograph's account is deadpan (printed p. 31): Nakaya “spent years examining how his synthetic snow crystals grew and developed as he varied the temperature and supersaturation within his growth chamber, soon combining all his observations into what is now called the snow crystal morphology diagram, or the Nakaya diagram.”

The diagram was recognised immediately for what it was. In Libbrecht's words (printed p. 31) it “was immediately recognized as being like a Rosetta Stone for snowflakes. With it, one can translate the shape of a falling snow crystal into a description of its growth history. Upon seeing a slender needle crystal, for example, one can deduce that it must have grown in high humidity at a temperature near -5 C.” Nakaya himself, on printed p. 32, liked to say that snowflakes are “hieroglyphs from the sky.”

Careful

The diagram's own fine print — the caption Libbrecht gives this same chart when it is reprinted later in the book, on printed p. 383 — is worth reading before anyone over-trusts it: “this chart provides just a rough approximation of the different morphologies, plus it applies only if the growth conditions are constant in time.” Real crystals fall through changing air. The map tells you what grows in a steady environment; a crystal that visited two environments will carry two answers at once.

Plates, columns, plates, columns

Now do the thing the animation asked you to do. Pick a moisture level, hold it, and cool the air steadily from freezing point downward. Here is what nature does.

Just below 0 °C: plates. Cool a couple of degrees further and the crystals flip to columns — pencils and needles. Keep cooling and around ten degrees below freezing they flip back to plates, and this is the band that produces the great flat stars of the holiday cards. Keep going, past twenty degrees below, and they turn back toward columns again — though this last band is the untidy one. Its printed label is Columns and Plates. Both habits turn up in it, and the published caption of the diagram those boundaries come from does not end the sequence at the boundary; it ends it at “predominantly columns (T < -30 C)”. Columns only clearly take over about nine degrees further down.

Flat, tall, flat, tall. Four bands, three switch points, from monotonously turning one knob in one direction.

How we know

The three temperatures in that table are not quoted from a sentence; they were read off a published figure. A 300 dpi render of arXiv:1211.5555v1 Figure 1 was measured, the three dashed boundary lines located, and the temperature axis calibrated tick by tick — which mattered, because that axis is drawn non-uniformly and shrinks by a factor of 1.77 from the warm end to the cold end. Calibrating it with a single straight fit misplaces every boundary. The stated read uncertainty is ±0.5 °C. That covers the width of the printed lines and the calibration; it does not cover the fact that the real transitions are fuzzy bands rather than lines. Other sources put the two warm boundaries a degree or two elsewhere; the coldest one is barely pinned at all, as you will see a few paragraphs below.

The switches are sharp. Libbrecht, on printed p. 32: “Even a change of a few degrees can dramatically alter its growth behavior.” Two degrees can be the difference between a pencil and a wafer.

Two obvious worries. Did Nakaya simply mis-draw his own boundaries? And is this an artefact of one freezer in one building in Hokkaido?

The later research literature states the sequence more precisely than the original chart does — and in doing so, admits how loose the cold end really is. Libbrecht, opening a 2020 paper: the diagram “illustrates a complex series of morphological transitions between thin platelike structures (appearing in air at temperatures near -14 C and again above -3 C) and slender columnar forms (near -4 C and again below -30 C).” Elsewhere he sets the sequence out as ranges rather than lines: growth in air is “platelike above -3 C, columnar from -4 C to -6 C, platelike again from -11 C to -18 C, and columnar again below -30 C” (arXiv:2012.12916v1, p. 1). Count what is missing there. The stretch from about −18 to −30 °C — twelve degrees, straddling the boundary our table prints at −21.5 — is simply left unassigned. That is a more honest picture of the coldest switch than any single number. And the cold end of the map has since been pushed much further out — Bailey and Hallett “extended these results with additional observations exploring temperatures down to -70 C.”

Hollow columns and needles: the same instability, sideways

Before the mystery, one loose end worth tying, because it shows that the machinery from the last chapter did not stop working when the crystal turned tall.

Take the column band near −5 °C and run the moisture axis upward. At low supersaturation you get exactly what you would expect: solid, fully faceted hexagonal prisms. Raise the supply to intermediate levels and something happens to the ends. The rim of each flat cap starts to outrun the middle of it, because the rim has better access to the incoming vapour — and once it is ahead, its access improves further. Libbrecht names the mechanism directly: “hollow columns form near -5 C as the supersaturation increases to intermediate values. The basic hollowing mechanism is a form of the Mullins-Sekerka instability.” That is the Mullins–Sekerka instability of Chapter 6, pointed at a different face. He draws the parallel to a picture you have already studied in this chapter — the three-panel hexagon of Figure 3.5, higher up the page. The hollowing “is analogous to the formation of branches shown in Figure 3.5, except now the edges of the basal surface remain faceted, or nearly so, as the hollows develop” (printed p. 114). Same runaway, same figure — only now the runaway edge stays flat while it wins.

The rims climb, the centres get left behind, and the crystal ends up with a conical pit sunk into each end.

Now the detail that makes this a piece of evidence rather than a curiosity: the two pits match — not perfectly, but very nearly, wiggle for wiggle. If the conditions wobbled while the crystal grew, both hollows record the same wobble at the same depth. Libbrecht spells out why: “Because both ends of the column experienced the same growth conditions as a function of time, the shape variations on the two ends of the column are nearly symmetrical.” The two ends are two independent recordings of one history.

Push the moisture higher still and the rims themselves give up. A rim can only stay flat and faceted for so long; eventually it succumbs to the same instability that hollowed the cap in the first place, and it splits. What sprouts from the corners are needles — which is why needle crystals live at the top of the column band, in the wettest air.

Be careful about what has and has not just happened, because it looks for a moment like a contradiction. This chapter spent a whole section arguing that diffusion on its own can never build a slender needle, and here is a purely diffusion-driven instability apparently building one. It is not. The instability decided where the ice would break out — at the corners of the basal edges, and nowhere else. It did not make the result slender. That is still the column band's own kinetic imbalance doing its work. Printed p. 114 states the requirement plainly — “an overall columnar shape indicates αprism ≪ 1 for moderate supersaturations near -5 C” — and the aspect-ratio rule from p. 111 supplies the comparison, that slender columns need αbasal ≫ αprism. Take that imbalance away and the corners would still break out; what came out of them would simply not be slender, because “diffusion effects alone cannot yield structures with extreme aspect ratios” (printed p. 111). Diffusion picks the place; the surface decides the shape.

What the map does not explain

Here is where the chapter has been going.

Everything the last few chapters built is still working. Diffusion explains why crystals get more elaborate as the air gets wetter — that whole vertical axis is accounted for. Attachment kinetics explains why facets stay flat. The branching instability explains stars, ferns, hollow ends and needles. All of it holds.

None of it explains the horizontal axis.

Work through what we now know. The alternation is real: three laboratories reproduced it. The aspect-ratio rule says an extreme shape can only come from a large imbalance between the two faces. Diffusion is ruled out as a source of that imbalance, and so is surface energy. So the flat–tall–flat–tall sequence can only mean one thing: as the air cools steadily in one direction, the basal and prism faces swap which of them is harder to grow on — and they do it three separate times.

Nothing in this site so far gives the slightest reason why a surface would behave like that. Cooling is one smooth change in one direction. Why should it produce three reversals rather than none, or one? Why at those particular temperatures? Sit with how strange that is: the two faces are made of the same substance, in the same lattice, in the same air, and yet the pecking order between them keeps flipping as the thermometer falls.

Still unsettled

This is not a gap in the teaching — it is a gap in the field. Opening a 2020 research paper, Libbrecht writes that the Nakaya diagram “has long presented an essential scientific puzzle in snow-crystal growth”, and that ever since it was “discovered empirically in the 1930s by Ukichiro Nakaya and collaborators” it has been hard to build a physical model that convincingly explains these changes in ice growth behaviour with temperature. In the monograph he is blunt about how the field got here: the observational side “was quickly well established. But the quest for a comprehensive physical model continues to this day.” Ninety years on, plates–columns–plates–columns is still the central open problem of the subject.

Quotations from K. Libbrecht, arXiv:2011.02353v1, p. 1, and Snow Crystals (arXiv:1910.06389v2), printed p. 157.

So be precise about what the diagram is and is not. It is a superb description. Handed a crystal, it tells you where the crystal grew; handed a cloud, it tells you what will fall out of it. That is a genuine scientific achievement and it is why the Rosetta Stone comparison stuck. But a Rosetta Stone lets you read the writing; it does not tell you why the language works the way it does. There is a candidate answer and this site will get to it, but it arrives as a curve fitted to real measurements, and it will only convince someone who has watched how those measurements are made. So do the better thing with the tension in the meantime: use it. A crystal that fell through changing air did not pick one square on this map. It visited several, and it wrote down the order.

Hold that question — and know in advance that the next two chapters will not answer it. They are not a prerequisite; they are the rest of Part III, which is about what actually comes out of the sky. The real prerequisites are Chapter 10, where you watch the rates being measured, and Chapter 11, where the surface finally gives up a number. Before we go and watch that happen: two chapters on what this map is already good for — reading a single crystal's journey off its own arms — and on what is really falling out there, most of which looks nothing like a holiday card.
