Chapter 28

The Exam Result

Two hundred and four crystals, twenty hours, three agreements out of ninety — and not one invalid run. Which of those numbers is the result?

~18 min read 7 interactives 2 equations

By the end you will be able to

  • Hold two true statements about the same run at once: it reproduced the −5 °C laboratory measurements its own inputs came from, and it failed the map.
  • Say why the headline is 3 of 90 and not the 5 of 90 the same experiment measured first — and why the fall was written down before it happened.
  • Explain why flawless numerics are the thing that lets a disagreement be blamed on a theory instead of on the code.
  • Follow a counting argument from curve crossings to habit boundaries, and say why counting is a stronger result than scoring.
  • Recognise a project aiming its honesty machinery at its own good news.

Two numbers came back

The exam was one command. node runner/src/main.ts phase6-sweep 6. It ran for about twenty hours on six worker processes and exited with status 0, having completed 204 of 204 points on the float64 CPU oracle — the slow reference engine, for the reason chapter 26 gave. Those lines are printed in the provenance table at the top of research/phase6-sweep-report.md, next to the fingerprint of the protocol it was run against — the twenty-five-row 8aeb2b80… that chapter 27 watched being sealed — the parameter set it ran, CAK, and two commit hashes with a verified relationship between them: the freeze commit is an ancestor of the commit that executed, which is the machine-checkable form of “the rules were written first”.

Two numbers came out, and they point in opposite directions.

The first is the score:

“Headline: 3 of 90 headline-scope points agree.”

research/phase6-sweep-report.md, “Scored result”

Three out of ninety — ninety being the counting points chapter 27 derived in advance, before anyone knew what would land on them. That is not a near miss. That is a miss so total that you would normally suspect the machine was broken.

The second number is the reason you cannot suspect that:

“Zero invalid runs in 204, in both sweeps. Every point converged under the dual criterion, held symErr = 0 with noise off, kept every per-tick attachment delta D6h-invariant, and cleared the 65% domain-contact guard. Nothing was excluded, so nothing had to be argued about.”

research/phase6-sweep-report.md, “Findings” §2

Hold on to the phrase in both sweeps. There were two, the first of which this project threw away; the next section is that story. The numerics were spotless in both, which is why the story is about bookkeeping rather than about broken code.

Four checks, decided before either sweep, applied automatically to every run. They live in one function in runner/src/phase6-sweep.ts called phase6ScorePoint, and they are four lines long: allConverged, deltaSymClean, symmetryError !== 0, domainContact. Fail any one and the run is stamped invalid and, in the file’s own words, “EXCLUDED BY NAME, never dropped — … Those are runs that did not happen properly, not statements about the model.”

None of them fired. Not once, across 204 crystals grown at 34 temperatures and six humidities. In plain English: every crystal converged on both of the two independent convergence tests chapter 22 described, not just the easy one; every crystal came out with a symmetry error of exactly zero; every single growth step stayed perfectly six-fold symmetric, which is what “D6h-invariant” means — D6h is the crystallographer’s name for the symmetry of a hexagonal prism; and no crystal ever grew far enough to touch the wall of its own box.

This is what makes the failure usable. The ordinary escape hatch from a bad comparison is some of the runs were no good, so the comparison is unfair. Here there are no bad runs to point at.

Chapter 15 taught you to separate two claims that sound alike, and the project’s own charter is blunt about why:

“Hold the distinction precisely, because it is the one this project exists to be honest about: giving a model real physics is not the same as showing the model reproduces reality.”

project charter.md, §1.5

So say it in the split form. The model was given real, measured physics — that claim is about where the numbers came from, and it is the claim chapter 16 spent its whole length supporting. The model reproduces the Nakaya diagram — that claim is about outcome, and it has now been tested once, under a scheme frozen in advance, and the measured result disagrees: a result that is not yet gate evidence and not yet a Phase 6 conclusion, because the verifier has not run and the second arm does not exist. Both claims are about the same 204 runs. Neither is allowed to stand in for the other.

Key idea

A wrong answer about nature is a measurement. A wrong answer about your own rules is a defect. The 204 runs produced the first and tripped none of the four numeric validity rules — which is what lets the disagreement be attributed to the physics rather than to the program.

Two things about the standing of that 3, before you read it anywhere else. Neither is a hedge added here; both are printed above the number in the report itself.

It is not gate evidence yet. The report says so in bold before it prints a single number, and names the step that has not happened — WP5, the project’s verification-and-closure work package: “These numbers are not yet gate evidence. WP5 has not run: no independent verifier has re-derived report.json from points.json, and the six negative controls have not executed.” What it is instead, in the report’s own phrase, is “a measured result with verified provenance, which is not the same thing”. Chapter 27 built the distinction; this is it being applied to the project’s own headline.

And it is one arm of two. This sweep is the control — the model with no SDAK in it at all. The treatment arm, the one with the dip mechanism switched on, has not been run, and the report refuses to draw the Phase 6 conclusion until it has: “The numbers below are complete and final for this arm; what they mean for the phase waits.” So this chapter is reading a result, not a verdict.

How the headline got corrected

The 3 replaced a 5, and how that happened is the most instructive thing on this page.

The first run of this same 204-point sweep finished, scored, and produced a headline of 5 of 90. Then an internal review compared the runs against the protocol they were supposedly run under, and found that they disagreed. The frozen protocol registered how one parameter should be built — aPrism, the prefactor curve for the prism faces, registered as "piecewise-linear-in-(Tm-T)", deliberately different from aBasal: "constant-1". Every one of the 204 runs had instead used Aprism ≡ 1 at every temperature, because the sweep harness never passed a parameter set to the child processes and a command-line default quietly supplied one. The decision that records it puts the mechanism in a single sentence:

“So an unregistered CLI default silently overrode a registered freeze row, through a path the freeze did not cover because paramSet was not itself a registered row.”

docs/decisions/0031-phase6-paramset-freeze-row.md

The charter clause that priced this was written long before anyone needed it: any post-freeze edit to parameters or protocol “invalidates prior sweep results — the full sweep re-runs”. So the 5 of 90 was not softened, annotated or kept as a secondary figure. It was invalidated — the decision insists on that word rather than “superseded” — and its artifacts were moved, unmodified, to a directory whose name carries its own obituary: out/phase6-sweep-6995868-cak-a1-superseded/. A full 204-point sweep’s worth of compute, thrown away by the project’s own rule, over a defect nobody outside the project would ever have found.

Key idea

A missing rule and a broken rule are different failures. Chapter 27’s earlier amendments added rows nobody had thought to write down, and cost nothing because no experiment had run yet. This one was worse: the rule was written down, and the runs contradicted it — “a materially worse failure than an unregistered degree of freedom, because the artifact and the evidence disagree while both look complete”.

Now the part that makes this an experiment rather than a search. Before the re-run, the project worked out which way the headline would move, and wrote it down:

“Registered prediction: the headline falls from 5/90 to approximately 2/90.” … “The corrected result is expected to be worse, and that is the point. A validation whose headline improves when you fix a defect is pleasant; one whose headline degrades is the case that tests whether the pre-registration is real.”

docs/decisions/0031-phase6-paramset-freeze-row.md, Decision §4 and Consequences

Read why that sentence had to exist. Two parameter sets were available, one of which scored better. If the choice is made after seeing both scores, the pre-registration is worthless — it has become a post-registration. So the decision fixed the choice on provenance (the registered curve has a printed closed form behind it; the flat Aprism ≡ 1 has only a documented modelling convenience), fixed the expected direction in writing, and added the clause that closes the escape hatch: if the re-sweep returns a worse number, “that is the predicted outcome of adopting the better-provenanced inputs and is not grounds for revisiting this decision”.

The re-sweep came in at 3 of 90. The report grades its own forecast in one line: “Direction correct, magnitude one point pessimistic.” Here is the whole correction, row by row — all five of the original agreements sat at −2 °C, where the registered curve throttles the prism prefactor by 3.6×, and you can watch three survive it and two fail.

The forecast had been built by interpolating between two calibration probes and predicted a rise near 1.9–2.2×; the measured rises are 1.35–1.61×. The two-point interpolation ran hot, which is exactly why three agreements survived instead of two. The report also names what that says about the method rather than only about the answer: the rise is not monotone in f, so “a single throttle-to-rise factor was the wrong model for it; the probes happened to sample the two ends”. A prediction that lands one point off, for a reason you can state afterwards, is worth more than one that lands exactly and cannot say why.

One more thing the correction taught, and it is a physics result rather than a bookkeeping one. Restoring the registered curve made the prism faces harder to grow on, which is a push away from plates — and at the temperatures where that push is hardest the model did not become a column. It became nothing in particular. A probe at −5 °C, where the correction bites hardest of all, moved the aspect ratio from 0.3821 to exactly 1.0000: an equant crystal, as thick as it is wide. The findings document states the lesson in one line — “The broad-facet correction moves the model from one habit to no habit, not to the other habit” — and you will meet that same shape again twice on this page.

The measured diagram

The sweep is a grid: 34 temperatures from −2 to −35 °C one degree apart, crossed with six humidity levels expressed as a fraction f of water saturation — 0.10, 0.15, 0.25, 0.40, 0.60 and 0.90. Thirty-four times six is 204. Each cell is one crystal, grown from a 19-site hexagonal seed until it reached a registered measurement size, then measured for its aspect ratio — thickness divided by width, so a low number is a flat plate and a high number is a stubby column — and sorted into one of three classes: plate (aspect ratio at or below 0.6667, which is 1/1.5), column (1.5 or above), or neutral — the model declining to commit to either. Those two numbers are the registered ones, spelled out in runner/src/phase6-protocol.ts as “AR = z-extent / T-extent; plate AR ≤ 1/1.5 (0.6667), column AR ≥ 1.5, otherwise neutral”.

Chapter 27 sealed the marking scheme before any of this ran — before either run of it, which is worth saying now that there have been two. Three of its clauses do all the work here. Neutral counts as disagreement, because the reference names a habit in every regime and the neutral band is the model’s invention, not the reference’s. Points within 1 °C of a reference boundary do not count, in either direction. And the coldest regime, which accepts both plates and columns, is reported but kept out of the headline, because a band that accepts both answers cannot discriminate between them. Ninety points survive those rules. Three of them agree.

Where those three sit matters more than the fact that there are three. In the temperature band where the reference demands columns, the model agreed on 0 of 24 counting points; in the band where it demands cold plates, 0 of 60. The denominators differ because the bands are different widths on the temperature axis, and each surviving temperature carries six humidities with it.

Do the same count on the withdrawn run — switch the interactive across and read it off the grid — and it was 31 plate, 143 neutral, 30 column. Restoring one registered curve cost the model twenty-five of its plates and bought it no columns at all: the column count is identical, because below −10 °C the two parameter sets are the same model — the source’s own figure caption says both prefactors are about 1 between −10 and −30 °C, so there was nothing there for the correction to change. That is the second sighting of it: away from one habit, not toward the other.

How we know

Each measured grid is published as 34×6 characters of plain text, small enough to print in a report and read with your eyes. That is a deliberate choice: it means any reader can re-run the scoring rule by hand and arrive at 3 of 90 without trusting the program that produced it. The corrected grid is the one in research/phase6-sweep-report.md today; the withdrawn grid is in the same file at the revision it was withdrawn from, commit 6995868 in the project’s history, with its raw artifacts preserved unmodified at out/phase6-sweep-6995868-cak-a1-superseded/ — so a withdrawn result stays checkable instead of vanishing. Every interactive on this page that uses model results reads from those transcriptions, and re-implementing the registered rule over them reproduces the published per-band totals — 3/6 warm plates, 0/24 columns, 0/60 cold plates — and, over the withdrawn grid, 5/6, 0/24, 0/60. Both headlines come out exactly as published.

All three wins at one temperature

Now the part where the honesty machinery turns on the project’s own good news. All three agreements are at the same temperature. The report does not bury that; it prints it in bold, immediately under the headline:

“All three agreements are at −2 °C, the single counting temperature in the warmest regime — which ADR 0025 registered pre-sweep as carrying essentially no statistical weight, precisely so this could not be presented as a result. The model agrees with the reference nowhere else inside the headline scope.”

research/phase6-sweep-report.md, “Scored result”

Read the timing carefully, because it is the whole point. The warm plate band spans temperatures warmer than −3.3 °C. On a one-degree grid starting at −2 °C, and with the ±1 °C ambiguity band removing −3 °C, exactly one temperature in that band can ever count. The scoring decision therefore wrote this down before either sweep, while the outcome was still unknown:

“A stated limitation rather than a discovered one: the warmest Plates regime has exactly ONE counting temperature (−2 °C). It can therefore only ever score 0% or 100% and carries essentially no statistical weight… Any conclusion about the warm plate regime is a one-point conclusion and must say so.”

docs/decisions/0025-phase6-agreement-scoring-rule.md, Consequences

The “0% or 100%” in that clause is loose, and worth correcting in passing rather than repeating: the single counting temperature carries six humidity cells, which can and do disagree with each other, so the band can land anywhere from 0 to 6. It scored 5 of 6 in the withdrawn run and 3 of 6 in this one. What the clause was actually protecting is untouched by that, and it is the part to keep: any conclusion about the warm plate band is a one-temperature conclusion and must say so.

So every agreement the model has is a one-temperature conclusion, and the rule that says so was sealed before either sweep. At that one temperature, three of the six humidity cells came out plate, which is what the reference asks for; the other three came out neutral, which the rule counts as a miss. That is the entire positive half of the headline: one temperature, three humidities — and one of those three is flagged extent-fragile, meaning it sits close enough to the plate ceiling that measuring the crystal at a slightly different size could move it out of the class. In the withdrawn run the same band had five of six. Nothing else in the headline scope has ever agreed, in either run.

And then the honesty machinery does something less expected: it fires in the other direction, and takes back an accusation the project had made against itself. Earlier versions of this record put the warm end under suspicion, on the grounds that the digitized prism σ0 curve — the one the whole warm end rests on — sat low by a factor of 1.6 to 3.2 against a closed form Libbrecht had printed. A closer reading reversed it. The current report labels the paragraph CORRECTED and explains that the dedicated measurement papers give σ0,prism = 0.03% at −2 °C and about 0.2% at −5 °C, that the project’s digitized curve matches those to roughly 7%, and that the closed form it had been judging itself against is the outlier of the three, high by 3.01×. Its conclusion: “The warm end does not rest on a suspect σ0,prism.”

Notice that this is a withdrawn criticism, not a withdrawn result, and it is reported with the same machinery and the same bold labels as the withdrawn headline was. A record that only ever corrects downward is not a record, it is a mood. The undercut that survives is the one that was registered before the sweep: one counting temperature, no statistical weight, and it says so itself.

Key idea

Anyone can be honest about their bad news. The test of an honesty system is whether it fires on the good news too — and here the same document that reports three agreements is the document that says, in bold, that all three sit at one temperature it had already registered as carrying essentially no weight, and that one of the three is fragile to how big the crystal was when it was measured.

The one temperature that agrees with the laboratory

There is a second result in the same sweep, and it points the other way.

At −5 °C the Nakaya diagram demands a column. The corrected run grew neither habit there and scored zero; the withdrawn run grew a plate and also scored zero. But −5 °C is also the subject of a dedicated laboratory paper by Libbrecht himself, and that paper reports the opposite of the diagram. His substrate experiments there found aspect ratios “throughout the range 0.1 < ρaspect < 1. Columnar crystals (with ρaspect > 1) were absent”, with many crystals “blockier, nearly isometric” and thick plates “the norm” at a typical ρ of about 0.5. His ρaspect is thickness over width — the same quantity the model calls AR.

The model’s two −5 °C measurements bracket AR 0.3821 to 1.0000 — the first is what the withdrawn run measured there, the second what the registered parameter set produces at the same conditions — and both sit at or inside his measured range, columns absent. The corrected sweep classes that cell as neutral, which is the same statement in the coarser language of habit classes: not a column. So the model agrees with the laboratory measurements and disagrees with the diagram, which is precisely what the source says a broad-facet model should do. On p. 10 he writes it out: “Producing columnar crystals at −5 C then requires the SDAK effect.”

This is the one place where the correction that cost the project its headline improved the physics comparison, and it is worth being exact about how little that buys. The registered curve moved the model from a thin plate to an equant crystal, which is closer to the “blockier, nearly isometric” crystals he describes. It still did not produce a column, because the diagram is not what his bench found here either.

Two things must be said about that agreement, and the project says both about itself.

First, the −5 °C laboratory data is upstream of the model’s own inputs. The attachment-kinetics curves the model runs on were built from Libbrecht’s measurement programme, and this paper is part of that programme. Agreement here is therefore a strong consistency check — it says the pipeline from his printed measurements to a grown 3-D crystal did not mangle anything — but it is not independent validation. The findings ledger records it in exactly those words: “The model agrees with the lab data its inputs came from.”

Second, neither of those two numbers is gate evidence, and for two different reasons. AR 1.0000 comes from a calibration probe: a run made to help choose settings, not a run made under the frozen protocol. The project maintains a two-tier truth system for exactly this reason, and the document that prints the number prints the restriction beside it: “These are CALIBRATION PROBES and are never citable as gate evidence.” AR 0.3821 was a swept value — from the run that has since been invalidated. And the corrected sweep, which is a frozen-protocol run and does have verified provenance, is still waiting on the verifier and the negative controls before anything in it can be called gate evidence at all. Two numbers, two separate reasons, and the run behind them not yet gated either — all three facts stated rather than left for a reader to discover.

Three explanations, two eliminated

A model disagrees with a measurement. There are exactly three places to put the blame. The code is wrong. The numbers fed into it are wrong. The theory those numbers encode is insufficient. Everything in Part Two before this chapter exists to let you eliminate the first two — not by assertion, but with specific measurements that would look different if either were the culprit.

Try it yourself. Pick a culprit and see what comes back.

The alibi for the code is not just “zero invalid runs”. It is also that the program which certifies the phase is forbidden to fail on a morphology miss. The plan writes the separation as executable behaviour, not as a promise:

“A morphology miss is not a gate failure: the gate fails on invalid numerics, provenance, artifacts, incomplete runs, or a protocol violation — never on the model disagreeing with the diagram. That separation is what makes ‘a negative result is a result’ executable rather than aspirational.”

docs/plans/phase-6-nakaya-validation.md

The alibi for the numbers has two parts, and only one of them is blind. The −5 °C reproduction is the loud one, and you have just seen why it is a consistency check rather than a proof. The quiet one is better evidence, and it is about one specific quantity: the prefactor curve Aprism — the ceiling on how sticky a prism face can ever get — was digitized off a printed figure, pixel by pixel, before anyone found the later paper that prints the same curve as an equation — arXiv:2009.08404v2, Eq. (5). Compared afterwards, the digitized Aprism anchors reproduce the printed formula to 8.4% at worst and under 2% typically. An extraction that agrees with a source it could not have peeked at is independently confirmed, not merely self-consistent. And note which run that alibi covers: it clears the registered curve, and the withdrawn 204 runs did not use it — they used Aprism ≡ 1 at every temperature. The evidence and the evidence’s subject only lined up once the sweep was re-run, which is one more way of saying why the re-run had to happen.

So the alibi is stamped “with scope” rather than clean, and the scope is now smaller than it was: the suspicion over the warm prism σ0 has itself been withdrawn, as the previous section described. What remains is the honest limitation that will not go away — the laboratory data these inputs reproduce is the laboratory data these inputs came from.

Careful

“Zero invalid runs” does not mean “zero errors”. It means zero runs tripped four named validity conditions. The Aprism substitution tripped none of them, because no automatic check was watching that row: an unregistered command-line default quietly overrode a frozen parameter, and all 204 affected runs still came out numerically spotless. A machine can only catch the mistakes somebody thought to name — and this one was caught by a reader comparing the runs against the protocol, not by the machine.

The corrected run carries a second example, found while it was running and recorded because it is not yet fixed. The harness stamps the provenance once at the start, but each of the 204 child processes launches the model from the working tree — so an edit to the solver mid-run would change the physics for the later points while the report still named the commit it started from. Nine commits from a parallel session did land during the twenty hours; the project checked afterwards that none of them touched the solver, so this sweep is unaffected. Its own verdict on that: “That was luck, not design.” The fix — a preflight check that the child command line carries every frozen parameter, plus a re-check at the end — is outstanding.

One crossing, three boundaries

Eliminating two suspects leaves the third by default, which is a weak kind of argument. The strongest result in the whole sweep is not the score at all. It is a counting argument that says the model could not have passed, whatever the code and whatever the grid.

Here is the chain. A habit boundary — the temperature where a crystal stops growing plates and starts growing columns — requires the basal and prism facets to swap which one is stickier. Stickiness in this model is set by a critical supersaturation σ0, one curve per facet: the lower the curve, the stickier the facet, so a swap in stickiness is exactly a crossing of the two curves. Which way round matters. When the prism faces are the stickier pair they advance faster, the crystal spreads sideways, and you get a plate; when the basal faces win, it grows tall and you get a column. So the number of crossings is a hard ceiling on the number of habit boundaries a model can produce — and that ceiling does not care about the diffusion solver, the grid spacing, the seed shape, the far-field condition or the domain size.

Three independent parameterizations of those curves have been evaluated — the project’s own digitized anchors at −10.00 °C and two printed closed forms from separate papers at −8.39 and −10.92 °C. Each has exactly one crossing, and all three cross in the same direction. The Nakaya diagram has three boundaries. One is less than three, and no amount of engineering closes that gap.

This is the part of the sweep the correction did not touch, and the report says the withdrawal actually strengthened it: the plate → neutral → column sequence, monotone in temperature with no return, “now holds under both parameter sets, which is stronger than holding under one”. A structural claim that survives its own experiment being re-run under different inputs is doing better than a score that does not.

σ0,basal(T*) = 0.02·T*1.75 + 0.3
σ0,prism(T*) = 0.02·T*1.9 − 0.025(T* − 0.3)

where
T*
how far below freezing, in degrees C, written as a positive number — so T* = 15 means −15 °C
σ0
the critical supersaturation of a facet, in percent: how much spare vapour the air must carry before that face grows readily. Low means sticky.
basal / prism
the flat top-and-bottom faces, and the six side faces, of a hexagonal ice prism

Printed closed forms, arXiv:2009.08404v2 p. 3, Eqs. (2) and (3), transcribed verbatim in research/libbrecht-figure-findings.md §2

Those two printed formulas are what the next interactive draws. Nothing is fitted; they are typed in as printed. Your job is to make three habit boundaries out of them.

Switch both dips on at the depth the page starts with and the count goes to five. That number is not this page’s invention: evaluating Libbrecht’s own printed dip-bearing parameterization gives five crossings too, at −3.70, −6.25, −8.46, −9.93 and −18.62 °C, against reference boundaries at −3.3, −9.9 and −21.5 °C. Two of those land within 0.4 °C of a real boundary. And notice what the mapping does to the sweep’s failures: the columns band that scored 0 of 24 contains the basal dip at −4.5 °C; the cold plates band that scored 0 of 60 contains the prism dip at −14.4 °C; the one band where the model scored at all contains no dip.

Which is also the banner’s point. Once you drag a dip into place to make a boundary appear, you have put the answer in by hand — and the dips were originally positioned against the very diagram in question. That is why the project fixed the ceiling on its own future experiment before running it: a dip-bearing run that reproduces the diagram can only ever be called in-sample reproduction, never independent validation.

Still unsettled

The crossing is robust in sense and count, and worthless in location. Within the ±25% uncertainty the project had already registered on its digitized curves, the single crossing can sit anywhere from −6.90 to −22.89 °C — a 16 °C span that swallows most of the diagram. The nominal crossing happens to land within 0.1 °C of Nakaya’s real −9.9 °C boundary, and the project killed that coincidence itself, calling it “doubly worthless as evidence”. The falsifiable claim was restated to be the sense and the count only.

The knob that helps nothing

The obvious objection to all of this is: you just did not tune it well enough. The most plausible knob is the starting seed — every run in the project starts from the same little 19-site hexagon, radius 2, thickness 1, which is itself plate-shaped. Does the model grow plates because the physics says so, or because it started as one?

That was measured, not argued. Five seed geometries were run at the two temperatures that discriminate: −5 °C, where the reference demands a column (AR at or above 1.5), and −15 °C, where it demands a plate (AR at or below 0.6667). The answer is more interesting than a flat “no”.

One disclosure first, because these ten runs predate the correction: all of them were run under the parameter set that was later withdrawn. At −15 °C that changes nothing at all — below −10 °C the two sets are the same model, so those five numbers stand as they are. At −5 °C the registered curve would shift the whole column of five upward, as it shifted everything else at that temperature. The conclusion below survives that shift because it turns on direction, not on the values: a seed pushes both temperatures the same way, and the two bands need opposite pushes.

Seed shape is not a small knob. It moves the aspect ratio by +0.41 at −5 °C and +0.51 at −15 °C between the registered seed and a needle-like one, and the needle-like seed does produce a genuine column — at −15 °C, where a plate is required. That is the honest, awkward version of the result, and the project states it plainly rather than claiming the knob is weak.

The reason it still cannot rescue anything is that a seed is the same at every temperature. It slides every point in the same direction, and the two failing bands need opposite moves. The seed that comes closest at −15 °C is the worst one at −5 °C; the seed that comes closest at −5 °C manufactures the wrong habit at −15 °C. Not one of the five agrees at either temperature.

Key idea

Diffusion, grid spacing, seed shape, far field and domain size can all move the aspect ratio, some of them a lot. None of them can reverse the plate/column ordering as a function of temperature — and the diagram requires that three times. The bound is about direction, not size.

The shape of the failure

A score tells you the model missed. It does not tell you how. Two facts about the shape of the miss came out of the sweep that, as far as a literature check in July 2026 found, nobody had reported before — because nobody had run a full 3-D broad-facet model across this grid.

The first is that the crossing marks where plate stops, not where column starts. This project had assumed the crossing temperature is the habit-switch temperature, and nothing in the literature says otherwise. It is not. Plate growth ends at −4 °C in the driest row, −3 °C in the next and −2 °C in the one after that — nowhere near the registered −10 °C crossing — and then columns do not begin until −19 °C or, at the next humidity up, −23 °C. The band in between, 14 to 19 °C wide depending on the row, is the model declining to commit to either habit, and it is the single largest feature of the measured diagram. On Nakaya’s side of the comparison there is no gap at all: a knife-edge boundary at −9.9 °C.

The correction widened that gap rather than closing it, which is the third sighting of the same shape. In the withdrawn run plate growth had persisted to −8 or −9 °C across four humidity rows and the neutral band was roughly 10 °C wide. Restoring the registered curve pulled the plate edge back to −4/−3/−2 °C, left the column edge exactly where it was, and so made the region of no habit at all bigger. Drag the humidity slider below and you are reading the corrected run; the grid interactive higher up will show you the other one.

The second is that turning up the humidity destroys habit outright. Columns appear only in the two driest rows — and at f = 0.40, 0.60 and 0.90 the model produces nothing but neutral across all 34 temperatures. Not a plate, not a column, at any temperature from −2 to −35 °C, in half the humidity range. The direction was predicted before the sweep, from the attachment formula itself — but predicted as a bias, and measured as total. In the withdrawn run only the wettest row had been that empty; the corrected run empties three.

alphaHK = A · exp(−σ0 / σsurf)

where
alphaHK
the attachment coefficient: the fraction of molecules striking this facet that actually stick
A
the facet’s prefactor, its stickiness ceiling
σ0
the facet’s critical supersaturation — low means sticky
σsurf
the supersaturation actually present at the surface, which rises as you make the air wetter

As printed in research/phase6-sweep-report.md, “Findings” §5, explaining the measured contrast collapse

Read the formula and the collapse is obvious in hindsight. As σsurf grows, the exponential term goes to 1 and every facet’s alphaHK saturates toward its own ceiling A. The facets stop being able to tell each other apart, so the crystal stops being able to choose a habit. That much was computed on the registered curves before either sweep ran, and written into the protocol as the reason the humidity axis stops where it does: the basal/prism contrast spans 0.34 to 3.76 across the temperature axis at f = 0.15 and only 0.84–1.25 at f = 0.90. The top row was kept anyway, deliberately, because weak facet contrast is not the same as weak habit variation — at high supersaturation shape is set by branching rather than by facets, so what the model does there was registered as a real question rather than a foregone one. The uncomfortable part is the answer: the real diagram is at its most structured exactly there, full of dendrites, sectored plates and needles, and the model produces a blank.

Both facts sharpen the same lesson: crossing location and habit-transition location are different observables, and only the second is what a morphology diagram records. A model can get the crossing right and still record nothing recognisable as a habit boundary.

What this does not show

The sweep report ends with a list of what its own result does not establish, and any honest retelling has to carry that list rather than the headline alone.

It is not gate evidence, and it is not a phase conclusion. The verifier has not re-derived the report from the raw points, the six negative controls have not run, and the SDAK arm this one is the control for does not exist yet. Everything on this page is a measured result with verified provenance, which the report is careful to say is not the same thing as gated evidence.

It is not a test of SDAK. Every run is a no-SDAK run; in the report’s own words, “SDAK appears in no source file”. The dip mechanism has never been tested here.

Around −5 °C, no deterministic per-point score can be right even in principle. The source reports that there “both platelike and needlelike crystals can grow under essentially identical conditions”. If two habits grow at the same temperature and humidity, then habit is not a single-valued function of the two axes the diagram is drawn on, and a scheme that awards one mark per grid point is asking a question with no unique answer in that band. The report lists this among the things producing columns would require: a fast-growth transient the model has no representation of, alongside a nucleation barrier whose controlling terrace width is about 50 nanometres against this grid’s 350-nanometre cells.

The comparison target is a redrawn 1954 schematic. Its supersaturation axis failed an independent check when the project digitized it, which is why only its three boundary temperatures are used and its humidity values never are. No observational habit boundary anywhere carries a numerical uncertainty; the ±0.5 °C the project uses is its own construction.

The habit class is what the sweep may claim; the aspect ratio underneath is not. Four convergence studies found the class robust on every axis tested, while the value beneath it converges late or, at the warm condition under grid refinement, not in any usable sense. The zone is the claim; the dot is not.

It ran on one computer. No cross-platform control has been run, so the result is scoped to the registered x64 host. The correction did void the old control: the two-point fixture meant to catch a habit class flipping between machines was built around −5 and −15 °C, and under the registered parameter set both of those come out neutral — the calibration probe’s AR 1.0000 and about 1.1053 — and a two-point test whose two points share a class cannot detect a class flip.

It has since been rebuilt, in the order that keeps it a test. The decision that replaces it registered the rule for picking new points while the re-sweep was still running and its results did not exist yet — because choosing fixture points after seeing which ones happened to land near a class boundary would be selecting the test from the answers, which is the same freedom the parameter-set correction was written to remove. Applied afterwards, the rule returns four points in two pairs. The robust pair is the smallest and largest aspect ratio in the whole sweep — −2 °C at AR 0.263158, a clear plate, and −28 °C at 2.33333, a clear column — and it asks whether the pipeline agrees across architectures at all. The fragile pair sits as close to the class thresholds as anything in the sweep — −3 °C at 0.684211, just over the plate ceiling, and −23 °C at exactly 1.5, one lattice site from changing class — and it asks whether any habit class here was decided by a coin toss in the last bit. The two pairs are reported separately and never pooled, and a difference in either is registered in advance as a finding rather than a bug to be tuned away. Their x64 baseline is already published, lifted verbatim from the corrected sweep’s own raw points rather than re-run on the side. What is outstanding is the other machine: those four runs plus a seconds-long arithmetic fingerprint on an Apple-silicon host, still labelled MAC RUN NEEDED at the top of its own runbook. That is why the scope sentence stays as written.

And habit is measured at one crystal size, and habit is size-dependent. Sixteen of the 204 points sit close enough to a class threshold that the registered drift allowance on measurement size could move them, and they are flagged fragile — including one of the three agreements.

One number on this page was already withdrawn once. The 3 of 90 is what stands; the 5 of 90 is what the same experiment measured before the project invalidated it, and it is quoted here only as the thing that got corrected. Neither number should ever be quoted bare. What the withdrawal cost, and what is still owed, is chapter 29’s.

And no human expert has read any of it. Every review this project has had — internal and external-facing, including the two-reviewer process that checked this page — has been an LLM review. The project’s own sharing rules require that at least one human domain scientist read any manuscript before submission, and record the current position without softening it: all reviews to date are LLM reviews, and for publication that is a disclosed limitation, not a substitute. Phase 6 is in flight as this is written. Phase 7, the product layer that would turn any of this into something a person could use, has not started.

What is left, after all of those are subtracted, is small and durable. A model built from one physicist’s published measurements was pointed at the most famous picture in his field, under a marking scheme sealed in advance, and it missed — in the way he had predicted in prose and nobody had ever tested in three dimensions. Keep the two halves apart: that it would miss is his claim, published years earlier. What the miss looks like — the fourteen-to-nineteen-degree gap where the model has no habit at all, the habit destroyed by humidity — is this project’s, and it is the smaller of the two. It missed while agreeing with his own laboratory data at a temperature where that laboratory data and the diagram contradict each other. And it missed structurally, not narrowly: one crossing cannot make three boundaries, so no broad-facet parameterization could have passed — a claim that now survives the same experiment being run twice under different inputs. What is left to blame is the physics, not the arithmetic.

The findings ledger has a phrase for that combination, and it is the most exact sentence in the whole repository: the signature of a correct implementation of an insufficient theory.

Which leaves the bill. Twenty hours of compute for the run that stands, an entire earlier sweep of the same size thrown away to get it, every phase of machinery that came before both, and a verifier, a set of negative controls, a second machine and a whole second arm still owed — that is the next chapter, along with the list of things this project still does not know.
