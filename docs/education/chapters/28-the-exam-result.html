Chapter 28

The Exam Result

Two hundred and four crystals, seventeen hours, five agreements out of ninety — and not one invalid run. Which of those numbers is the result?

~16 min read 7 interactives 2 equations

By the end you will be able to

  • Hold two true statements about the same run at once: it reproduced the laboratory measurements it was built from, and it failed the map.
  • Explain why flawless numerics are the thing that lets a disagreement be blamed on a theory instead of on the code.
  • Follow a counting argument from curve crossings to habit boundaries, and say why counting is a stronger result than scoring.
  • Recognise a project aiming its honesty machinery at its own good news.

Two numbers came back

The exam was one command. node runner/src/main.ts phase6-sweep 6. It ran for about seventeen hours on six worker processes and exited with status 0, having completed 204 of 204 points on the float64 CPU engine — the slow, exact one, for the reason chapter 26 gave. Those lines are printed in the provenance table at the top of research/phase6-sweep-report.md, next to the fingerprint of the protocol it was run against and the two commit hashes that prove the protocol was sealed first.

Two numbers came out, and they point in opposite directions.

The first is the score:

“Headline: 5 of 90 headline-scope points agree.”

research/phase6-sweep-report.md, “Scored result”

Five out of ninety. In the temperature band where the reference diagram demands columns, the model produced agreement on 0 of 24 points. In the band where it demands cold plates, 0 of 60. That is not a near miss. That is a miss so total that you would normally suspect the machine was broken.

The second number is the reason you cannot suspect that:

“Zero invalid runs in 204. Every point converged under the dual criterion, held symErr = 0 with noise off, kept every per-tick attachment delta D6h-invariant, and cleared the 65% domain-contact guard. Nothing was excluded, so nothing had to be argued about.”

research/phase6-sweep-report.md, “Four findings” §2

Four checks, decided before the sweep, applied automatically to every run. They live in one function in runner/src/phase6-sweep.ts called phase6ScorePoint, and they are four lines long: allConverged, deltaSymClean, symmetryError !== 0, domainContact. Fail any one and the run is stamped invalid and, in the file’s own words, “EXCLUDED BY NAME, never dropped — … Those are runs that did not happen properly, not statements about the model.”

None of them fired. Not once, across 204 crystals grown at 34 temperatures and six humidities. In plain English: every crystal converged on both of the two independent convergence tests chapter 22 described, not just the easy one; every crystal came out with a symmetry error of exactly zero; every single growth step stayed perfectly six-fold symmetric, which is what “D6h-invariant” means — D6h is the crystallographer’s name for the symmetry of a hexagonal prism; and no crystal ever grew far enough to touch the wall of its own box.

This is what makes the failure usable. The ordinary escape hatch from a bad comparison is some of the runs were no good, so the comparison is unfair. Here there are no bad runs to point at. Nothing was excluded, so nothing could be argued about afterwards.

Chapter 15 taught you to separate two claims that sound alike, and the project’s own charter is blunt about why:

“Hold the distinction precisely, because it is the one this project exists to be honest about: giving a model real physics is not the same as showing the model reproduces reality.”

project charter.md, §1.5

So say it in the split form. The model was given real, measured physics — that claim is about where the numbers came from, and it is the claim chapter 16 spent its whole length supporting. The model reproduces the Nakaya diagram — that claim is about outcome, and it has now been tested and has failed. Both sentences are true of the same 204 runs. Neither is allowed to stand in for the other.

Key idea

A wrong answer about nature is a measurement. A wrong answer about your own rules is a defect. The 204 runs produced the first and tripped none of the four numeric validity rules — which is what lets the disagreement be attributed to the physics rather than to the program. A different rule was broken, and the very next paragraph says so: the runs used a parameter set the frozen protocol had not registered. That one was caught by review, not by the runs, and it cost the sweep.

One warning before you read the number anywhere else on this page. The score of 5 of 90 is measured, and it has since been superseded. After the sweep finished, an internal review found that the runs had used a parameter set the frozen protocol had not registered, which by the project’s own rule invalidates the sweep and forces a re-run. That story, including the fact that the corrected headline was predicted in advance to be worse, is chapter 29’s. Everywhere below, every swept number — the score, the aspect ratios, the class totals, the row-by-row grid — is a measurement from a run since superseded by the project’s own protocol rule. Read each of them as “as measured in the sweep of commit 6995868, which the project has since invalidated”.

The measured diagram

The sweep is a grid: 34 temperatures from −2 to −35 °C one degree apart, crossed with six humidity levels expressed as a fraction f of water saturation — 0.10, 0.15, 0.25, 0.40, 0.60 and 0.90. Thirty-four times six is 204. Each cell is one crystal, grown from a 19-site hexagonal seed until it reached a registered measurement size, then measured for its aspect ratio — thickness divided by width, so a low number is a flat plate and a high number is a stubby column — and sorted into one of three classes: plate (aspect ratio at or below 0.6667, which is 1/1.5), column (1.5 or above), or neutral — the model declining to commit to either. Those two numbers are the registered ones, spelled out in runner/src/phase6-protocol.ts as “AR = z-extent / T-extent; plate AR ≤ 1/1.5 (0.6667), column AR ≥ 1.5, otherwise neutral”.

Chapter 27 sealed the marking scheme before any of this ran. Three of its clauses do all the work here. Neutral counts as disagreement, because the reference names a habit in every regime and the neutral band is the model’s invention, not the reference’s. Points within 1 °C of a reference boundary do not count, in either direction. And the coldest regime, which accepts both plates and columns, is reported but kept out of the headline, because a band that accepts both answers cannot discriminate between them. Ninety points survive those rules. Five of them agree.

How we know

The measured grid is published as 34×6 characters of plain text in the sweep report. That is a deliberate choice: it means any reader can re-run the scoring rule by hand and arrive at 5 of 90 without trusting the program that produced it. Every interactive on this page that uses model results reads from that transcription, and reproduces the published per-band totals — 5/6, 0/24, 0/60, and 26/78 in the excluded mixed band.

All five wins at one temperature

Now the part where the honesty machinery turns on the project’s own good news. All five agreements are at the same temperature. The report does not bury that; it prints it in bold, immediately under the headline:

“Every one of the five agreements is at −2 °C, the single counting temperature in the warmest regime — which ADR 0025 registered pre-sweep as carrying essentially no statistical weight, precisely so this could not be presented as a result. The model agrees with the reference nowhere else inside the headline scope.”

research/phase6-sweep-report.md, “Scored result”

Read the timing carefully, because it is the whole point. The warm plate band spans temperatures warmer than −3.3 °C. On a one-degree grid starting at −2 °C, and with the ±1 °C ambiguity band removing −3 °C, exactly one temperature in that band can ever count. The scoring decision therefore wrote this down before the sweep, while the outcome was still unknown:

“A stated limitation rather than a discovered one: the warmest Plates regime has exactly ONE counting temperature (−2 °C). It can therefore only ever score 0% or 100% and carries essentially no statistical weight… Any conclusion about the warm plate regime is a one-point conclusion and must say so.”

docs/decisions/0025-phase6-agreement-scoring-rule.md, Consequences

A band that can only score 0% or 100% scored 100%. Five of the six cells at −2 °C came out plate, which is what the reference asks for; the sixth, at the wettest humidity, came out neutral, which the rule counts as a miss. That is the entire positive half of the headline: one temperature, five humidities.

And then the project undercuts its own five wins a second time. The warm end of the model rests on one digitized curve — the prism facet’s σ0 — that the project has since found suspect. Libbrecht’s own printed closed form puts those digitized anchors low by a factor of 1.6 to 3.2 at −2 to −5 °C, far outside the ±25% uncertainty the project had claimed for them. The report states the consequence in the same breath: “and −2 °C is where all five agreements sit.”

Key idea

Anyone can be honest about their bad news. The test of an honesty system is whether it fires on the good news too — and here the same document that reports five agreements is the document that says, in bold, that those five carry essentially no weight and rest on a parameter it now doubts.

The half that agrees with the laboratory

There is a second result in the same sweep, and it points the other way.

At −5 °C the Nakaya diagram demands a column. The model grew a plate, and scored zero. But −5 °C is also the subject of a dedicated laboratory paper by Libbrecht himself, and that paper reports the opposite of the diagram. His substrate experiments there found aspect ratios “throughout the range 0.1 < ρaspect < 1. Columnar crystals (with ρaspect > 1) were absent”, with many crystals “blockier, nearly isometric” and thick plates “the norm” at a typical ρ of about 0.5. His ρaspect is thickness over width — the same quantity the model calls AR.

The model’s −5 °C points land at AR 0.3821 to 1.0000: inside his measured range, columns absent. So the model agrees with the laboratory measurements and disagrees with the diagram — which is precisely what the source says a broad-facet model should do. He writes it out on the same page: “Producing columnar crystals at −5 C then requires the SDAK effect.”

Two things must be said about that agreement, and the project says both about itself.

First, the −5 °C laboratory data is upstream of the model’s own inputs. The attachment-kinetics curves the model runs on were built from Libbrecht’s measurement programme, and this paper is part of that programme. Agreement here is therefore a strong consistency check — it says the pipeline from his printed measurements to a grown 3-D crystal did not mangle anything — but it is not independent validation. The findings ledger records it in exactly those words: “The model agrees with the lab data its inputs came from.”

Second, the upper end of that range, AR 1.0000, comes from a calibration probe: a run made to help choose settings, not a run made under the frozen protocol. The project maintains a two-tier truth system for exactly this reason, and the document that prints the number prints the restriction beside it: “These are CALIBRATION PROBES and are never citable as gate evidence.” The swept, citable value at −5 °C is 0.3821.

Three explanations, two eliminated

A model disagrees with a measurement. There are exactly three places to put the blame. The code is wrong. The numbers fed into it are wrong. The theory those numbers encode is insufficient. Everything in Part Two before this chapter exists to let you eliminate the first two — not by assertion, but with specific measurements that would look different if either were the culprit.

Try it yourself. Pick a culprit and see what comes back.

The alibi for the code is not just “zero invalid runs”. It is also that the program which certifies the phase is forbidden to fail on a morphology miss. The plan writes the separation as executable behaviour, not as a promise:

“A morphology miss is not a gate failure: the gate fails on invalid numerics, provenance, artifacts, incomplete runs, or a protocol violation — never on the model disagreeing with the diagram. That separation is what makes ‘a negative result is a result’ executable rather than aspirational.”

docs/plans/phase-6-nakaya-validation.md

The alibi for the numbers has two parts, and only one of them is blind. The −5 °C reproduction is the loud one, and you have just seen why it is a consistency check rather than a proof. The quiet one is better evidence, and it is about one specific quantity: the prefactor curve Aprism was digitized off a printed figure, pixel by pixel, before anyone found the later paper that prints the same curve as an equation — arXiv:2009.08404v2, Eq. (5). Compared afterwards, the digitized Aprism anchors reproduce the printed formula to 8.4% at worst and under 2% typically. An extraction that agrees with a source it could not have peeked at is independently confirmed, not merely self-consistent. The σ0 curves got no such clean bill — that is the discrepancy the previous section reported, and it is why the alibi for the numbers is stamped “with scope” rather than clean.

Careful

“Zero invalid runs” does not mean “zero errors”. It means zero runs tripped four named validity conditions. A different kind of error — an unregistered command-line default quietly overriding a frozen parameter row — was found afterwards, by review, and cost the entire sweep. Both facts belong in the same chapter, and the second one is the more impressive.

One crossing, three boundaries

Eliminating two suspects leaves the third by default, which is a weak kind of argument. The strongest result in the whole sweep is not the score at all. It is a counting argument that says the model could not have passed, whatever the code and whatever the grid.

Here is the chain. A habit boundary — the temperature where a crystal stops growing plates and starts growing columns — requires the basal and prism facets to swap which one is stickier. Stickiness in this model is set by a critical supersaturation σ0, one curve per facet: the lower the curve, the stickier the facet, so a swap in stickiness is exactly a crossing of the two curves. So the number of crossings is a hard ceiling on the number of habit boundaries a model can produce — and that ceiling does not care about the diffusion solver, the grid spacing, the seed shape, the far-field condition or the domain size.

Three independent parameterizations of those curves have been evaluated — the project’s own digitized anchors and two printed closed forms from separate papers. Each has exactly one crossing, all in the same direction, all landing between −8.4 and −10.9 °C. The Nakaya diagram has three boundaries. One is less than three, and no amount of engineering closes that gap.

σ0,basal(T*) = 0.02·T*1.75 + 0.3
σ0,prism(T*) = 0.02·T*1.9 − 0.025(T* − 0.3)

where
T*
how far below freezing, in degrees C, written as a positive number — so T* = 15 means −15 °C
σ0
the critical supersaturation of a facet, in percent: how much spare vapour the air must carry before that face grows readily. Low means sticky.
basal / prism
the flat top-and-bottom faces, and the six side faces, of a hexagonal ice prism

Printed closed forms, arXiv:2009.08404v2 p. 3, Eqs. (2) and (3), transcribed verbatim in research/libbrecht-figure-findings.md §2

Those two printed formulas are what the next interactive draws. Nothing is fitted; they are typed in as printed. Your job is to make three habit boundaries out of them.

Switch both dips on at the depth the page starts with and the count goes to five. That number is not this page’s invention: evaluating Libbrecht’s own printed dip-bearing parameterization gives five crossings too, at −3.70, −6.25, −8.46, −9.93 and −18.62 °C, against reference boundaries at −3.3, −9.9 and −21.5 °C. Two of those land within 0.4 °C of a real boundary. And notice what the mapping does to the sweep’s failures: the columns band that scored 0 of 24 contains the basal dip at −4.5 °C; the cold plates band that scored 0 of 60 contains the prism dip at −14.4 °C; the one band where the model scored at all contains no dip.

Which is also the banner’s point. Once you drag a dip into place to make a boundary appear, you have put the answer in by hand — and the dips were originally positioned against the very diagram in question. That is why the project fixed the ceiling on its own future experiment before running it: a dip-bearing run that reproduces the diagram can only ever be called in-sample reproduction, never independent validation.

Still unsettled

The crossing is robust in sense and count, and worthless in location. Within the ±25% uncertainty the project had already registered on its digitized curves, the single crossing can sit anywhere from −6.90 to −22.89 °C — a 16 °C span that swallows most of the diagram. The nominal crossing happens to land within 0.1 °C of Nakaya’s real −9.9 °C boundary, and the project killed that coincidence itself, calling it “doubly worthless as evidence”. The falsifiable claim was restated to be the sense and the count only.

The knob that helps nothing

The obvious objection to all of this is: you just did not tune it well enough. The most plausible knob is the starting seed — every run in the project starts from the same little 19-site hexagon, radius 2, thickness 1, which is itself plate-shaped. Does the model grow plates because the physics says so, or because it started as one?

That was measured, not argued. Five seed geometries were run at the two temperatures that discriminate: −5 °C, where the reference demands a column (AR at or above 1.5), and −15 °C, where it demands a plate (AR at or below 0.6667). The answer is more interesting than a flat “no”.

Seed shape is not a small knob. It moves the aspect ratio by +0.41 at −5 °C and +0.51 at −15 °C between the registered seed and a needle-like one, and the needle-like seed does produce a genuine column — at −15 °C, where a plate is required. That is the honest, awkward version of the result, and the project states it plainly rather than claiming the knob is weak.

The reason it still cannot rescue anything is that a seed is the same at every temperature. It slides every point in the same direction, and the two failing bands need opposite moves. The seed that comes closest at −15 °C is the worst one at −5 °C; the seed that comes closest at −5 °C manufactures the wrong habit at −15 °C. Not one of the five agrees at either temperature.

Key idea

Diffusion, grid spacing, seed shape, far field and domain size can all move the aspect ratio, some of them a lot. None of them can reverse the plate/column ordering as a function of temperature — and the diagram requires that three times. The bound is about direction, not size.

The shape of the failure

A score tells you the model missed. It does not tell you how. Two facts about the shape of the miss came out of the sweep that, as far as a literature check in July 2026 found, nobody had reported before — because nobody had run a full 3-D broad-facet model across this grid.

The first is that the crossing marks where plate stops, not where column starts. Everyone, this project included, had assumed the crossing temperature is the habit-switch temperature. It is not. The last plate cell in every row from f = 0.10 to 0.40 sits at −8 °C, with the first neutral cell at −9 °C — which is why the report brackets the plate boundary as “−8/−9 °C”, and it is what the strip interactive below prints when you read it off the grid. Either way it lands essentially at the registered −10 °C crossing — and then columns do not begin until −19 °C or, at the next humidity up, −23 °C. The roughly 10 °C band in between is the model declining to commit to either habit, and it is the single largest feature of the measured diagram. On Nakaya’s side of the comparison there is no gap at all: a knife-edge boundary at −9.9 °C.

The second is that turning up the humidity destroys habit outright. Columns appear only in the two driest rows. From the third row upward there is not one column anywhere across all 34 temperatures, and at the wettest row the model produces nothing but neutral, everywhere. The direction was predicted before the sweep, from the attachment formula itself — but predicted as a bias, and measured as total.

αHK = A · exp(−σ0 / σsurf)

where
αHK
the attachment coefficient: the fraction of molecules striking this facet that actually stick
A
the facet’s prefactor, its stickiness ceiling
σ0
the facet’s critical supersaturation — low means sticky
σsurf
the supersaturation actually present at the surface, which rises as you make the air wetter

As printed in research/phase6-sweep-report.md, “Four findings” §4, explaining the measured contrast collapse

Read the formula and the collapse is obvious in hindsight. As σsurf grows, the exponential term goes to 1 and every facet’s α saturates toward its own ceiling A. The basal/prism contrast, which spans 0.34 to 3.76 across the temperature axis at f = 0.15, compresses to 0.84–1.25 at f = 0.90. The facets stop being able to tell each other apart, so the crystal stops being able to choose a habit. The uncomfortable part is where that happens: the real diagram is at its most structured exactly there, full of dendrites, sectored plates and needles.

Both facts sharpen the same lesson: crossing location and habit-transition location are different observables, and only the second is what a morphology diagram records. A model can get the crossing right and still record nothing recognisable as a habit boundary.

What this does not show

The sweep report ends with a list of what its own result does not establish, and any honest retelling has to carry that list rather than the headline alone.

It is not a test of SDAK. Every run is a no-SDAK run; in the report’s own words, “SDAK appears in no source file”. The dip mechanism has never been tested here, and if it were switched on and the diagram appeared, the rules already written would label that in-sample reproduction rather than validation.

The comparison target is a redrawn 1954 schematic. Its supersaturation axis failed an independent check when the project digitized it, which is why only its three boundary temperatures are used and its humidity values never are. No published habit boundary anywhere carries a numerical uncertainty; the ±0.5 °C the project uses is its own construction.

The habit class is what the sweep may claim; the aspect ratio underneath is not. Four convergence studies found the class robust on every axis tested, while the value beneath it converges late or, at the warm condition under grid refinement, not in any usable sense. The zone is the claim; the dot is not.

It ran on one computer. No cross-platform control has been run, so the result is scoped to the registered x64 host. Habit is also measured at one crystal size, and habit is size-dependent: 25 of the 204 points sit close enough to a class threshold to be flagged fragile.

The score has already been superseded. Chapter 29 opens with that, so it stays there — but the number on this page must never be quoted bare. It is 5 of 90 as measured in a run the project has since invalidated under its own rule, with a re-sweep pending and a worse corrected headline registered in advance.

And no human expert has read any of it. Every review this project has had — internal and external-facing, including the two-reviewer process that checked this page — has been an LLM review. The project’s own sharing rules require that at least one human domain scientist read any manuscript before submission, and record the current position without softening it: all reviews to date are LLM reviews, and for publication that is a disclosed limitation, not a substitute. Phase 6 is in flight as this is written. Phase 7, the product layer that would turn any of this into something a person could use, has not started.

What is left, after all of those are subtracted, is small and durable. A model built from one physicist’s published measurements was pointed at the most famous picture in his field, under a marking scheme sealed in advance, and it missed — in the specific shape he had predicted in prose and nobody had ever tested in three dimensions. It missed while agreeing with his own laboratory data at a temperature where that laboratory data and the diagram contradict each other. And it missed with 204 numerically clean runs out of 204, so there is nothing left to blame but the physics that was fed in.

The findings ledger has a phrase for that combination, and it is the most exact sentence in the whole repository: the signature of a correct implementation of an insufficient theory.

Which leaves the bill. Seventeen hours of compute, every phase of machinery that came before it, and a headline the project threw away on its own initiative almost as soon as it had measured it — that is the next chapter, along with the list of things this project still does not know.
