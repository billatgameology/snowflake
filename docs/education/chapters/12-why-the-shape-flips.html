Chapter 12

Why the Shape Flips

Two kinetic inputs can swap order. What does that local diagnostic establish, and what still requires a three-dimensional forward run?

~38 min read 13 figures 8 interactives

By the end you will be able to

  • State the restricted crossing test: at one shared positive surface supersaturation, equal attachment coefficients mark an order swap, not a three-dimensional habit boundary.
  • Count equality roots without treating their count as a theorem about morphology.
  • See why comparing σ0 alone, though it looks decisive, is not quite the whole criterion for a habit flip — and what that means for what a broad-facet model can and cannot be shown to do.
  • Describe what SDAK proposes: that a very narrow facet has a much lower effective barrier than a wide one of the same crystal plane.
  • Explain why the proposed reduction must be a narrow dip — shut off on the cold side by a rigid step barrier, shut off on the warm side by a fully liquid-coated surface — and why near-vacuum experiments would not see it even if it is real.
  • Locate the two model-conditioned narrow-facet reductions — basal near −4 °C, prism near −14 °C — while keeping them distinct from M1’s exact P3 dip formula.
  • Say honestly which part of this answer is measured and which part is still a working hypothesis.

A crossing is an input-order diagnostic

Plate, column, plate, column. Over the forty degrees between freezing and −40 °C a growing snow crystal changes its mind about its own shape three times, and nothing about the six-sided lattice it is built from predicts that. Chapter 7 left the alternation standing as a mystery. This chapter takes it apart, using a tool the last chapter handed over.

That chapter reduced a whole crystal face to a single number. The number is σ0: roughly, how much spare water vapour the air right next to the surface has to be carrying before a new molecular layer will start on that face. A big σ0 means the face is stubborn. A small σ0 means layers start more easily under otherwise matched local conditions.

The link from σ0 to actual growth is one equation, and it is worth looking at closely because everything in this chapter hangs on the shape of it.

alphaHK(σsurf) = A exp(−σ0 / σsurf)

where
alphaHK
the attachment coefficient — the fraction of the water molecules arriving at this face that actually stick. alphaHK = 1 means everything sticks; alphaHK = 0.001 means only one in a thousand does
σsurf
the spare water vapour in the air immediately above the face, as a percentage of what ice can hold at that temperature
σ0
the barrier for this face — how much spare vapour it takes before new layers start readily
A
the number the exponential is multiplied by. It sets the fastest this face could possibly grow, once the barrier has stopped holding it back. It is 1 on the basal facet (arXiv:2009.08404v2, p. 3, Eq. 4). On the prism facet it drops noticeably below 1 over part of the warm range — that is the lower panel of Figure 4.5, in the next section — which the monograph calls “a relatively minor issue that mainly affects fast-growing prism surfaces at high temperatures” (printed p. 148)

Libbrecht, Snow Crystals, Figure 4.5 caption, printed p. 144

The exponential is the important part. Because σ0 sits inside an exponential, a modest difference in barrier becomes an enormous difference in growth speed. At σsurf = 0.5 %, a face with σ0 = 0.35 % has alphaHK ≈ 0.5, while a face with σ0 = 2.6 % has alphaHK ≈ 0.005 — a hundredfold gap from a barrier only seven times larger. That calculation is useful, but it is deliberately local: the two facets were assigned the same positive σsurf. A real crystal solves different surface fields around a changing 3-D geometry. Barrier order alone therefore neither classifies habit nor bounds how often habit may change.

Chapter 7 described the geometric intuition. If the six side faces (the prism facets) grow much faster than the flat top and bottom (the basal facets), the crystal spreads sideways and thins into a plate. If the basal facets grow much faster, the crystal stretches along its axis into a column. Libbrecht states the proposed requirement with a double inequality, and the double matters. It is a source-level physical interpretation, not this project’s habit evaluator:

“thin snow-crystal plates only form when αbasal ≪ αprism, while slender columns only appear when αprism ≪ αbasal. Neither diffusion-limited growth nor surface-energy effects produce growing snow crystals with high overall aspect ratios.”
Libbrecht, arXiv:2306.13087v1, p. 4

Two bits of notation in that sentence. “≪” means much less than — not merely smaller, but smaller by a large factor. That is the whole point: a face that is slightly slower gives you a slightly squat crystal; only a face that is dramatically slower gives you a wafer or a needle. And aspect ratio is just how many times longer the crystal is one way than the other — a pencil has a high aspect ratio, a sugar cube has one close to 1.

Key idea

Plot σ0 for the basal and prism faces against temperature. A crossing says only that the two barrier inputs are equal. If A is also equal and both facets are assigned one shared positive σsurf, their attachment coefficients are equal there too. Those restrictions do not hold across a general 3-D crystal, so this is an order-swap diagnostic — never a habit boundary or a limit on habit changes.

Careful

It is tempting to think the plate-or-column question is settled by how much water is around — more spare vapour making one shape, less making the other. Extra vapour makes a crystal more elaborate: branches, side branches, a more feathery outline. What it will not do, acting through diffusion alone, is stretch a crystal strongly one way or the other. That is the job of the ratio of the two attachment coefficients, which is why the quoted sentence above says diffusion alone will not produce high aspect ratios at all.

Vapour can still change the answer indirectly, by tipping a crystal into a runaway that narrows one set of facets. That is what “The edge that sharpens itself” below is about, and it is why both hollow columns and thick plates are found at −5 °C.

One equality root is not a habit theorem

So far this is only bookkeeping. The physics arrives when you actually put numbers on the two curves and count.

The barrier curves for large, flat faces are the best-grounded part of the model, and their author is plain about what they are: “little more than parameterized fits to experimental data” (printed p. 145). The data being fitted are precision growth-rate measurements of the kind you met in Chapter 10, and the fits are published as smooth functions of temperature.

One label to decode before you read the next several captions. They all call the whole package the CAK model — the comprehensive attachment kinetics model, the author's own name for these barrier curves plus everything later bolted onto them. The expansion is spelled out in the caption of Figure 4.15, printed p. 155.

Two different sets of formulas for these broad-facet curves have been printed, by the same author, fitted to different bodies of data. Evaluate either one across the whole range from just below freezing down to −40 °C and solve for where prism equals basal. One set (arXiv:2306.13087v1, p. 7) crosses once, at about −8.4 °C. The other (arXiv:2009.08404v2, p. 3, Eqs. 2 and 3) crosses once, at about −10.9 °C. The two sets share the same basal formula — it is printed identically in both papers — and differ only in the prism formula. They disagree about where the crossing is. They agree completely about how many there are.

One smooth curve can conceal two branches

At −2 °C the dedicated follow-up does not give the prism facet one universal pair of numbers. Large, slow prism surfaces use Aprism = 0.25 and σ0,prism = 0.03 %. Smaller or faster facets move toward a branch labelled prism kinetic roughening, with Aprism = 1 and σ0,prism = 0.15 % (arXiv:2004.06212v1, pp. 3–4). The name is a guess about mechanism, not a diagnosis.

The two branches overlap for 0.1 % < σsurf < 1 %; experiment does not say whether the transition is smooth or abrupt. That paper ignores pressure dependence and later says some supersaturation labels in its earlier source may have been too high by a factor of two. A still later faceting model keeps the same two barrier scales but adds two nucleation terms instead of choosing one branch (arXiv:2306.04042v1, pp. 8–9). Those are two modelling policies for the same partial evidence, not a settled switch law.

Revised

There used to be a third estimate, cruder than either of these two, from the same author. An earlier paper assumed the prefactor A ≡ 1 on both facets — a simplification it named as such at the time — and its own Figure 4 put the crossing near −6 °C: “Taken at face value, these data suggest that plate-like crystals should grow at temperatures above T ≈ −6 C, while columnar crystals should grow below this temperature” (Libbrecht, “A Quantitative Physical Model of the Snow Crystal Morphology Diagram,” arXiv:1910.09067v2, p. 6). Once A was modelled properly instead of fixed at 1, the crossing moved two to five degrees colder, to the −8.4 and −10.9 °C above. That is the same author revising his own earlier estimate as the model improved — not a sign the −6 °C figure was careless, but a reminder that even this plain crossing temperature, not just the speculative SDAK dips later in this chapter, has moved before and is not pinned to the degree.

Now put that beside the diagram it is supposed to explain. Libbrecht lists the bands he needs to account for as “platelike forms near -2 C, needle-like crystals near -5 C, especially thin plates near -14 C, and columnar forms around -40 C” (arXiv:2009.08404v2, p. 6). That is four bands, which means three boundaries.

Compared in this deliberately restricted way — using the two σ0 curves alone — these particular fitted curves have one equality root. That is a property of these formulas, not a statement about 3-D habit. Humidity does not enter these curves at all; the model also assumes air pressure does not, though that is an assumption rather than a measurement — the monograph flags “an implicit assumption that the attachment kinetics does not depend on background gas pressure or other chemical effects” (printed p. 145).

But σ0 alone is not even the complete local coefficient comparison. Look back at the equation this chapter opened with: alphaHK = A exp(−σ0/σsurf). At a selected shared positive σsurf, the larger alphaHK changes when A is restored, and A is not always 1 — the equation's own gloss above already flagged that the prism A dips well below 1 over part of the warm range. Comparing σ0 alone silently assumes A cancels out of the comparison, and it does not. Put A back in and the crossing count stops being a fixed property of the two curves and becomes a property of σsurf too — the same spare vapour the slider in the last section let you move. Evaluated under that shared-field restriction, at σsurf = 0.20–0.30 % (outside that band the count drops back to two, one, or zero), these same broad-facet curves — no SDAK, no dips — swap order three times, not once. These are coefficient-order swaps, not simulated morphology transitions.

Corrected

An earlier version of this chapter counted σ0 crossings only, and called the result “structural, not numerical” and independent of diffusion. An adversarial review in 2026 found the claimed theorem invalid. Restoring A shows that even the local alphaHK order depends on the chosen shared positive σsurf (see the correction in research/libbrecht-figure-findings.md; the swap count itself is a direct evaluation of the basal and prism σ0 and A forms printed in Libbrecht, arXiv:2009.08404v2, p. 3, Eqs. 2–5). No crossing count establishes what a broad-facet 3-D model can or cannot produce. What survives is a narrower diagnostic: the three coefficient-order swaps found this way sit at different temperatures than the diagram’s three boundaries — at σsurf = 0.20 %, for instance, near 3.9, 5.0 and 10.6 °C below freezing, clustered rather than spread across the full range — so showing that three is possible is not the same as this particular broad-facet set explaining the diagram. That distinction — possible versus actually appearing in an input diagnostic versus emerging in a 3-D run — is the reason SDAK remains a live proposal rather than a settled result.

The first line of defence would be to doubt the diagram. Perhaps the three boundaries are an artefact of one experimenter's apparatus. They are not.

How we know

The broad-facet source fits and the morphology diagram use different observations. The kinetic parameterizations are inferred by fitting growth-rate experiments; they are not direct measurements of σ0. The morphology diagram catalogues shapes. That separation makes comparison informative, while the later P3 dip placement remains explicitly Nakaya-informed and therefore in-sample.

They are not measured under the same conditions either. The shapes of the barrier curves come largely from growing crystals in near vacuum (arXiv:2306.13087v1, p. 6), while the morphology diagram comes from crystals grown in ordinary air. Hold on to that difference — in the last-but-one section it turns out to matter.

A facet 200 molecules wide

Here is the assumption nobody had questioned. All of the above treats a facet as a facet: a flat patch of a particular crystal plane, with one barrier value that depends only on temperature. But look at where the flat patches actually are on a real snow crystal.

w ≈ √(8Ra)

where
w
the width of the last flat strip right at the rim — the actual facet you are growing on
R
how sharply the edge is curved, measured as the radius of the circle that fits the curve
a
the size of a water molecule, about 0.3 nanometres — not Chapter 7's a, which was the width of the whole crystal

Libbrecht, Snow Crystals, Figure 4.9 caption, printed p. 149

Put real numbers in. Measurements and theory together give edge and tip radii “often in the 1-2 µm range for a broad range of snow crystal morphologies growing in normal air” (printed p. 148). Feed that in and the last terrace comes out at “as low as about 50 nanometers on many snow crystal structures, which is less than 200 molecules across” (printed p. 148). Two hundred molecules. That is the surface on which a plate's entire sideways growth is decided.

And now the contradiction becomes sharp. Thin plates near −15 °C are one of the most common natural forms; their existence demands alphaHKprism ≫ alphaHKbasal. But the broad-facet curves in Figure 4.5 “clearly indicate that αprism ≈ αbasal at -15 C, thereby precluding the formation of thin plates” (printed p. 148). The monograph puts the choice plainly: “Either something is very wrong with the large-facet measurements, or small facets have a different story to tell” (printed p. 149).

The proposal is the second option, and it has a name:

“Delving into this discrepancy over some years, I reached the conclusion that the facet attachment kinetics must depend strongly on the local mesoscopic crystal structure, specifically the facet width w, a phenomenon I call Structure-Dependent Attachment Kinetics (SDAK).”
Libbrecht, Snow Crystals, printed p. 149

Mesoscopic is the in-between scale: far bigger than one molecule, far smaller than the whole crystal. The claim is that at that scale, size itself changes the rules.

Recall the vocabulary from the last chapter — a terrace is one flat molecular tread, admolecules are the molecules skating about on it, and terrace nucleation is the birth of a new layer, the difficulty of which σ0 measures. The captions from here on lean on all three. The sliver of width w in Figure 4.9 is one such terrace — the topmost one, fewer than 200 molecules across.

A wide face and a 50-nanometre sliver of the same crystal plane, at the same temperature, do not grow at the same rate. Why would they? The suggested mechanism runs in three steps, drawn in the first three panels below, and the figure is worth reading panel by panel.

The subtle part is what has and has not changed. The ice itself is unaltered; the energy cost of a step edge on that crystal plane is exactly what it was. What changed is the local crowd. “With a greater admolecule surface density, terrace nucleation is statistically more likely, and this results in a lowering of the effective nucleation barrier” (printed p. 150). The model-inferred effective barrier behaves as though σ0 fell, because layers do start more readily — and, crucially, “This phenomenon cannot occur on large facets, because large facets, by definition, are not influenced by edge effects” (printed p. 151).

It is worth giving that distinction a name, because everything downstream of it turns on keeping the two apart. Chapter 11’s barrier was real: the terrace step energy sets the price of a part-built layer, and nothing about being 50 nanometres wide lowers that price by a single joule. What SDAK changes is the supply side. Crowd more admolecules onto the strip and new layers are attempted more often, so more of them make it past the critical size — and a growth measurement, made from outside, sees only the outcome. A face that starts layers more readily because its surface chemistry eased and a face that starts layers more readily because its queue lengthened trace out exactly the same curve: a raised admolecule density is mathematically indistinguishable from a lowered σ0. The dips this chapter is heading toward are dips in an effective barrier. The door is unchanged. The queue at the door is longer.

The mechanism paper even puts a number on the disguise. Working the corner-to-facet flux through the nucleation model, at a surface supersaturation of about 1 % and a corner radius near 100 nm, it finds that nucleation on the narrow strip proceeds as if the supersaturation had been doubled — which, inside the exponential this chapter opened with, is the same statement as halving the effective σ0 (arXiv:2012.12916v1, p. 9). Hold on to that factor of two. The closing section weighs it against what the narrow-facet experiments seem to require, and the comparison does not flatter the mechanism.

Two more things follow, and they are what make the idea useful rather than merely plausible. First, narrower is stronger: “The narrower the facet surface, the higher the resulting admolecule density” (printed p. 151). Second, it has an on-switch. Molecules can only reach the strip if they can walk down over a step, and on a rigid lattice they mostly cannot — at the lip of a step a molecule is briefly almost detached from the surface, which costs energy. That is the Ehrlich–Schwoebel barrier, a standard effect in crystal growth.

So the mechanism has a temperature switch built into it. As the surface premelts and the top layers go disordered, the lip stops being sharp: “When the temperature reaches some critical value Tonset, when the surface disorder is sufficiently large, it stands to reason that the Ehrlich-Schwoebel barrier will become quite leaky” (printed p. 151). Below that temperature, narrow facets behave like wide ones. Above it, they do not. That is where the temperature dependence comes from.

Three regimes, and why the dip has to be narrow

Put the switch together with the mechanism and you get something better than an on-switch: you get a reason the barrier reductions should be dips rather than steps. Run the temperature from cold to warm and a narrow facet passes through three regimes. Well below the onset there is no premelting at all. The lattice is clean, the terrace steps are sharp, and the full Ehrlich–Schwoebel barrier stands at the lip of every step — so the admolecules crowding the rounded corners cannot take the last hop down onto the top terrace. However narrow the facet, the flux never arrives, and the mechanism simply does not run.

At the onset, the first places to soften are the steps themselves. Premelting does not switch on across a whole facet at once; molecular binding is weakest at a step edge, so that is where the disorder shows up first. A half-molten step no longer enforces the barrier a sharp one did, and the surface-tension push drawn in Figure 4.10 can finally drive its large admolecule flux onto the top terrace: “This inhibition is greatly reduced at the onset of surface premelting, however, which the model postulates as occurring near -14 C on the prism facet” (arXiv:2012.12916v1, p. 4). This is the sweet spot — steps soft enough to leak, the rest of the surface still rigid enough that the crowding picture holds. Strong SDAK.

Keep warming and the disorder stops being confined to the steps. The quasi-liquid layer spreads until it sheathes the entire surface, broad facets included — the fourth panel of Figure 4.10, whose caption warned you it would close off the warm side. Within that liquid-like film an admolecule does not get far before it is absorbed: the mechanism paper estimates the surface-diffusion length there at only about 20 nanometres (arXiv:2012.12916v1), too short for the corner-to-facet traffic to pile anything up, and the curvature-driven perturbation dwindles to nothing. The mechanism has not been beaten. It has been dissolved.

Count the regimes and the shape of the prediction falls out. The mechanism cannot operate in the first regime and cannot operate in the third; it lives only in the transitional window between them, where steps have softened but surfaces have not. A barrier curve that drops and then recovers — a dip, and a narrow one — is the only shape such a story can draw. Be clear, though, about what this does and does not explain. The onset temperature is postulated, not derived: nothing in the model computes −14 °C from the physics of ice, and the “Two proposed dips” section below shows the author saying in as many words that the onsets were chosen to make the diagram come out right. What the three regimes buy is the dip’s shape given its location — why the reduction is confined to a band instead of persisting all the way to the melting point — not the location itself.

It is worth saying how long this took. Basil Mason and colleagues attacked the problem from the late 1950s by measuring how far a loose surface molecule wanders before it attaches, and got roughly the right temperature trends — but, as Libbrecht puts it, “The Mason et al. model enjoyed some early popularity, but it has generally not withstood the test of time for several reasons” (printed p. 158), the technique being prone to systematic errors and the modern consensus being that layer-starting, not surface wandering, is what limits facet growth. Toshio Kuroda and R. Lacmann tried again in the 1980s, and built their model on the same premelting you have just met — the top layers going disordered as the ice warms — behaving differently on the two facets. That idea is the one that survived: “My selective positioning of the SDAK dips, postulated from facet-dependent premelting, was an idea adopted from the KL model” (printed p. 158). Standing back from sixty years of this, the monograph's own verdict is blunt — it is worth “examin[ing] just how difficult it has been to understand the enigmatic Nakaya diagram, even at a basic qualitative level” (printed p. 157).

At −5 °C, the crystal remembers how it started

The −5 °C follow-up paper turns “narrow facets grow differently” into two explicit basal branches. A broad basal face uses Abasal = 1 and σ0,basal ≈ 0.7 %. A narrow basal rim or needle tip, with radius of curvature of order 1 µm, is represented by the same A but a barrier of only 0.1 %. The author calls the narrow form a rough approximation and says a real crystal may lie anywhere between the two curves depending on facet width (arXiv:1912.03230v1, pp. 3–4).

That split changes the simple Nakaya slogan. In the paper’s full model interpretation, with broad basal and prism facets at low σsurf, targeted observations give plates at −5 °C. Columns generally require the narrow basal branch. Temperature and the current vapour supply therefore do not select a unique shape. A period of fast early growth can create a sharp basal feature, move the crystal onto the narrow branch, and leave it there while later conditions match those of a crystal that stayed broad. This is history dependence — hysteresis — not noise added after the fact. Those morphology statements come from the reported growth histories; the coefficient comparison below does not independently derive them.

What the −5 °C evidence can and cannot select

Free-fall experiments did produce strongly columnar examples, with reported aspect ratios 5.8, 5.0, 4.2, 3.7 and 3.7 at far-field supersaturations 0.5, 1.1, 2.2, 4.2 and 6.5 %. A low-supply substrate experiment instead produced a broad distribution 0.1 < H/R < 1 and no columns. But the column measurements were biased toward well-formed columns, blockier forms were rejected, and the full population distribution was not recorded. The paper calls the set imperfect and notes residual substrate interactions (arXiv:1912.03230v1, pp. 12–14).

Aspect ratio is itself a history integral, not a direct meter of the current basal-to-prism attachment-coefficient ratio. At high growth rates, latent heating at the crystal extremities becomes large; in normal air, diffusion dominates so strongly that the surface attachment coefficients are difficult to infer. The paper also assumes background gas pressure has no direct kinetic effect. Those limits are why the demo exposes the two branches but does not pretend to choose one from a photograph.

Two proposed dips, then two model-conditioned inversions

Turn the mechanism into a prediction. If narrow facets start layers more easily, then the barrier curve for a narrow facet should sit below the broad-facet curve — but only over the band of temperatures where the Ehrlich–Schwoebel barrier is leaky and the surface has not yet gone so disordered that the whole picture changes. Not a shift. A dip.

At this point a fair reader should be suspicious. The dip positions were chosen to make the answer come out right: the monograph says so, in as many words — “Here I have chosen different values of Tonset for the basal and prism facets to impose agreement with the known ice-growth behaviors from the Nakaya diagram and other growth measurements” (printed p. 152). Choosing two parameters to fit three boundaries is not, by itself, an explanation. It is a placeholder for one.

What later work adds is model-conditioned support. Dedicated growth experiments infer narrow-facet barrier reductions across temperature, but they do not directly measure σsurf or the barrier. They support the existence and approximate location of reduction regions; they do not determine M1’s exact dip shapes, depths, or widths.

One bookkeeping note before you read the two graphs, because the numbers shift by a degree. The postulated dips were placed near −5 °C and −15 °C. The model-inferred reduction regions are reported nearer −4 °C and −14 °C. These are approximate, model-dependent observational summaries. M1’s analytic dip centres are separately fixed at 4.5 and 14.4 degrees below freezing.

That same experiment connects the curve to a visible transition. The inferred basal attachment coefficient rises rapidly around σsurf ≈ 0.5 %, at the onset of basal hollowing. Across the photographic series, the lowest far-field label, 0.5 %, appears flat; 8–16 % produces deep hollows, then more complex dendritic structures. But the air experiment cannot isolate every part of the model: σsurf is inferred using a prism “witness” curve, a 1.3 correction was applied to slanted needles, and the basal branches are expected to shift with air pressure (arXiv:2011.02353v1, pp. 5, 8–10).

The witness surface is not an independent ruler

Strong diffusion prevents a meaningful direct measurement of σsurf in this one-bar experiment. The analysis assumes the broad basal behaviour is known, uses it to infer the local supply, and can therefore test mainly the ratio of basal and prism attachment coefficients. The paper allows systematic errors of perhaps a factor of two in inferred σsurf for some morphologies and says its central witness assumption cannot be independently verified inside the same experiment (arXiv:2009.08404v2, pp. 9–10, 16). The roughly tenfold narrow-versus-broad contrast supports a challenge to the broad curve within that model; its magnitude and absolute placement remain model-conditioned.

How we know

These two inversions are narrower in scope than they look. The prism reduction was inferred at a single supersaturation, so nothing in that data set says how the dip depends on the supply of spare vapour. Near the bottom of the dip the simple functional form stops working: “The measurements near the peak at -14 C do not fit the simple exponential functional form, but the fit is reasonable on either side of the peak” (arXiv:2009.08404v2, p. 15). And the dotted lines drawn through both sets of points are eye guides, not fits: the two papers that report these analyses print no equation for either dip.

Formulas for the dips do exist, in a later paper, and the chart in the next section uses them. But their shape was chosen by hand rather than fitted to these points, which is why their own author calls the functional forms “completely ad hoc” (arXiv:2306.13087v1, p. 6). The experiments support narrow-facet reductions within their model assumptions; the exact M1 shape is a P3 choice and is not pinned down by them.

Set those three beside the numbers Chapter 7 actually printed. Its table of band boundaries, measured off a published figure with ±0.5 °C read uncertainty, gave −3.3, −9.9 and −21.5 °C. As a descriptive comparison only, the warm equality root here, −3.1 °C, agrees with −3.3 °C to within that read uncertainty. The middle equality root, −8.1 °C, misses −9.9 °C by 1.8 °C — outside the read uncertainty, and a plainly weaker match than the exact hit an earlier, miscalculated version of this table reported. Be precise about what the match that remains does and does not show: the dip onsets were chosen to put the dips near −5 and −15 °C, so the bands were fitted; the count of three survives that fitting, and the warm boundary lands close, but the middle boundary does not.

The cold one does not match either, and Chapter 7 warned you in advance that the coldest boundary is barely pinned at all: the research literature sets the sequence out as “platelike again from -11 C to -18 C, and columnar again below -30 C” (arXiv:2012.12916v1, p. 1), leaving the twelve degrees in between simply unassigned. The −24.7 °C equality root here sits inside that gap, as the miscalculated version's root did too, though it now sits further from Chapter 7's −21.5 °C than that version claimed.

So: under this site’s base-10 interpretation, the evaluated equality-root count is three. Read the exercise narrowly: it verifies the implemented closed forms and shows how their order changes. It is not proof that the dips are placed correctly, not a habit simulation, and not evidence that any root becomes a morphology boundary. The reduction hypothesis rests on the model-conditioned analyses in the preceding section; the exact M1 formula remains P3.

Key idea

“the various transitions between platelike and columnar growth are caused largely by changes in the effective nucleation barriers on narrow basal and prism facets. If correct, the SDAK phenomenon thus plays a crucial role in defining the long-mysterious temperature transitions observed in snow crystal morphology.” (printed p. 153)

The edge that sharpens itself

There is one more consequence, and it is the reason snow crystals are extreme rather than merely lopsided.

A facet that grows faster piles its terrace steps closer together, which makes the top terrace narrower. A narrower top terrace has a lower effective barrier — that is the whole SDAK claim. A lower barrier means faster growth. Faster growth narrows the terrace further. That is a loop.

One symbol you met in Chapter 6 and have not needed since. σsurf, the one you have been using all chapter, is the spare water vapour right at the crystal's surface. σ∞ is the spare water vapour far away, out in the surrounding cloud — the supply the crystal is drawing on. The real word for both is supersaturation, always measured relative to ice.

With that, here is the loop in one printed sentence: “The result is a growth instability brought about by a positive feedback effect – the prism edge sharpens, σ0 decreases, the edge sharpens more, σ0 decreases more, etc. Above some threshold value of σ∞, the process runs away and a thin plate forms atop the initial blocky crystal” (printed p. 155). It is called the Edge-Sharpening Instability.

Runaways need a stopping condition, and this one has a well-defined one: “Once the instability kicks in, it often does not stop until the edge thickness reduces to a width of 1-2 microns” (printed p. 156). One to two microns. That is the same tip radius you met in Chapter 6, arrived at from a completely different direction, and it is why that scale keeps turning up across thin plates, thin-walled hollow columns and fast-growing dendrites alike.

The feedback has a pressure gate

ESI is not a surface-only switch that should run in every chamber. The molecular-mechanism paper says it operates only when growth is substantially limited by water-molecule diffusion through an inert background gas. That diffusion couples faster growth to a sharper edge and closes the positive-feedback loop; in near vacuum, the broad-facet experiments can measure attachment kinetics without automatically producing the same runaway (arXiv:2012.12916v1, p. 11).

The molecular numbers are estimates, not fitted precision constants. The facet diffusion coefficient Dfacet ≈ 10−7 m²/s could be wrong by an order of magnitude or more; the surface-diffusion length xSD ≈ 100 nm is called a rough estimate, and there is no measured numerical width at which a facet becomes “sufficiently narrow” (arXiv:2012.12916v1, pp. 7, 9). The calculation captures a plausible direction of molecular traffic. It does not yet provide a predictive transition surface in temperature, pressure, supersaturation and width.

The pressure gate is only half of the reason near-vacuum experiments come up empty. The other half is plainer: geometry. Whatever the corner traffic does, it does within roughly one surface-diffusion length of the facet edge — the same xSD ≈ 100 nm the callout above called a rough estimate — “as a substantial increase in admolecule surface density will be found only within a distance of roughly xSD from the facet edge. This covers most of the surface on a thin-plate crystal, but only a small fraction of the area on a large faceted surface” (arXiv:2012.12916v1, p. 11). Terrace nucleation is a numbers game played over the whole face: total layer starts scale with area. On the 50-nanometre rim of a thin plate, the enhanced strip is essentially the facet. On the broad prism face of a vacuum-grown crystal it is a hair-thin ribbon around the border, and the ordinary nucleation of the vast interior swamps whatever the ribbon contributes. The enhancement is not necessarily absent there. It is unmeasurable there.

Put the two reasons together and notice what they jointly imply. The crystals on which SDAK would show — narrow-rimmed plates, needle tips — are themselves products of diffusion-limited growth in ordinary air, the one environment where strong diffusion makes attachment kinetics hardest to read. Grow the crystal instead in the near vacuum where clean measurement is possible, and the facets come out broad, the edge strip is masked by area, and the feedback that would narrow it is switched off. The mechanism hides from exactly the experiments that could pin it down directly. That is genuinely an explanation — it says why decades of careful vacuum work never stumbled over the effect — but be honest about its other face: a hypothesis that predicts its own invisibility in every clean test has the shape of an unfalsifiable idea. Escaping that shape takes the indirect, model-conditioned inferences this chapter has been careful to label as such, and it is one more reason SDAK is filed here as live but unproven rather than settled.

Notice what a runaway buys you that a gradual trend does not. Snow crystals fall through clouds whose temperature changes smoothly, so you would expect their shapes to change smoothly too. They often do not.

This is not the same thing as the branching instability from Chapter 6. That one — Mullins–Sekerka — is pure diffusion, and it makes branches. It does not make thin anything: “diffusion-limited growth and the MSI alone will not produce morphologies with extreme aspect ratios, like thin plates” (printed p. 155). Edge sharpening is what you get when you add SDAK on top of diffusion, and it is what makes the difference between a chunky branched crystal and the paper-thin stellar plates people photograph. The author's own assessment of its importance is not modest:

“I believe that this growth instability is largely responsible for the remarkably robust appearance of thin plates near -15 C and the similarly robust formation of hollow columns near -5 C. If this addition to the CAK model is correct, then the ESI is one of the most important physical processes shaping the growth of atmospheric snow crystals.”
Libbrecht, Snow Crystals, printed p. 154

How much of this is settled

Every load-bearing claim in this chapter comes with a different level of confidence, and it is worth separating them.

Well established. The broad-facet σ0 points are model-dependent inversions from growth measurements, and the smooth curves are source fits; neither should be relabelled as a direct measurement of surface supersaturation. The three published morphology figures above show broadly repeated temperature-dependent habit boundaries, with differences in their exact placement. That the two broad-facet σ0 curves alone cross once is arithmetic; that this by itself proves no broad-facet model could ever produce three boundaries is not — an earlier version of this chapter claimed exactly that, and it does not survive putting the full attachment coefficient back into the count (see “One crossing, three boundaries” above). The two SDAK dips are model-conditioned inferences from growth measurements, published as data points with error bars.

A working hypothesis. Everything explaining why the dips exist. The corner-to-facet molecular flow, the leaky step barrier, the onset temperature — all of it is a physical story constructed to account for the data, not derived from first principles. Its author is explicit about that, and about where its authority comes from instead:

“Of course, the critical reader may balk at the speculative nature of this model, along with its complexity and ill-defined curves. Fair enough. What the reader may not immediately see, however, is that the model is largely driven by empirical data from a variety of ice-growth measurements. The large-facet curves are well grounded in experiments, and the SDAK dips, or something similarly complex, are required to explain a substantial body of additional data.”
Libbrecht, Snow Crystals, printed p. 153

Somebody has tried to turn that story into a number, and it is worth reading what came back. A companion paper works the corner-to-facet diffusion mechanism from “A facet 200 molecules wide” above into an actual estimate of how much it should lower σ0,prism near −14 °C, and gets “a factor-of-two reduction in the effective σ0,prism” — the barrier dropping to about half its broad-facet value. But the effective dip inferred from the narrow-facet experiments discussed above drops it to “about one-tenth of its broad-facet value”, and the same sentence calls the factor-of-two prediction “perhaps too small to reproduce the actual SDAK dip at -14 C” (arXiv:2012.12916v1, p. 9). Half versus a tenth is roughly a fivefold shortfall — that arithmetic is this site's, not the paper's — between what the proposed physical mechanism predicts on its own and the effective narrow-facet parameter inferred from those experiments. That inference depends on the growth model and its assumed surface supersaturation; it is not a direct measurement of σ0 or of σsurf. The same page draws its own conclusion plainly: “this basic picture is too simplistic to explain the observed SDAK phenomenon” (arXiv:2012.12916v1, p. 9). None of that erases the observed narrow-facet growth behaviour in Figures 4.26 and 4.27 above, but the exact dip reconstructed from those observations remains model-dependent. The comparison is a sharper, more citable measure of the gap than “completely ad hoc” curve-fitting alone: even working the mechanism forward from first principles, its own author comes up several times short of the effect he is trying to explain.

Still unsettled

The whole picture rests on the two facets premelting differently from each other. Libbrecht calls this “the only reasonable way to explain the observed transitions between platelike and columnar growth as a function of temperature” — and then immediately concedes that computer simulations tracking individual molecules do not see the difference: “MD simulations generally see similar premelting behaviors on the two facets, but the calculations may not be accurate enough to see small facet-dependent effects” (printed p. 159). The load-bearing assumption is not confirmed by the best available molecular calculations. It is not refuted by them either; they may simply not be sharp enough to see it. He adds that “no one has suggested a viable alternative that would yield a physically sensible, yet sufficiently complex, model of the attachment kinetics” — which is a reason to keep the idea, not a reason to believe it.

The source calls SDAK “a working hypothesis” and argues that it “provides the only viable option currently available” for the Nakaya diagram and other ice-growth data (arXiv:2306.13087v1, p. 5). That is the author’s assessment of alternatives, not a theorem or a project priority claim. The formulas used to draw the dips are described by their own author as “completely ad hoc” in shape; the reduction regions underneath them are model-conditioned inversions rather than direct barrier measurements (p. 6). The goal is stated without any pretence of finality: “My objective with the CAK model presented in this chapter is not to declare a full solution to the attachment kinetics problem, but rather to move the ball forward while stimulating additional progress in this area” (printed p. 159).

The implemented forward model carries the same limit in a concrete form. Premelting enters only insofar as the recorded attachment parameterizations encode it; there is no separately evolved quasi-liquid layer or explicit surface-diffusion dynamics, and latent-heat transport is omitted. None of those omissions is automatically harmless for validation. Their regime-dependent error must be bounded or reported as a systematic before a quantitative claim is made over the affected temperatures and growth conditions.

That is one published working hypothesis rather than an accepted result. Three specific things would strengthen it: showing that the two facets really do premelt differently from each other; finding what sets the shape of the dips, instead of drawing them by hand; and computing attachment kinetics from first principles instead of fitting them to growth data. Chapter 13 picks up each of those and asks how far anyone has got.
