Chapter 10

How We Know

How do you measure a crystal smaller than a grain of salt, that grows to its final shape in a few minutes and disappears if you breathe on it?

~22 min read 17 figures 4 originals

By the end you will be able to

  • Say why a snowflake that fell out of a cloud is a poor experiment, and a chamber-grown one is a good one.
  • Explain how two thermometers can set a humidity that no humidity sensor could ever measure.
  • Describe how interference fringes turn a colour, or a flicker, into a thickness — and why the white-light version is the precise one and the laser version the robust one.
  • Say what electric ice needles and plate-on-pedestal are each good for.
  • State honestly what these machines cannot reach, and why that is not a failure.
  • Tell laboratory snow crystals apart from ski-resort artificial snow, and from travel-worn natural ones.

The sky is a terrible laboratory

Everything in the last nine chapters was a claim about numbers. Facets win when the surface is slow to accept molecules. Branches take over when it stops being slow. The pattern flips from plate to column and back again at particular temperatures. Every one of those statements is a statement about a measured rate — and rates have to be measured somewhere.

Not in the sky. A crystal drifting through a winter cloud is carried by air that is always moving, through pockets that are always changing. Its temperature and its water supply change from second to second along a path nobody chose and nobody recorded. Two crystals that fall together will have taken two different routes. So a natural snowflake is a beautiful object and a hopeless experiment: you can see the answer written in its shape, but you never learn the question.

Ukichiro Nakaya, who produced “the first photographic documentation of the broader menagerie of falling snow” (printed p. 31), hit this wall almost at once. As the monograph puts it, “Nakaya quickly realized that laboratory experiments would be essential for better understanding the origin of what he saw falling from the clouds.” (Printed p. 31.) So he constructed a walk-in freezer laboratory at Hokkaido and grew his own. On 12 March 1936 he got one.

Once conditions are under your control, something becomes possible that the sky can never offer: you can run the same experiment twice. Put two seed crystals side by side on one substrate, drive them through one shared schedule of temperature and humidity changes, and they respond in step — not identically, but recognisably as a matched pair. That is the single most convincing demonstration that snow-crystal growth obeys rules rather than luck.

Key idea

A snowflake is not an experiment until somebody holds its surroundings still and changes one thing at a time. Most of what the rest of this site treats as a measured fact came out of an aluminium cylinder 7.5 cm across (printed p. 254).

Made in a machine, still real snow

Before the machine, one word. Nothing in Figure 1.23 is in doubt any more — but the word people reach for, artificial, is already taken, and it belongs to something genuinely different. Ski resorts make artificial snow: water blasted through atomising nozzles with compressed air, which cools as it expands until the droplets freeze. The result is what you would expect from freezing raindrops in mid-air — small globules of ice with no facets and no branches.

Laboratory crystals are made the other way round, grown from water vapour: molecules arriving one at a time out of the gas and attaching to a lattice, which is precisely how a snowflake grows inside a cloud. Same process, same object. The word for them is synthetic, and it means only “grown indoors”. Libbrecht's own analogy is that a Disneyland animatronic dinosaur is artificial, whereas a Jurassic-Park dinosaur would be synthetic: “It was created by unnatural means, but it is still a real skin-and-bones dinosaur.” (Printed p. 216.)

Careful

“Lab snowflakes are second-rate copies.” It is usually the reverse. Snow that reaches the ground has spent many minutes in transit: “even the best specimens are inevitably a bit travel-worn, with rounded, partially sublimated features.” In a growth chamber you catch the crystal at its peak. The monograph puts it flatly — a laboratory nursery yields “razor-sharp facets, highly symmetrical branching, and a degree of morphological perfection that is rarely, if ever, found in nature.” (Printed p. 215.)

Temperature is easy. Humidity is the monster.

A growth experiment needs two numbers held steady: how cold it is, and how much spare water vapour the air is carrying — the quantity we have been calling supersaturation, written σ. One of those is easy to the point of being boring. The other is close to impossible.

Temperature is easy because it is an equilibrium quantity. A thermometer dropped into a box drifts to the local value and then sits there, without changing what it is reading. A precision bead thermistor — a tiny resistor whose resistance climbs about 4.5 % for every degree of cooling — costs around ten dollars, is good to ±0.1 °C, and works down to −40 °C. (Printed p. 221.)

Supersaturation is not an equilibrium quantity. It exists only because vapour is flowing somewhere, and anything you put in the flow becomes part of it. In the monograph's words: “In contrast to temperature, supersaturation is an intrinsically nonequilibrium quantity, so even the presence of the measuring device can greatly affect the outcome … if a hygrometer sensor is placed inside a cold chamber, then ice will eventually condense and grow on the sensor, removing water vapor from the nearby air, and thus influencing the measurement.” (Printed p. 221.) The humidity sensor turns into a snowflake and eats the reading.

So you do not measure it. You construct it, out of two temperatures.

Here is the trick, and it is simple enough to draw on a napkin. Build a small sealed chamber. Coat the ceiling with frost and hold that ceiling at one controlled temperature. Put a single test crystal on the floor and hold the floor at a second controlled temperature. Ice sitting in air fixes the vapour density right above itself at the saturated value for its own temperature — so the frost ceiling is the vapour source, and its temperature sets the supply. The floor temperature sets what the test crystal considers “enough”. The gap between the two temperatures fixes how much spare vapour the crystal sees — the equation below turns one into the other — and it followed from two thermistor readings and no humidity sensor at all.

σ1 = [ csat(Treservoir) − csat(Tsubstrate) ] / csat(Tsubstrate)

where
σ1
the supersaturation a small crystal on the floor feels — the spare vapour, as a fraction of the amount that would sit in balance with ice
csat(T)
how much water vapour air holds when it is exactly in balance with an ice surface at temperature T; it climbs steeply as T rises
Treservoir
temperature of the frost layer on the ceiling — the vapour supply
Tsubstrate
temperature of the floor the test crystal sits on

Libbrecht, Snow Crystals, Equation 6.1, printed p. 222. It is greater than zero whenever the reservoir is warmer than the substrate.

Make the two temperatures equal and σ1 is exactly zero — not approximately zero, exactly zero, because the two terms on the top of that fraction are the same number. That built-in zero point is the reason the method is so precise. In the real apparatus the operator finds it by hand for every single crystal, nudging the reservoir temperature until the crystal is caught between growing and sublimating (sublimation is ice going straight to vapour without melting first, so a sublimating crystal is quietly evaporating away). Write ΔT for the temperature gap between ceiling and floor, and δσ for the leftover uncertainty in the supersaturation once that hunt is done. The hunt locates σ = 0 “with an absolute uncertainty of about δσ ≈ 0.001… equivalent [to] a temperature accuracy for ΔT of about 0.01 C.” (Printed p. 255.)

How we know

How do you check that a supersaturation you never measured is right? With droplets. Empty the chamber of crystals and widen the temperature gap until liquid water droplets condense on the substrate, then back off until the droplets neither grow nor shrink. At that setting the supersaturation must equal the value at which liquid water and vapour balance — a number theory hands you for free. Comparing the two gives a test with nothing to tune: as the monograph notes of the resulting plot, “the line is not a fit to the data, but shows the theoretical expectation with no adjustable parameters.” (Printed p. 255.)

The mistake that looks like nothing

Now the expensive lesson. Suppose the floor of your chamber is not perfectly clean. Suppose a scattering of tiny ice crystals came down with the seed — each about 10 microns in radius, so 20 microns across, spaced roughly 180 microns apart, covering just one per cent of the floor area. Look down through the microscope and the floor appears empty. Ninety-nine per cent of it is empty.

Every one of those specks is drinking vapour. Collectively they pull the supersaturation near the floor down to about one twentieth of the value your two thermometers say you set. The monograph is blunt about it: “one might naively think that σbottom ≈ σ1 would be reasonably accurate. But this naïve assumption would overestimate σbottom by a factor of twenty!”

σbottom ≈ (1 + 2εβL / R)−1 σ1     with   β = πR² / ℓ²

where
σbottom
the supersaturation the crystals actually feel
σ1
the supersaturation you set with the two temperatures
β
the filling factor — the fraction of the floor covered by ice. (This β is not the step energy β of Chapter 5 — the monograph reuses the letter. Here it is a bare area fraction, a pure number between 0 and 1, with no units.)
L
the height of the chamber, from floor to frost ceiling
R
the radius of one of the small crystals
ℓ
the spacing between neighbouring crystals
ε
a factor between 0 and 1 saying how close the crystals are to drinking as fast as diffusion can deliver; ε = 1 is the greediest case

Libbrecht, Snow Crystals, Equations 7.6 and 7.7, printed p. 251.

Read the bracket. The correction depends on the ratio L/R — the chamber height divided by the crystal size. That is a ratio of a centimetre to ten microns: a thousand. It is multiplied by a coverage of one per cent, and one per cent of a thousand is ten, doubled to twenty. The factor of twenty is not a subtle effect hiding in a decimal place; it is the first thing that falls out of the arithmetic, as soon as you do the arithmetic at all.

The fix is visible in the same bracket: shrink L. Bring the frost ceiling down close to the crystal and the correction shrinks with it. In the apparatus behind “some of the most accurate snow-crystal growth-rate measurements to date” (printed p. 222), the gap between reservoir and test crystal is L = 1 mm — “as small as practical”, chosen for exactly this reason (printed p. 254). Combine that with a rule of only ever putting one crystal on the substrate, and the trap closes.

One more feature of that chamber, because it changes how far its numbers travel. It is a vacuum chamber. Once a good test crystal has been found and centred, a needle valve to a pump is eased open and most of the air is drawn out, down to a pressure of about 20 mbar — roughly a fiftieth of the air pressure at sea level (printed p. 257). That is done for the sake of the zero point. With the background air gone, water vapour crosses the one-millimetre gap almost unimpeded, so the crystal answers a change in ΔT immediately instead of easing towards the new state over minutes. In the monograph's words, locating σ = 0 “can be accomplished with especially high accuracy at low pressures, when the response to temperature changes is swift” (printed p. 255). That is where the δσ ≈ 0.001 comes from.

The price is on the label, and it is worth carrying forward. This is ice growing in near vacuum, not ice growing in a cloud. Everything about snowfall you have read so far happens in air at roughly a thousand millibars, where vapour has to shoulder its way through nitrogen and oxygen to reach the crystal. So the sharpest numbers in this field — the ones the next two chapters lean on — were taken in conditions no snowflake has ever experienced. That does not make them wrong. It means they describe the ice surface itself, stripped of the air, and carrying them back out into weather is a separate step that has to be done on purpose.

Careful

It would be comfortable to treat this as a historical curiosity. It is not. Libbrecht's assessment of the older literature is that “it is possible to overestimate σbottom by even a factor of 100 if one is not careful… For this reason, I have found that most of the earlier growth measurements are generally unreliable for determining attachment coefficients.” (Printed p. 252.) Decades of published numbers were thrown out over a mistake that looked like a clean microscope slide. When you meet disagreeing measurements anywhere in science, this is the shape the explanation usually has.

Vapour crowding is not the only way a substrate can distort a measurement it is supposed to stay out of. The instrument paper behind these techniques is candid about two more, and neither is fixed by better bookkeeping. First, that hard-won zero point is not actually measured independently for the crystal in front of you: the monitor voltage marking σ = 0 “can be measured from independent measurements, but not to high accuracy”, so in practice it “is usually treated as an adjustable parameter determined mainly from the onset of sublimation in the image data, for the specific test crystal being analyzed” — a fit, chosen crystal by crystal, not a reading off a gauge. The author's own verdict on the whole extraction: “this overall analysis process is necessarily somewhat subjective” (arXiv:1912.09440v1, pp. 9, 12). Second, the substrate itself is not an inert stage. “Whenever ice crystals are grown on a substrate… substrate interactions must be considered as a possible source of systematic errors in the growth measurements”, and the usual fix — a hydrophobic coating — trades one unknown for another: “while hydrophobic and superhydrophobic coatings can decrease unwanted substrate interactions, they may also increase unwanted thermal effects, so one should proceed with caution when using such coatings” (arXiv:1912.09440v1, pp. 3, 8). Neither problem shows up as noise on a plotted curve. Both are folded into a number that looks exactly as clean as any other.

Counting flickers of laser light

You now have a crystal in a known environment. How do you watch it thicken, when the whole crystal is a few tens of microns tall and the growth you care about is a fraction of a micron?

You use the light itself as the ruler. Shine a beam straight down at a crystal sitting on a transparent substrate. Part of the beam bounces straight off the top surface of the ice; the rest carries on down through the crystal and reflects off the boundary where the ice meets the substrate. Two reflections come back, one having travelled a little further than the other — twice the crystal's thickness further. Where the two waves arrive in step they reinforce each other and the spot looks bright; add half a wavelength to the separation and they cancel, and it looks dim.

Now let the crystal grow. The extra distance creeps upward, and the reflected spot cycles dim → bright → dim, over and over. It is the cycling that carries the information, not any one brightness: every full cycle means the crystal thickened by exactly the same amount — an amount set by the wavelength of the light and by how much ice slows light down, both known. Counting flickers is measuring growth.

There are two versions of this in practice. With a plain white lamp and a grating, the reflected light is first spread out into a spectrum — a rainbow band, red at one end and blue at the other. Different colours have different wavelengths, so each colour hits the in-step or out-of-step condition at a different thickness, and the rainbow comes out crossed by dark bands. The closer those dark fringes sit, the thicker the crystal. That relationship can be turned round: measure the spacing, get the thickness — not a change in thickness, the absolute thickness, from a single snapshot.

h = λ² / (2nΔλ)

where
h
the thickness of the ice crystal — the number you want
λ
the wavelength of the light, its colour expressed as a length; visible light runs from about 400 to 700 nanometres
Δλ
the fringe spacing — how far apart, measured along that rainbow, neighbouring dark bands sit
n
the refractive index of ice: how much ice slows light down compared with empty space

Libbrecht, Snow Crystals, Equation 6.3, printed p. 229. Because Δλ sits underneath, closely spaced fringes mean a thick crystal.

This is the precise version: sub-micron accuracy on the thickness, and growth velocities readable “down to 1 nm/sec in ideal cases” (printed p. 229). A nanometre is a millionth of a millimetre; that is the resolution of the ruler. With a Helium-Neon laser instead, you lose the absolute thickness, and the growth-speed numbers come out far less precise than the white-light version (printed p. 229) — but you keep the cycling, and it still works on tall columnar crystals, where the white-light fringes have crowded together into a blur.

How we know

The data stream is less glamorous than the physics. Three images — a direct view of the crystal, the interference fringes, and a close-up of a voltmeter reading that encodes the temperature difference — are projected side by side onto a single camera sensor, so one video recording carries all three in sync. The operator narrates into the audio track while the crystal grows. Finding a specimen worth recording takes, in the monograph's words, “sometimes… 10-20 minutes.” (Printed p. 257.)

And that is the whole apparatus, so here is what falls out of it. Transcribe one video: the fringe pattern becomes a thickness, the direct image becomes a width, the voltmeter becomes a ΔT and so a supersaturation. Plot thickness and width against time, take the slope of each, and you have the speed at which the flat top face is advancing and the speed at which the six side faces are advancing — two numbers, from one crystal, under conditions somebody chose.

Two ways to hold a snowflake still

Everything so far has been one machine, and that machine has a shape. It buys its precision by working small and working thin: one prism a few tens of microns across, a millimetre under a frost ceiling, in near vacuum. What it cannot do is grow you a stellar crystal a millimetre wide, with branches and sidebranches, in ordinary air, and let you photograph the whole performance.

So put it down and pick up two different instruments. Both are built for that other job — large, complicated crystals, growing in air at normal pressure, in front of a camera — and both have to solve the same problem first: how do you hold a growing snowflake still, isolated, and in view? They answer it in opposite directions.

The first is startling the first time you see it. Take a wire, coat it in frost, put it in very humid air near −6 °C, and apply about two thousand volts. Slender ice needles spring out of the frost in a few seconds, growing tip-first along the crystal's c axis — the axis running through the two flat hexagonal ends, so the needle grows end-on — at 100–150 microns per second, so a three-millimetre needle takes under a minute. (The recipe is fussier than it sounds: the effect wants the temperature near −6 °C, a supersaturation near 100 per cent — twice as much vapour as would sit in balance with ice — and trace amounts of acetic acid vapour in the air.) Switch the voltage off and ordinary growth resumes on the needle tip. You are left with a clean single-crystal spike sticking out into open air, with a known orientation and nothing else nearby — an ideal perch on which to grow the crystal you actually want to watch.

There is a second, more basic reason for the needle, on top of the isolation just described. A seed shaped like a small prism carries two basal faces, and growing a plate from both at once risks what Libbrecht’s dedicated paper on the technique calls the “double-plate” problem: the two sides “interfere with one another’s growth, often yielding split plates and split stars.” A needle tip avoids the problem by construction — it “presents only a single basal facet”, so when a plate forms, only one emerges, cleanly, exactly what Figure 8.2 shows (arXiv:2109.00098v1, pp. 9–10).

The second technique goes the other way and pins the crystal to a window. Let small ice prisms rain gently onto a transparent plate held near −12 °C; some land flat, one hexagonal face down. Expose them to a decent supersaturation and a thin plate begins growing sideways out of the top edge of that little prism, spreading out over the substrate without touching it. The crystal ends up balanced on its own pedestal, held perfectly still, perfectly flat, and directly beneath a microscope objective. That is the plate-on-pedestal geometry, and it is what makes those long time-lapse sequences of a snowflake growing possible at all.

In practice the operator keeps the crystal within a few degrees of −15 °C, because that is where the big stellar plates grow (printed p. 334). And because temperature and supersaturation can be changed on cue while the crystal is growing, the shape becomes something you steer. That is how “designer” snowflakes are made — and how the identical twins at the top of this chapter were made, by steering two of them with the same hand.

The edge of the reachable

Every apparatus has a boundary, and saying where it is out loud is part of doing the science properly rather than just doing it.

Plate-on-pedestal has a hard ceiling on humidity, and the ceiling is made of fog. Push the supersaturation high enough and water droplets condense all over the substrate. Once they are there, they hold the air around them at the value where liquid water and vapour balance: “The supersaturation near the droplets is essentially clamped at σwater, making it nearly impossible to achieve supersaturations substantially above this level using the PoP method.” (Printed p. 345.)

You have met that ceiling before. σwater is the water saturation line from Chapter 7 — the same curve, wearing per cent instead of grams per cubic metre. And here is the part that has to be said carefully, because it is easy to get backwards: the sky has the same ceiling. A cloud thick with droplets is clamped at σwater too. The laboratory is not being beaten by a supersaturation the atmosphere can supply and the apparatus cannot.

What differs is where the fog is. In the apparatus it condenses on the substrate, immediately around the crystal, and the crystal drinks from that same patch of air — “The growing crystal absorbs water vapor in its vicinity, keeping the supersaturation below σwater nearby” (printed p. 345). So a plate on a pedestal never gets to work at the ceiling, only somewhere under it. A crystal falling free through a cloud has no substrate beside it for droplets to nucleate on, and it moves, which helps a little more: for a fernlike dendrite at terminal velocity the airflow past the tips is estimated to add “roughly a 25 percent increase in tip growth velocity” (printed p. 110). The upshot is a limit of geometry, not of altitude on a graph. As the monograph puts it, “the condensation of water droplets on the substrate prevents the supersaturation from attaining values much above σwater, and this is too low for the type of dendritic sidebranching seen in fernlike stellar dendrites” (printed p. 349) — and so “one cannot grow true fernlike stellar dendrites using this apparatus.” (Printed p. 345.) The prettiest crystal in the book falls out of the sky perfectly happily, and is out of reach of the machine that takes the prettiest pictures.

Libbrecht is equally direct that the method he uses for his most beautiful images is not the one he trusts for numbers: the plate-on-pedestal technique, he writes, “is not especially well suited for scientific measurements”, and he developed it “as a means to grow snow crystals in a more artistic realm” (printed p. 330). Different instruments for different jobs; the honest move is to say which is which.

The opposite limit is stranger. Some shapes need supersaturations so high that the atmosphere never supplies them — so those shapes have never fallen on anybody. Fishbone dendrites are the example: “the formation of fishbone dendrites requires high supersaturation levels that do not occur in nature, so they are entirely a laboratory creation.” (Printed p. 86.)

Put those two facts side by side and you get an honest map, with two limits that are not the same kind of limit. The sky and the pedestal share a ceiling — the same σwater curve, for the same reason. What separates them is delivery: the sky hands vapour to a crystal in free fall, while the pedestal hands it across a substrate that fogs first and takes its cut. That is why one of them grows fernlike dendrites and the other does not, and it is a difference in arrangement rather than in numbers. The genuinely asymmetric case is the other one: fishbone dendrites need more than the atmosphere ever supplies, so there is a band the laboratory reaches that the sky never does. Neither gap is a failure. They are the shape of the territory, and knowing the shape is what stops you from over-claiming.

Still unsettled

Precision and coverage pull against each other, and no instrument yet has both. The two-temperature vacuum chamber pins its zero point to δσ ≈ 0.001 but works on one tiny crystal at a time. The chambers that sweep whole ranges of temperature and humidity carry supersaturation uncertainties “probably not better than 20-40 percent” (printed p. 222) — and yet their results “[remain] some of the best quantitative data covering a broad range of temperatures and supersaturations in air” (printed p. 235). Holding both of those judgements at once is the actual skill. Libbrecht's own summary is worth keeping: “If there is one thing I have learned with great certainty in this field, it is that ice growth exhibits a mystifying variety of complex growth behaviors that will require much additional work to appreciate.” (Printed p. 280.)

You can start this winter

None of this needs a laboratory to begin. The equipment for real observation is a folding magnifier and a piece of card.

Get a cheap fold-up loupe — “A magnification of 4X or 5X is about right, as this provides a reasonable amount of detail with a fairly wide field of view” (printed p. 445) — and a sheet of dark-blue foam-core board. Let the board get properly cold outside before you use it, then catch crystals on it in mid-fall and look at once. Catching them matters: once flakes have landed on the ground “they will stick together and soon metamorphose into clumps of crystals with much changed morphologies.” (Printed p. 445.) The matte dark surface makes individual crystals easy to pick out, and the loupe tells you in a second whether one is worth a closer look.

Then use what you already know to decide when to go out. If you want the big six-armed stellar crystals, you want a snowfall coming from cloud near −15 °C — “such crystals can only be found when the cloud temperatures are around -15 C.” (Printed p. 383.) Most of what falls, most of the time, will be irregular, or rimed — crystals that collided with supercooled cloud droplets on the way down and froze them on as a crust of bumps. That is not bad luck; that is the honest population. Reading the sky before you go out, and then checking the prediction on a piece of card, is exactly the same activity as everything else in this chapter, scaled down to one person and one snowfall.

Key idea

Almost nothing in this chapter was measured directly. The width of the crystal you can just look at. The thickness and the humidity you cannot — and those are the two that mattered. Both came instead from something easy: a count of flickers, two thermometer readings, whether a droplet was growing. Then physics converted the easy measurement into the hard quantity. That substitution is what experimental science mostly consists of.

The isolated crystal, the two thermometers, the counted fringes: put them together and out come growth speeds, one for each kind of face, of the sort plotted in Figure 7.11. But a growth speed is not yet an answer, because it mixes two things together — how much vapour arrived at the surface, and how much of what arrived actually stayed. Separating them takes one short equation and one number: the odds that a molecule which hits the ice sticks to it.
