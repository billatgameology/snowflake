Chapter 8

A Snowflake Is a Record

The morphology diagram only works if the weather holds still. So what does a crystal that fell through changing weather actually look like?

~18 min read 14 source figures 3 originals

By the end you will be able to

  • Explain a crystal's shape as a path across the morphology diagram rather than a point on it.
  • Read a capped column as a two-part growth story, and say which part came first.
  • Explain why the six arms match without any signal passing between them — and why the fine sidebranches do not match.
  • Give the precise version of “no two alike”, including the case where two crystals really do match.
  • Recognise ridges and ribs on a crystal's surface as a written record of what happened to it.

A map for weather that holds still

The last chapter handed you a map. Temperature across the bottom, spare water vapour up the side, and four bands telling you what shape you get: plates, then columns, then plates again, then a region where both appear. It is a good map. But it comes with a condition printed on it, and the condition matters more than it first looks. In the source's own words, the chart “provides just a rough approximation of the different morphologies, plus it applies only if the growth conditions are constant in time” (Figure 10.1 caption, printed p. 383).

Constant in time is not what a cloud does. A growing crystal is not parked at a fixed altitude in still air. It is carried around by drafts, lifted, dropped and blown sideways through air whose temperature and humidity change from metre to metre. Over the minutes it takes to grow, the conditions around the crystal change repeatedly. So the crystal never sits at a single point on the map. It drags a line across it.

That single shift — from point to line — is the whole chapter. It turns the crystal from a thermometer reading into a recording. Every structural feature on the finished crystal was laid down at a particular moment, under whatever conditions happened to be true at that moment, and it stayed there while the crystal moved on. Nothing gets overwritten. The crystal grows outward, so the oldest ice is in the middle and the newest is at the tips, and the whole object is a stack of moments in order.

Key idea

A snow crystal's shape is not a reading of where it was. It is a recording of where it went.

Libbrecht puts it directly: “The final shape of the branch, therefore, reflects on the entire history of its growth, which was determined by the meandering path it took through the atmosphere” (printed p. 21). And a page later, as the summary of the whole introduction: “the detailed shape of any snow crystal is determined mainly by the environmental conditions it experienced as it grew” (printed p. 23).

Nakaya — whose diagram you met in the last chapter — had a phrase for this. He liked to remark that snowflakes are “hieroglyphs from the sky.” The idea is that with the diagram in hand you can run the reading backwards: from the shape on your sleeve to the conditions in the cloud it came from, “like a kind of meteorological hieroglyphics” (printed p. 32). Hold on to that image, and also hold on to the fact that hieroglyphs are hard to read and often ambiguous. We will come back to how far the reading can honestly be pushed.

Reading a capped column

Here is the cleanest example of a two-part story, and it is common enough that you can find one yourself in a decent snowfall. A capped column is a stubby hexagonal column with a flat plate stuck on each end, like a cotton reel or an axle with two wheels.

Last chapter told you where each of those two shapes comes from. Columns are what grows in the band around −5 °C. Plates are what grows in the band around −15 °C. A capped column is both. No temperature in the plate band makes columns and no temperature in the column band makes plates, so the two halves of this crystal were made in two different bands. (There is a mixed band below −21.5 °C where both habits turn up, but neither half of this specimen looks like it was grown that cold: the column is a −5 °C column and the plates are −15 °C plates.) So it has to be a sequence: the crystal grew as a column while it was in the warmer band, then the wind carried it somewhere colder, and plate growth took over.

And you can tell which half came first, from geometry alone. New ice can only attach to ice that is already there. The plates are on the ends of the column, which means the column already existed when the plates started spreading. Had the order been reversed, the later growth would have had nowhere to go but out from the middle of an existing plate. The capped column is a sentence with its words in a readable order.

Why the six arms match

This is the question everyone asks, and the answer is genuinely surprising: nothing coordinates them. No signal passes between the arms that carries the pattern.

A stellar crystal is one object, a few millimetres across, falling as a unit. Its six arms, in Libbrecht's phrase, “all travel together through the cloud” (printed p. 21). Whatever happens to one arm — a rise in temperature, a plunge into denser cloud — happens to all six at the same instant, because they are all inside the same couple of millimetres of air. Each arm responds to that change on its own, by the ordinary rules of faceting and branching from Chapters 5 and 6. Six independent responders, one shared input, six matching outputs.

The source is explicit about what does the work: “the growth of the six branches is not synchronized by any communication between them, but rather by their common history. And because no two snowflakes follow exactly the same path through the turbulent atmosphere, no two look exactly alike” (printed p. 21).

Careful

It is often said, or implied, that the six arms somehow coordinate — that a signal runs through the crystal keeping them in step, or that the lattice “imposes” the pattern. Neither is true. Nothing travels between the arms that tells one what the others are doing. They do share the air around them, and they compete for it — that competition is part of why arms end up unequal in length — but competition is not a message, and it cannot copy a sequence of features from one arm to the next. The lattice, for its part, fixes the geometry — six directions, 60 degrees apart — and nothing more. The matching pattern along the arms comes only from the fact that all six were in the same place at the same time.

You can test this claim rather than take it on faith, because you can arrange the same experiment on purpose. Grow two separate crystals side by side on the same surface, then change the temperature and humidity around both of them at the same moments. If shared history is really the whole story, two crystals with a shared history should come out matching — even though neither can possibly know what the other is doing. That experiment has been done. We will come to it at the end of the chapter, because it is also the loophole in “no two alike”.

The same logic explains a crystal like the one below, which was not made by the weather at all. It was made in a laboratory by a person turning knobs. Its complexity is not an accident of a lucky journey; it was written into the crystal on purpose, one condition change at a time, over about forty-five minutes (printed p. 22).

That method has a name, and because it appears in several of the published captions ahead it is worth knowing: the Plate-on-Pedestal technique, PoP for short — a crystal grown on a small pedestal of ice above a transparent slide, so the experimenter can watch it from underneath and change the temperature and the humidity around it whenever they choose (printed p. 329). That is the whole point of the arrangement: it turns the growth conditions into something a person can schedule. Chapter 10 shows the apparatus, and what it cannot do.

Where the symmetry runs out

If shared history explains the matching, then anything the arms don't share should not match. That is exactly what happens, and it happens on the same crystal, at a smaller scale.

The big structure of an arm — where it widens into a plate, where it necks back down — is driven by the environment, which is shared. The fine sidebranches are not. A sidebranch starts as a tiny random bump on the side of a growing branch tip, which then runs away under the branching instability from Chapter 6. Where those bumps happen to occur is a matter of chance, and chance is not shared. Libbrecht is blunt about the result: because the perturbations “were somewhat random in nature, the sidebranches are largely uncorrelated among the primary branches, and even the two sides of a single primary branch exhibit quite different sidebranches. Thus, the detailed six-fold symmetry of the crystal in Figure 3.8 is quite poor” (printed pp. 83–84). That crystal is the next figure — look at it while you read the sentence again.

So one mechanism produces both the order and the disorder. Shared signal, shared structure; private noise, private detail. A stellar crystal is symmetric when you squint and asymmetric when you look closely, and that is not a flaw in the explanation — it is a prediction of it.

That is not quite the last word, and the same passage supplies the exception: “It is possible, however, to stimulate symmetrical sidebranches with not-so-random perturbations” (printed p. 84). A shove big enough — a sharp jump in conditions, whether the weather delivers it or an experimenter dials it in — can set all six arms sidebranching at the same moment, and then even the fine detail lines up. That is the difference between the two crystals you have now seen: the laboratory crystal in Figure 1.11, whose matching sidebranch clusters were scheduled, and the natural crystal in Figure 3.8, whose sidebranching was left to chance.

There is a bigger correction hiding here too. Well-formed six-fold stars are the crystals that get photographed, which makes them look far more typical than they are.

The writing on the surface

So far the record has been written in the crystal's outline. But there is a second layer of writing, on the flat faces themselves. Light a plate crystal from the side and its surface turns out not to be blank: it carries raised lines, hexagonal rings and shallow hollows, repeated six times around.

Two of these markings are worth telling apart, because they record different things.

Ridges are the raised lines that run outwards from each of the six corners. They do not need any change in the weather — they appear during ordinary steady growth near −15 °C, on whichever of the plate's two large faces is gently domed rather than perfectly flat, because the corners of each molecular terrace collect vapour slightly faster than its flat parts. That is the runaway bump of Chapter 6 all over again, one molecular terrace at a time — the source calls it “a two-dimensional manifestation of the Mullins-Sekerka instability” acting on the molecular steps (printed p. 119). In the laboratory the domed face is reliably the underside, and for a reason peculiar to the laboratory: the vapour is supplied from above the substrate, so “the upper surface of a POP crystal stays flat or slightly concave, containing no ridges. But the bottom surface is convex” — convex meaning domed outwards, which is the shape ridges need (printed p. 120). It helps that the shielded lower surface also “grows much more slowly” (printed p. 119). A plate falling freely in a cloud has no substrate under it, so ridges there “may appear on one, both, or neither of the basal surfaces” (printed p. 120). What makes ridges a record is where they end up. A ridge starts at a corner of the plate and gets left behind as the plate grows past it, so the ridge line marks the spot where that corner used to be. String all those spots together and the ridge is, quite literally, a graph of corner position against time.

Ribs are different, and they are the ones that matter for this chapter. They are hexagonal rings where the ice is a little thicker than the plate around them, and unlike ridges they cannot form under steady conditions at all. In the source's words: “While ridges and inward-propagating rings readily appear under constant environmental conditions, other common snow crystal structural features require changing conditions for their formation. One prominent example is the creation of hexagonal ‘ribs’” (printed p. 125).

The mechanism is a three-step sequence. While the air is very humid, the plate grows with a thin, sharp edge. Drop the humidity and the edge stops being sharp: growth slows and the rim thickens. Raise the humidity again and thin-plate growth restarts from the top of that thickened rim, leaving the thick rim stranded behind as a raised hexagonal ring on the underside (printed pp. 125–126). One dip in humidity, one rib. A run of ribs is a run of dips, in order, spaced according to how fast the crystal was growing between them.

How we know

This is not read off a photograph and argued backwards — it is done on demand. In the laboratory a rib can be planted wherever you want one. The recipe for the crystal in Figure 9.28 below was “lowering σ [the spare-vapour measure from Chapter 4] for about thirty seconds to thicken the plate edge, and then raising σ back up again to continue the thin-plate growth” (Figure 9.28 caption, printed p. 354). The words in square brackets are our gloss, not Libbrecht's. Repeat the recipe on a timer and you get evenly spaced ribs like a spider's web. Being able to write the feature is much stronger evidence than being able to explain it after the fact.

Natural crystals carry the same markings, but the transcript is far harder to read, because nobody wrote the schedule down. A crystal that tumbles as it falls can pick up ribs on both of its flat faces at once, and the result is a pile-up: one natural specimen is described as showing “a cacophony of rib-like features that reflect the ever-changing and unknown conditions in which it grew” (printed p. 126). Note that word unknown. It is doing honest work.

No two alike, precisely

Now the famous claim. It is not a myth, and it is not quite a fact either — it is a statement that only becomes true or false once you say what “alike” means. There are three useful answers, and they do not agree.

Alike down to the last molecule? Then no, essentially never, and the reason has nothing to do with shape. Ordinary water is not chemically uniform. About one water molecule in 5,000 carries a deuterium atom — a hydrogen atom with an extra neutron in its nucleus, so slightly heavier than usual — in place of a normal hydrogen, and about one in 500 carries oxygen–18, a heavier version of the ordinary oxygen–16 atom (printed p. 42). A typical small snow crystal contains something like 1018 water molecules, of which roughly 1015 are one of these odd ones, scattered through the ice at random (printed p. 43). Two crystals matching in every one of those positions is so unlikely that, even at around 1024 snow crystals made per year, it has effectively never happened. The same argument then runs a second time on the crystal's dislocations — the places where the molecules did not stack together with perfect regularity, which are also scattered at random. The one loophole is a crystal of only a handful of molecules, which can easily be isotopically clean and defect-free — “but perhaps six molecules does not a snowflake make” (printed p. 43).

Alike under a microscope? Then yes, easily — if the crystals are simple. A plain hexagonal plate has almost nothing about it that can vary except its size. Libbrecht notes that if you sifted through enough Antarctic snow, where the cold, dry climate produces simpler shapes, “you would find two that were optically identical under a microscope” (printed p. 43). He also has a photograph of two that landed side by side in his own laboratory.

Alike, and complex? Now the slogan is on solid ground, and the reason is counting. Look closely at an elaborate crystal under a microscope and you can pick out something like a hundred separate features — a widening here, a sidebranch there, a rib at this radius. Each one could have come out differently, or landed somewhere else. The source makes the point with books on a shelf: three books can be arranged six ways, fifteen books over a trillion ways, and a hundred books “just under 10158, which is about 1070 times larger than the total number of atoms in the entire known universe” (printed p. 43).

Careful

“No two snowflakes are alike” is usually offered as a fact about snow. It is really a fact about complexity, and it needs the word complex to be true: it is “virtually impossible that any two complex snow crystals, out of all those made over the entire history of the planet, have ever looked exactly alike” (printed p. 44). Drop that word and the claim is false — simple plates match all the time. The refined version is the more impressive one, because it tells you why: uniqueness comes from the number of independent things that had to go one way rather than another, not from anything mysterious inside the ice.

And then the deliberate loophole, which is also the best evidence in this chapter. If a crystal's shape really is set by the conditions it met as a function of time, then two crystals given the same conditions as a function of time ought to come out the same. That is a testable prediction, and it has been tested by growing two seed crystals side by side on one surface and putting them both through the same schedule of abrupt changes.

How we know

The trick that makes twins work is instructive. “A key trick for growing identical-twin snow crystals is to make lots of large, abrupt changes in growth conditions… Smaller, gradual changes typically yield less perfect symmetry between the two crystals, or even among the six branches of a single crystal” (printed p. 358). Big shared shoves drown out the small private differences — stray temperature gradients, each crystal disturbing its own neighbourhood. That is the shared signal beating the private noise, exactly as in the six arms, and it is why the twins confirm that growth is “largely deterministic, meaning that two crystals experiencing the same conditions as a function of time will grow into nearly identical shapes” (printed p. 383).

What the record does not say

It would be easy to over-sell all this, so here is the ceiling. Reading a snow crystal is not like reading a data logger. You get one look, at the finished object, after the journey is over.

Three limits are worth naming. First, the map itself is coarse: it “provides just a rough approximation of the different morphologies” (Figure 10.1 caption, printed p. 383), and several different versions of it have been published by different investigators (Figure 1.25, printed p. 32) — the figure below puts three of them one above the other. Second — and this one follows from the geometry rather than from anything Libbrecht writes down — the crystal does not record time directly. It records distance grown, and converting one to the other means knowing how fast it was growing, which you generally do not. Third, and most honestly, the source's own summary of the exercise: “there are limitations to how much one can say about the growth history of a snowflake by examining a single photograph of its final state; but usually one can envision a plausible scenario to explain an observed morphology” (printed p. 383).

A plausible scenario. Not a measurement. That is the right weight to give a reading like “this one started near −5 °C and finished near −15 °C” — a well-supported story, consistent with the evidence, not a transcript with timestamps you could check.

Still unsettled

Notice what this chapter did not do. Everything here works just as well whichever way round the plate and column bands run, so the question you were left holding at the end of Chapter 7 — what physically flips ice between growing flat and growing tall, three times, as it gets colder — is untouched. Chapter 12 is where that answer, so far as there is one, lives.

You arrived with two questions, and you now have both answers. Why six sides: Chapter 3. Why no two alike: this chapter — one meandering path through the cloud, six arms reading it at the same instant, and twins on demand the moment somebody copies the path. That is a fair place to feel finished, so here is why not to be. One question is left, and it is the one nobody has finished answering: what flips the shape three times as the air cools. The next five chapters walk you out to the edge of it. First the crystals the photographs leave out — most of what actually falls is plainer, stranger and messier than a well-formed star, and any real answer has to cover those too. Then how the measurements are actually made. Then the answer, such as it is. And finally, in Chapter 13, the graph the whole thing rests on with its lines named, so you can decide for yourself which one is a measurement and which one is a hand drawn through the points to guide the eye.
