Chapter 5

The Restless Surface

Why does ice bother to be flat-sided at all — and why is the usual explanation for it the wrong way round?

~20 min read 13 figures 4 animations · 1 chart

By the end you will be able to

  • Describe an ice surface as a staircase of molecular terraces separated by one-molecule steps.
  • State the counterintuitive core of faceting: flat faces survive because they are the worst at growing.
  • Define the attachment coefficient, written alphaHK in this project, and say what nucleation barrier stands behind it.
  • Rule out the “lowest surface energy” explanation of faceting using three independent checks.
  • Explain why a snow crystal at −5 °C is a rigid lattice wearing a slushy skin.
  • Separate crystallography — solved — from crystal growth, which is not.

The last chapter delivered the fuel. Air that is holding more water vapour than ice can tolerate pushes molecules onto the crystal, and diffusion decides how much arrives where. But arriving is not the same as joining. A water molecule that lands on ice can settle into the lattice, or it can skitter about and leave again. This chapter is about that decision, because the decision is made differently on different faces — and that difference is the entire reason snowflakes have flat sides.

A staircase, not a wall

Zoom in on a growing ice face far enough and it stops looking like a wall. It looks like a staircase. The crystal is built of flat molecular layers, called terraces, stacked on top of one another. Where one terrace ends and the layer beneath it is exposed, there is a step, exactly one molecular layer high — on the basal face that layer is a bilayer, two molecules thick, which is why its spacing is quoted below as c0/2 rather than c0. The rise of each step is tiny: the spacing between basal layers is 0.37 nanometres and the spacing between prism layers is 0.39 nanometres — roughly a third of a millionth of a millimetre either way (printed p. 53).

This matters because of where a newly arrived molecule can find neighbours. Out in the middle of an open terrace, a molecule sits on top of a flat floor. It is touching the layer below and almost nothing else — a weak grip, easy to break, and off it goes. At the edge of a step, the same molecule is touching the floor and the vertical wall of the terrace above. Two contacts instead of one. It stays.

Key idea

Molecules do not stick to surfaces. They stick to steps. A face with no steps on it has almost no way to grow.

Now the crucial part: not every plane through the lattice produces a good step. Slice the hexagonal ice lattice along the flat top and bottom of the crystal — the basal planes — or along the six side walls — the prism planes — and the steps come out distinct and clean-edged. Slice it instead along a third family of planes, which crystallographers label {112̄0} and say aloud as “one-one-two-bar-zero”. The label is just an address for one particular way of cutting the lattice — the bar is part of the notation, not a subtraction, and you do not need to decode the digits to follow the argument. Cut there and the steps come out shallow and indistinct. The lattice barely notices.

The consequence is stark. On a prism or basal face, growing means starting a brand-new terrace from nothing — assembling a small island of molecules on a flat floor, with every molecule in that island poorly held until the island is big enough to hold itself together. That is a genuine barrier, and it is called a nucleation barrier. On the {112̄0} surfaces there is no such barrier, because there is no meaningful step to make. As the monograph puts it, “the larger terrace steps on the basal and prism surfaces creates a substantial nucleation barrier that promotes strong faceting on these surfaces”, while “the fact that the terrace steps are relatively shallow on the {112̄0} surfaces… likely explains the absence of faceting on these surfaces” (printed pp. 53–54).

None of this is a thought experiment. Terrace steps have been photographed directly on real ice, and they behave exactly as the picture demands: new terraces appear, then spread sideways across the face until they run out of room.

Watch one of those lines being born. A few molecules meet by chance on the open floor of the facet and form a tiny island. Almost always it falls apart again, because the molecules round its rim are barely held. But there is a size above which the island is stable — the critical radius — and the step energy sets it: the more it costs to make an edge, the bigger an island has to be before keeping it pays. That cost is a measurable quantity in its own right — the section “The price of an edge” puts a number on it, and the note beneath the animation below quotes that number in advance. Crowd more spare vapour onto the surface and the critical size drops. At a surface supersaturation of about 1 per cent, using the measured ice step energy, a barely-stable island has a radius of about ten molecular widths and contains roughly 300 water molecules in all (printed p. 142). Below that it dissolves. Above it, it sweeps the entire face. The same thing happens on a basal face and on a prism face; the drawing below takes a prism facet and looks straight down onto it.

That is the whole engine of facet growth, and it is why a facet is slow. When the critical size is large, almost every island that forms is too small to survive, so successful events are rare and the face spends most of its time waiting. Hold on to that stalled picture: two sections from now it turns into a single number.

The slowest faces are the ones you see

Here is the sentence that most popular accounts get backwards. You see the flat faces because they are the worst at growing.

Water vapour molecules strike the crystal everywhere, more or less evenly. Whether they stay depends on where they landed. A molecularly rough patch — jagged, full of half-finished bonding sites — catches almost everything that hits it. A facet, aligned with the lattice and molecularly smooth, catches very little. So the rough patches race outward. And a surface that races outward runs out of itself: it grows until it collides with its neighbours and disappears from the outline. What remains, once the fast surfaces have eaten themselves, is the slow one. The facet.

You can test the logic yourself. In the animation below, a lumpy seed crystal grows. Every direction on its surface advances at its own speed: the six facet directions advance at a speed you control, everything else advances at full speed. Slide the facets up to full speed and the crystal grows into a blob. Slow them down and a hexagon emerges — not because anything drew a hexagon, but because the slow directions are the last ones standing.

This is a general rule about crystals, not a fact about ice. Quartz shows striking flat faces; copper almost never does, even though both are crystalline. The lattices differ, but that is not the reason — the reason is what happens to a molecule when it lands. As the monograph asks and answers: “quartz often exhibits striking faceted features that reveal its lattice structure, while copper rarely does. Why? The answer lies in… the surface attachment kinetics” (printed p. 29).

Putting a number on stickiness

“Sticks more” and “sticks less” are not measurements. The quantity that makes them one is the attachment coefficient — this is Libbrecht's Hertz-Knudsen coefficient, written alphaHK in this project: the probability that a molecule striking the surface actually joins the lattice and stays. It runs from zero — nothing sticks — to one, where every arrival is captured. The whole collection of surface processes that set it is called the attachment kinetics.

With alphaHK in hand, the growth of any patch of surface is one short equation.

v = alphaHK · vkin · σsurf

where
v
how fast that patch of surface moves outward
alphaHK
attachment coefficient — the fraction of arriving molecules that actually join the crystal, between 0 and 1
vkin
a reference speed set by temperature and by how heavy a water molecule is — the speed the surface would advance if every arriving molecule stuck and the air carried 100 % spare vapour. The real speed is that figure multiplied by both alphaHK and σsurf, so it is always very much smaller
σsurf
the supersaturation right at the surface — how much spare water vapour the air immediately above that patch is carrying, measured against what ice can hold

Libbrecht, Snow Crystals, printed p. 93 (Eq. 3.8)

Read it as a product of two things you have now met separately. Chapter 4 supplied σsurf: the fuel actually delivered, after diffusion has taken its cut. This chapter supplies alphaHK: the surface's willingness to accept it. Fuel without willingness grows nothing. Willingness without fuel grows nothing. The shape of a snowflake is decided by the product. Most of what follows on this site is an argument about one factor or the other.

A facet is a surface with small alphaHK. A rough surface has alphaHK close to one. And the reason alphaHK is small on a facet is the nucleation barrier you met at the start of this chapter: to move at all, the facet must first build a new terrace out of nothing. That already sorts every surface on a snow crystal into three kinds.

The price of an edge

How big is that barrier? Put a number on it, and the number is the step energy — the energy cost of creating one metre of terrace edge.

The definition is concrete enough to picture. Take a flat island of molecules sitting on a facet and cut it in two. You have to break the bonds along the cut, and the molecules along the two new edges are now exposed. The energy you paid is proportional to the length of new edge you made. Energy divided by length is the step energy, written β (“beta”): “the step energy is the amount of energy needed to create the edge, or step, of a molecular terrace on a crystal facet” (printed pp. 59–60).

The numbers are tiny, as they should be for something measured per metre of a molecular ledge. For ice growing from liquid water, the basal step energy near the triple point is about 5.6 × 10−13 joules per metre (printed p. 60). For ice growing from vapour — the snowflake case — the monograph quotes a representative value of roughly 10−12 joules per metre from the measurements below (printed p. 65). Both are far smaller than the roughly 3 × 10−11 joules per metre you would predict by naively snapping bonds in a rigid lattice (printed p. 62, Eq. 2.16), because real surface molecules relax: they lean into the gap and soften the edge, and a softened edge is cheaper.

How we know

Nobody photographs a step edge and reads off its energy. The ice/vapour step energies were obtained indirectly: measure how fast a facet grows when growth is limited by the need to nucleate new terraces, then invert classical nucleation theory to recover the barrier that would produce that rate. The monograph reports them “measured with good accuracy at temperatures ranging from −2 C to −40 C”, while noting in the same breath that “the potential for systematic errors is significant” and that whether the data survive the test of time remains to be seen (printed p. 60). Take the shape of the curves seriously; hold the last decimal place loosely.

One more honest caveat before moving on. The prism curve's collapse near the melting point has often been described as a roughening transition — a sharp switch at which the surface stops being smooth. The measurements do not show a switch. They show a slide: “this phenomenon is better interpreted as a gradual reduction, not an abrupt transition” (printed p. 60). And only the basal and prism step energies have been measured at all. The pyramidal facets, which show up rarely and only in very cold conditions, have never had theirs measured (printed p. 54).

A rigid crystal in a slushy coat

Figure 2.15 left a debt: why should a warmer lattice relax more and charge less for an edge? The answer is that near the melting point the ice surface stops being a lattice at all.

Every lattice sketch so far has drawn the ice surface as a tidy row of molecules parked in their assigned places. At the temperatures where snow actually forms, that is a fiction. Thermal motion distorts the top layers, molecules leave and rejoin at a prodigious rate, and the monograph issues the warning directly: “real ice surfaces are neither rigid nor static” (printed p. 54).

The most dramatic version of this is surface premelting. A molecule deep inside the crystal is surrounded on all sides. A molecule at the surface is not — it has neighbours below but open space above, so it is less tightly held. Near the melting point that looseness wins, and the top layers give up their ordered arrangement entirely while the crystal underneath stays perfectly solid. The disordered film that results is not quite water and not quite ice. It is called the quasi-liquid layer, or QLL.

Michael Faraday predicted it in 1859, long before anyone had a way to look. Working out what it actually is has occupied a great deal of recent research.

How thick is it? Around −10 °C the disordered film is roughly one molecular layer deep. From there it keeps thickening as the temperature rises, and it never levels off — but it thickens slowly, needing each further step closer to 0 °C to be a smaller and smaller step. Physicists say the thickness diverges logarithmically (printed p. 55). It becomes especially pronounced above −1 °C. Go the other way and it fades out: at the coldest end of the measured range, “surface premelting is essentially absent” (printed p. 62), and the lattice stiffens until it charges nearly the full rigid-lattice price for a terrace edge.

So the picture to carry is this: a snow crystal growing at −5 °C is a rigid, perfectly ordered lattice wearing a thin, slushy, disordered coat. And the terrace steps that control everything in this chapter are not sitting at the outer edge of that coat. They live at the boundary underneath it, where the true ice meets the quasi-liquid layer: “the location of the ice/vapor step energy must be at the ice/QLL interface” (printed p. 61). That has a consequence you can check against Figure 2.15, a few paragraphs above. If the steps really sit against a film that is behaving more and more like liquid water, then as 0 °C is approached the ice/vapour step energy ought to slide toward the ice/water step energy — and the measurements “do seem to show a good correspondence” (printed p. 61).

Still unsettled

It has long been suspected that premelting is the hidden hand behind snow crystal behaviour — perhaps even behind the whole pattern of which shapes form at which temperatures. It is a reasonable suspicion, and it may well be right. But as of the monograph: “For the present, there seems to be no concrete, well-established physical connection between surface melting and snow crystal growth, although this situation may change at any time” (printed p. 55). Different experiments probe different properties of the film and are hard to compare with each other. Keep the QLL in mind as a strong lead, not as an explanation.

How a flat face stays flat

A facet has a problem, and if you have been reading carefully you may already have spotted it.

The six corners of a hexagonal crystal stick out further into the humid air than the middle of a face does. Chapter 4 showed why that matters: the crystal eats the vapour around it, so the air closest to the crystal is the most depleted, and the parts that reach furthest out into the undepleted air see more fuel. That is the Berg effect, which Chapter 4 named and drew for you. The corners have the higher σ. So the corners should grow faster, the face should bow outward, and the flat face should be destroyed within moments of forming.

It is not destroyed. Something holds it flat, and that something is the steps.

New terraces nucleate preferentially near the corners, because that is where the supersaturation is highest. Once born, each step sweeps inward toward the middle of the face. But it sweeps fastest near the corners, where there is most fuel, and slows as it travels inward. Steps that start fast and finish slow pile up behind one another — so the step density is highest in the middle of the face. And steps are exactly where molecules stick. So alphaHK is highest in the middle of the face, precisely where σ is lowest.

High σ and low alphaHK at the corners. Low σ and high alphaHK at the centre. The growth rate is their product, and the product is the same everywhere. The face advances as one flat sheet.

This is a feedback loop, and it self-corrects in both directions. If the face becomes too flat, the corners — with their fuel advantage and no compensating step density — pull ahead and re-curve it. If it becomes too concave, the centre's step pile-up overcompensates and the centre catches up. There is a stable curvature, and the surface finds it. In the monograph's words, “there is a negative feedback that maintains the precise concave shape needed to keep the growth velocity… constant across the entire facet surface” (printed p. 80).

Key idea

A facet is not passively flat. It is actively held flat by a feedback loop that trades fuel against stickiness. The monograph calls this “one of the simplest examples of spontaneous structure formation” (printed p. 80).

There is a delightful consequence. Reaching that balance means letting the corners run a little ahead of the centre first — so a “flat” facet is in fact very slightly concave, dished inward by an amount measured in molecular layers. You will never see it. The dish is only a few molecular layers deep, and the monograph is explicit that the concave facet shape is “normally imperceptible” (printed p. 80). What reaches your eye is a surface as smooth and flat as a pane of glass.

Careful

“Corners always see more vapour” is a rule of thumb, not a law. On a very thin plate, where the flat basal faces grow far more reluctantly than the thin edge, the contour lines of supersaturation bunch tightly around the rim and the highest supersaturation ends up in the middle of the basal face instead. The monograph is blunt about it: “Even though the prism edge sticks out farther into the supersaturated air, σsurf is lowest there” (printed p. 112). The Berg effect survives only while the attachment kinetics stay reasonably even-handed.

The low-energy myth

You have already met half the correction this chapter exists to make — that facets are the slowest surfaces, not the favoured ones. Here is the other half: where the flatness does not come from.

Almost every popular account of snowflakes explains flat faces like this: nature minimises energy, flat faces have the lowest surface energy, therefore crystals grow flat faces. It is a tidy story. It is also wrong — and it is wrong in an instructive way, because the premise is basically true and the conclusion still does not follow.

Surface energy is the cost of having a surface at all: every molecule at the boundary has unfulfilled bonds, and that costs energy per square metre of exposed area. For ice near the melting point the measured values are about 76 mJ/m2 for the water/vapour surface, about 30 ± 5 mJ/m2 for ice against water, and about 106 ± 15 mJ/m2 for ice against vapour. And yes, facets do have a slightly lower surface energy than rough surfaces. The premise holds.

Three independent checks kill the conclusion.

One: the anisotropy is far too small. For surface energy to sculpt a faceted shape, the energy has to depend strongly on which way the surface faces — a difference between directions that physicists call anisotropy. For ice, that difference is far too small. The facet's energy must be enough lower than the rough surfaces' energy to be worth the extra area, and there is a threshold you can derive with high-school geometry: a hexagon beats its inscribed circle only if the facet energy is below 0.91 of the unfaceted value, and a square only below 0.79. Extending the argument, a fully faceted equilibrium shape needs the facet energy to be roughly 10–20 % lower. Ice does not come close. For ice against water the difference between orientations is “no more than a few percent” — comparable to many ordinary metals — and the ice/vapour surface energy is judged to be nearly as uniform. A few percent, where ten to twenty are needed.

Two: nobody has ever measured ice's equilibrium shape. The lowest-energy shape a crystal would settle into, given unlimited time, is called the equilibrium crystal shape. For ice it has never been definitively observed — not in vapour, not in water. There is circumstantial evidence pointing away from a faceted answer: researchers have examined bubbles trapped in glacial ice for decades, an equilibrated bubble would take the same shape, and nobody has reported a faceted one. That evidence has limits, and the monograph states them itself — bubbles in natural ice “are usually not particularly spherical either, so the conclusions one can draw from bubble observations regarding the ice ECS are limited”. Evidence runs the other way too: the sole experimental investigation of the question concluded that the ice/vapour equilibrium shape is fully faceted below −10 °C, and the step energies of Figure 2.15 “suggest, however, that the ice/vapor ECS might become faceted at lower temperatures, at least to some degree” (printed p. 65). So this check does not prove the equilibrium shape is round. What it establishes is weaker and still fatal: an argument that leans on the equilibrium shape is leaning on a quantity nobody has in hand. The next check needs no such caution.

Three: there is nowhere near enough time. This is the number that finishes the case. Reaching equilibrium means shipping molecules from one part of the crystal surface to another until the shape settles, and that takes time. For a crystal 50 microns in radius — about a tenth of a millimetre across — in ordinary air, that time is about eight days (printed p. 65). A real snow crystal grows from a frozen droplet to full size in minutes, inside a cloud that will not hold still for eight seconds. Snowflakes are not shapes that settled. They are shapes frozen mid-race.

Careful

The claim is not that facets have ordinary surface energy. They do have lower-than-average surface energy — that part of the popular story is correct. The error is in which consequence you draw from it. Facet planes are molecularly smooth, and smoothness does two things at once: it lowers the surface energy and it hinders the attachment kinetics. As the monograph says, “the latter property, more than the former, controls the formation of snow crystal facets” (printed p. 46). Both effects are real; one is decisive and the other is a rounding error. “It is a common misconception that faceted morphologies appear because faceted surfaces have the lowest surface energies… anisotropic attachment kinetics are by far the dominant effect causing faceting” (printed p. 40).

Careful

None of that makes surface energy powerless. It keeps one job, at a scale where nothing else can do it. A molecule sitting on a strongly curved bump has fewer neighbours than one on a flat plain, so it is held more loosely and escapes more easily — a curved surface holds its molecules slightly less well, and the vapour it can keep in balance above itself is a little higher. That is the Gibbs–Thomson effect. Its size is set by a length dsv, about one nanometre for ice against vapour: the correction to the effective supersaturation is Δσ ≈ 2dsv / R for a surface curved through radius R (printed p. 63), which comes to roughly 0.1 % at a radius of one micron (printed p. 64) — nothing at all beside everything else in this chapter. But shrink R and the term grows teeth. At the razor rim of a very thin plate, it is the thing that says no.

Statics is not dynamics

The surface-energy myth was one instance of a wider habit of thought — reasoning about a growing crystal from the arrangement it would settle into if it stopped. Here is the habit itself, and it is worth breaking before you go on.

Crystallography is the study of how molecules stack — the lattice, its symmetry, its spacings. It is a statics problem: find the lowest-energy arrangement of things sitting still. Modern science is extremely good at that kind of problem, which is why the structure of ice has been settled since the 1920s and Chapter 3 could hand it to you as established fact.

Crystal growth is a different animal. It is a dynamics problem: enormous numbers of molecules interacting, arriving, leaving, far from equilibrium, with the answer depending on the whole history of the process rather than on any minimum. Science is much weaker there. “It is a common misconception to think that crystallography explains crystal growth, but this is far from the truth… Modern science is good at statics problems, but less so with many-body dynamics problems” (printed p. 30).

That single distinction explains the strange state of this field. The ice lattice has been known for the better part of a century. The attachment kinetics — what alphaHK is, on which face, at which temperature, and why — is still being argued about: “the crystallography of ice has been essentially solved for decades, while many important aspects the ice attachment kinetics remain quite puzzling” (printed p. 30). The theory of terrace nucleation built in the 1930s by Stranski, Kaischew, Becker, Döring and Volmer, and extended in 1951 by Burton, Cabrera and Frank, explains a great deal of what you have read here. It does not explain all of it. Even now, “snowflake science is very much a work in progress, as several rather basic aspects of the surface structure and dynamics of ice at the molecular level remain quite mysterious” (printed p. 16).

You now have both halves of the engine. Chapter 4 gave you σ — the fuel, and how diffusion rations it. This chapter gave you alphaHK — the surface's willingness to accept it, the nucleation barrier behind that willingness, and the feedback loop that keeps a face flat while it grows.

But look again at what that feedback loop depends on: the facet centre must always be able to raise its alphaHK enough to make up for its fuel deficit. And alphaHK cannot exceed one. Push a crystal hard enough that molecules stick everywhere they land, and the flatness machine has nothing left to give — which is where the next chapter is heading: the moment a smooth face gives up and a branch shoots out of the corner.
