Chapter 24

Watching It Starve

Phase 3 is the phase where the project finally gets to look at what it built. So why is a picture the one thing the gate will not accept?

~16 min read 1 chart 8 interactives

By the end you will be able to

  • Explain why a visualisation can be completely indispensable and still not be evidence.
  • Read a metric definition and see that it is a definition — a rule anyone can re-run — rather than an opinion with digits attached.
  • Say why you certify your measuring instruments before you point them at the thing you actually care about.
  • Tell a blocking gate from a diagnostic gate, and say what each one is for.

The microscope came first

What do you build when the important part of your simulation is invisible?

The crystal is the easy half. It is a set of cells marked ice, and you can draw it. The half that decides what the crystal becomes is the vapour around it, and vapour has no surface to draw. If the model grows something wrong, the wrongness is almost always in that field — and you cannot see a field by looking at the crystal it produced. A malformed crystal can look plausibly organic. A malformed field is obvious immediately, if you can see it at all.

So Phase 3 built the instrument. The charter lists what goes in it: the crystal drawn as instanced hexagonal prisms of the boundary cells with an orbit camera; a surface overlay you can switch between quantities; a draggable slice plane through the vapour field with freeze-and-inspect and an adjustable value range; and cell picking with a hover readout using model-honest labels (project charter.md, §3.2, Phase 3). "Instanced" just means the renderer is handed one hexagonal prism and a list of places to put it, which is how you draw tens of thousands of cells without the page dying.

Freeze-and-inspect is the piece that turns a movie into a microscope. The solver runs on its own thread; the display never touches it. Every view refresh reads the latest snapshot object, "so pausing the solver freezes a fully inspectable state: slice, overlays, picking, and HUD keep working on it" (app/src/main.ts, lines 4–5). You hit pause, the crystal stops, and you can still drag the slice through it and click individual cells. Nothing you inspect changes what you inspected.

Key idea

Most projects ship a product and bolt on debugging tools afterwards. This one built the debugging tool at Phase 3 and scheduled the product last — Phase 7 is still marked not started in docs/PROGRESS.md. The charter's own comment on that: "These views are debugging instruments first and product features second — that they are the same artifact is the project's luckiest property."

Watching a facet centre starve

You already know the mechanism from Part One. A growing flat face eats the vapour directly in front of it. The middle of a wide face is further from any fresh supply than the edges are, so the middle runs hungry first. Widen the face and the middle gets hungrier. Keep widening it and the middle stops keeping up with the rim altogether: the face goes concave, then it opens into a cavity. That is where hollow columns come from — and notice that no step in that story is a rule about hollowness.

The slice plane is how you look at it. It cuts through the vapour field and paints what it finds. Below is that view, rebuilt from scratch: a vertical cut through a growing plate, with the vapour relaxed to a steady state by repeated averaging. Replace every empty cell with the average of its four neighbours; then do it again, and again, until a whole pass changes nothing. That method is called Jacobi iteration, and it comes with one rule. Each pass writes its answers into a second copy of the grid rather than overwriting the one it is reading — that is what "two buffers, never in place" means. It is not a style preference. The reference solver's own header says why: "Jacobi over ping-pong buffers, NEVER in place (in-place Gauss-Seidel is a silent physics bug)" (solver-cpu/src/gg-solver.ts, line 7). Overwrite as you go and half your neighbours are from this pass and half from the last one, which is a different physics problem that still produces a smooth, convincing picture. Drag the plane. Widen the plate. Watch the number in the readout, not the picture.

That is Phase 3's "done when", as written in the charter before the phase started:

“Done when you can watch a facet center starve in the slice view while the plate grows — and the automated center-vs-rim depletion metric (facet-center σ vs rim σ, tracked over the run) confirms it (added v1.3: §3.3's automated-metric rule applies to this gate like any other; the visualization stays, the eyeball stops being the evidence).”
snowflake repository, project charter.md — §3.2, Phase 3, “Done when”. Emphasis added.

Read that parenthesis again. It is an amendment. An earlier version of this gate stopped at the slice view — you could see it, so it was true. Version 1.3 added the metric clause and applied the project's standing rule to its own most photogenic milestone. The gate row in docs/PROGRESS.md carries the same "(v1.3)" tag on the same clause.

The eyeball stops being the evidence

So what did close the gate?

A number, and a command that reproduces it. The metric is called centerRimDepletion, it lives in core/src/metrics.ts, and its convention is written out in the file above the code, in full, including the awkward cases. Here it is, compressed:

depletionRatio = depletionCenter / depletionRim

where
ktop
the highest layer holding any ice — the crystal's top basal face
depletionCenter
the field value in the single cell directly above the middle of that face. Undefined (NaN) if that cell is off the grid, a wall, or ice
rim set
the ice cells in layer ktop that still have at least one free neighbour beside them in the same layer — the face's edge ring
depletionRim
the mean field value in the cells directly above the rim set. NaN if there are none
ratio < 1
the centre is starved relative to the rim

core/src/metrics.ts, “Center-vs-rim depletion”; definition restated in docs/plans/phase-3-dev-visualization.md, “The metric (WP1) — precise definition”

Two things about that definition are worth more than they look. First, it is a ratio of two samples of the same field, which means it is meaningful without anybody claiming to know what the field's units are — and under the borrowed threshold rule (GGThreshold, Gravner and Griffeath's rule from chapter 18) nobody does. Second, every failure mode has a name in advance. A missing crystal gives NaN. A zero rim mean gives infinity. The file says what happens next: "non-finite either way, which downstream gate criteria must treat as a named failure, not a pass."

The run itself was registered before it was run. The command is node runner/src/main.ts gate3 — flagless, meaning there are no options to fiddle with, because the whole protocol is pinned in the code and in the plan: the plate preset, dimensions 128×128×64, a hexagonal-prism domain, reflecting walls, seed 1, noise off. The metric is sampled every 100 ticks. The registered window is the 41 samples at ticks 400 to 4400. The run keeps going past the window to its natural stop and the file records every sample, "so nothing is hidden by the window."

Then the thresholds, registered on 2026-07-15 from a calibration probe that the plan labels in the same sentence as "observational, not citable as the gate result". Then the run.

Be precise about what that buys, because it is less than it first looks. The probe ran the same deterministic protocol the gate would run — same preset, same 128×128×64 domain, same seed, same 100-tick sampling cadence — so the thresholds are the probe's own numbers with a margin cut into them: 0.75 against a probe median of 0.5315, 80 % against a probe 37/41 = 0.902, radius 30 against a probe 38. The plan is the one that says what that margin is testing: "The run is deterministic (noise 0, pinned engine), so these margins cover protocol drift, not run-to-run noise." Registering a threshold you have already seen a probe clear is a legitimate move — it is disclosed, and it fixes the number so it cannot be nudged afterwards — but it is a weaker kind of prediction than a gate whose outcome nobody has seen. Pass A below, and the pre-registration in chapter 27, are the stronger kind.

Median depletion ratio over the window: 0.531454, against a registered ceiling of 0.75. Fraction of window samples below 1: 90.2 %, against a registered floor of 80 %. Final bounding radius 38, against a floor of 30. Every per-tick attachment delta exactly symmetric under the hexagonal lattice's rotations and mirrors (D6h), and — a separate, belt-and-braces clause — the final symmetry error exactly 0. Far-field stop at tick 4800, no domain contact. Exit code 0 — the single number a finished program hands back when it stops, where 0 means every enforced criterion held and anything else means at least one did not.

And one criterion that exists purely to stop a certain kind of cheating: G3-WINDOW requires all 41 samples to be present. An incomplete window is a named failure — "never a rescaled or partial statistic." You cannot lose three awkward samples and quietly take the median of the remaining thirty-eight.

The last thing that makes a metric evidence is that it is able to fail. Phase 3 committed five negative controls as tests. Three of them are fake fields, and you can run those here: a state with no crystal, a hand-built uniform field, and a hand-built inverted field where the rim is starved instead of the centre. A fourth recomputes the centre and rim by brute force on a small hand-built lattice with known values and requires an exact match against the library function. The fifth is the one that closes the loop: a test proving each named G3 criterion individually trips, so that no criterion is dead code. A criterion that can never fire would sit in the gate looking like a check and passing everything.

How we know

There is one more check, and it is the cleanest in the chapter. Building a viewer should not change the science — but "should not" is a hope. The Phase 3 checkpoint is byte-identical to the accepted Phase 2a artifact, the same SHA-256 f1796b5015…a389. Not "looks the same". Not "within tolerance". The same bytes. Whatever else the viewer did, it did not touch the crystal.

What the readout refuses to say

Click a cell in the app and it tells you about that cell. The interesting question is what it is allowed to tell you.

The charter's rule, from §1.5: 'A hover readout says "high relative attachment tendency," not "attachment rate: 91%." A designer tool can be honest about being a model; a fake physics readout is worse than none — and a real-looking number resting on an untested model is the most convincing fake of all.'

This is not fussiness about wording. The project runs two different surface rules through the same machinery, and they store different things that look identical on screen. The borrowed threshold rule tracks a made-up diffusive vapour mass d in phenomenological model units. The physics rule tracks a real dimensionless supersaturation sigma. Both are one number per cell that you can colour blue. So the code carries a tag with every display snapshot and a function whose entire job is to reject any combination that would show one rule's state under the other's name: "f is NOT a reuse of G-G boundary mass b, and sigma is NOT d in different clothes" (app/src/display.ts).

Careful

It is tempting to read a vague label as a weaker claim than a precise one. It is the other way round. "High relative attachment tendency" claims exactly what has been earned: a computed quantity, unvalidated. "Attachment rate: 91 %" claims a measured physical rate that nothing in the project has established. The precise-looking number is the dishonest one, and it is dishonest because it looks precise.

A metric is a definition, not an opinion

How do you write down "branchy" so that two people who disagree cannot argue about it?

You cannot, unless you say what you mean in a way that can be re-run. The header of core/src/metrics.ts is one sentence long on this point: "Conventions are documented at each metric — a metric with an undocumented convention is a feeling with digits."

The file holds a small set of instruments, each with its convention spelled out beside it.

The branch counter is the one to play with, because it is the one where a definition most obviously replaces an argument. Try to make a hexagon score above zero. Try to make a six-armed star score five.

It is worth naming what this replaced. The model this project's machinery is built on — Gravner and Griffeath's three-dimensional snowflake automaton — is admirably honest about its own method, and the sentence is quoted in the project's notes on the paper (docs/gg-machinery.md): "visual comparison with snow crystal photographs is the only method we use to decide whether a snowfake is a failure or a success." Replacing that sentence with a file full of conventions is the project's first upgrade over its own source. It is also what makes any later score possible at all: you cannot count how many points agreed until "agreed" is a rule rather than a judgement.

Certify the ruler first

Suppose your instruments are all defined. How do you know they can detect the thing you are about to go looking for?

This is the question Phase 4 answers, and it answers it with a structural trick. Phase 4 is a list of shapes the model has to be able to produce: a plate, a solid column, a continuous transition between them, visible facet-centre starvation, hollowing, a capped column made by changing conditions mid-growth, and branching. The gauntlet is run twice — once with the borrowed threshold rule, once with the real physics. The charter says why:

“Pass A, under GGThreshold, certifies the machinery and the metrics: it proves the lattice, diffusion, and measurement pipeline can express and detect every target morphology, independent of any physics. Pass B, under LibbrechtKinetics, re-runs the gates with temperature as the swept axis — the project's first single-point Nakaya probes, and a cheap early warning, months before Phase 6, about whether the habit flip is in reach.”
snowflake repository, project charter.md — §3.2, Phase 4. Emphasis added.

Pass A contains no physics claim at all. Its habit sweep turns one abstract knob from one end of its range to the other, in five equal steps. The plan writes that knob u: an "abstract columnarity control" running from 0 to 1, taking the values u = [0, 0.25, 0.5, 0.75, 1]. u is not a temperature and not any other physical quantity — it is a dial with no units, and its only job is to set one number inside the borrowed threshold rule: the basal attachment threshold ggThreshBeta(0,1) = 3 − 2u, so the five runs use the thresholds [3, 2.5, 2, 1.5, 1]. That single slot is the plate↔column axis of chapter 18's borrowed rule: a high basal threshold means the top and bottom faces accept vapour reluctantly and growth goes sideways into a plate, a low one means they accept it easily and growth goes up into a column. So u = 0 is the plate end of the sweep and u = 1 the column end, and everything else is held still — "every other scalar and vector slot byte-identical across the five runs". If the plate that comes out at one end and the column at the other are correctly measured as a plate and a column, and the three steps in between move the right way, then those rulers work. Its other scenarios do the same job for the depletion metric, the two hollowness numbers, the cap score and the branch counter. That is all Pass A is for.

That fourth card is a true story, and a small one, which is why it is worth telling. Before the Pass A protocol was frozen, a review checked the intended column endpoint and the hollowness metric reported 0.1260 — the "solid column" was a tube. The instrument said no. The target was corrected on the record — the reasoning is written into the plan's own “Tried and rejected” list — before the gate ran, and when it did run, the column endpoint passed A-HABIT-SOLID as one of the 24 green records. A ruler that cannot report a failure is not a ruler.

One gate must pass; one only has to report

Pass A is blocking. Pass B is diagnostic. Those are different objects with different jobs, and the charter wires the difference into the exit code: "a failed pass B is a reportable finding and does not block Phase 6 — Phase 6 is precisely the phase whose job is to report that failure properly."

Pass B is not, however, unconstrained. Its execution criteria are every bit as blocking as Pass A's. In plain terms, each run has to clear five execution criteria before its shape is even looked at:

  • It records the exact build of the program that ran it, down to the version of the JavaScript engine underneath.
  • It compares every input setting byte for byte against what was registered, so the only thing allowed to differ from run to run is the one knob the scenario names — temperature, seed, noise, or the far-away vapour level.
  • Every vapour solve has to pass two different tests, not one twice. First, the repeated averaging must report itself settled: a finite residual below the registered relaxTol = 1e-9, on a whole number of sweeps inside the registered range. Second, and separately, the runner recomputes chapter 22's divergence identity from the run's own totals — the fraction by which the vapour injected at the outer shell and the net exchange at the surface fail to match — and requires that fraction to come out below divTol = 1e-7. Two tolerances, two registered constants, two bars. It is the second meter from chapter 22, not a second opinion about the first (B-EXEC-CONVERGENCE, docs/plans/phase-4-morphology-gauntlet.md).
  • The runner adds up the vapour bookkeeping independently, step by step — how much vapour the surface should have consumed this step — instead of trusting the solver's own total.
  • The saved state has to reload to exactly the bits it was written from.

What Pass B is allowed to do is come out the wrong shape.

Still unsettled

Five misses and three passes, on execution-valid runs, before the validation sweep. (The charter expected Pass B to be an early warning “months before Phase 6”; in the event docs/PROGRESS.md records Phase 4 complete on 2026-07-18 and Phase 6 in progress from 2026-07-26.) This chapter spends neither. The three passes are habit readings from a five-point temperature sweep on a 48×48×48 dev-scale grid, under a pass whose result cannot block anything: an aspect ratio that inverts between −5 °C and −15 °C is not the Nakaya diagram, and an early warning that says “in reach” is not evidence that it was reached. Note also which way the registered criterion asks it to invert — a plate at −5 °C and a column at −15 °C, which is the direction the model's own measured facet parameters imply and the opposite of the order the Nakaya diagram puts at those two temperatures. That gap is deliberate, disclosed, and the thing chapter 28 turns on. What the five misses mean — whether the physics is insufficient, or the dev-scale grid is too small, or the early-warning thresholds were set too tight — is not something a diagnostic pass is entitled to say either. Phase 6 is in progress; Phase 7 has not started. Chapter 28 is where all eight get an answer, and the answer is not the comfortable one.

Hollowing, with no hollowing rule

Where in the program does it say "when the face gets wide, make it hollow"?

Nowhere. That is the second scientific gate of the whole project: "hollowing emerges with no explicit hollow rule… Done when the hollowness metric rises from field dynamics alone, reproducibly across seeds." Search the rule for yourself. Below is the attachment and melting step of the reference solver, copied out of the file.

The absence is not left as an assurance. A-HOLLOW-STRUCTURAL makes it a checked criterion: "Phase 4 introduces no center-, radius-, cavity-, or hollowness-driven branch in either solver." A reviewer reads the solver diff as part of the criterion, the runtime evidence byte-hashes the exact reviewed sources against pinned SHA-256 values, and — the detail that shows somebody had thought about how this could be faked — "the runner may not self-attest with a hard-coded true."

The capped column carries a smaller lesson about specifications. A capped column is a shaft with a plate stuck on each end, made by changing the conditions partway through the growth. The charter originally asked for a plate→column history. That is backwards: Gravner and Griffeath grow the axial shaft first and then grow plates at its ends, and a plate cannot retroactively become that shaft. Decision 0011 corrected the milestone to column→plate, on the record, with the reason written down — and Pass A then produced a cap score of 1.3. The specification was wrong, it was fixed by a numbered decision rather than a quiet edit, and both versions are still readable.

The picture still has a job

If screenshots are never evidence, why did Phase 4 take twenty of them?

Because pictures are how a person catches the thing a metric was not asked about. The Phase 4 plan lists under out of scope: "Treating screenshots as gate evidence. They are required visual QA and explanatory diagnostics; automated metrics and exit semantics carry the scientific claims." Two lanes, one gate. Only one lane is wired to the exit code.

But the lanes are cross-checked, and this is the part people usually skip. Criterion V4-4: "Column captures show a visibly darker/lower-value band above the basal center than the rim whenever the metric says ratio < 1; a mismatched picture and number is a blocker." Not a rounding quibble, not a note for later — a blocker. If the image and the number disagree, at least one of them is lying and you do not yet know which.

The real set was 20 captures out of 20 registered views, inspected at original resolution by two parties, with zero absent, error, clipped or hash-mismatched views, published under a manifest hashed to 19e0fcfe…573b.

How we know — and who checked

"Two parties" needs saying out loud. Every review in this project to date has been an LLM review: the adversarial review agents that looped over each Phase 3 work package, the coordinator and reviewer who inspected all twenty Phase 4 captures, and the two-reviewer process that checked this page. The project's own Rule 10 requires every review round to record "the reviewing agent's model and whether it shared context with the developer; what it independently re-executed; and what it did not check, stated as a limit of the evidence rather than left implicit." That is a serious discipline and it is not the same thing as human expert review. The project treats it as a disclosed limitation. So does this page.

Everything in this chapter leans on the same trick: there was a right answer to compare against — a plate is not a column, a starved centre is not a fed one, a hexagon has no branches. The next chapter takes that away. When you write a program to compute something nobody has ever computed, there is no correct answer to test it against — so what, exactly, do you test?
