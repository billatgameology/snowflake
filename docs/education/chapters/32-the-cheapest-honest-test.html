Chapter 32

The Cheapest Honest Test

Before rebuilding a simulator, ask whether the new idea survives a smaller test that can already prove it wrong.

Guiding questionHow can a cheap calculation save months of expensive, misleading work?

By the end, you can

  • Define a discriminator and explain why it should be cheap.
  • Compare a proposed model with a simple baseline.
  • Explain why “all no-pass” can be a successful development result.
  • Separate a diagnostic mapping from a physical score.
What Phase 9 actually finished: it screened development proposals against the Phase 8 library. Every eligible route stopped without promotion. No production solver was changed, and no models were combined.

A discriminator is a small door with a strong lock

A model proposal can be very complicated: new surface physics, a rescaled temperature curve, or a rule borrowed from another experiment. Implementing it inside the full crystal simulator might take weeks. A discriminator asks for the smallest calculation that the proposal must pass before that work is justified.

The key word is must. A cheap test is useful only when failure really contradicts the proposal’s stated reason for existing. A random toy calculation is cheap, but it does not discriminate.

The baseline gets a seat at the table

Suppose a proposal predicts six temperature-dependent measurements. Its error looks large, but “large” needs context. A simple baseline might ignore most of the new mechanism and rescale the remaining histories using only the other histories. If that humble baseline predicts much better, the complicated proposal has not earned implementation.

One Phase 9 Lamb-data comparison did exactly this. The proposed D-BT mapping won zero of six matched cases. Its mean squared error was 431.3416, while the leave-one-history-out rescaling baseline achieved 0.6578183. Mean squared error squares each miss, averages the squares, and rewards smaller values.

“No-pass” is not the same as “useless”

Phase 9 began with 70 proposal aliases. Those resolved to 59 complete artifacts because several names pointed to the same underlying candidate. The adapter examined 112 proposed purposes: 51 were eligible with a stated limit, 41 were ineligible, and 20 were source-blocked.

Only two routes reached quantitative comparison. Neither earned promotion. That result saved the project from adding attractive complexity whose advertised improvement had not survived its first fair test.

Some numbers are maps, not measurements

The other quantitative route compared M-F and M-K2 through a mapping that depends on modeling choices. It produced a diagnostic 2/5 result. Because the mapping itself was not a direct physical measurement, Phase 9 correctly refused to turn 2/5 into a physical score.

This distinction matters whenever software transforms evidence. A graph pixel converted with a documented axis calibration can become a physical value. A category translated through a speculative model mapping remains a model-dependent diagnostic.

The handoff to Phase 10

An all-no-pass development phase leaves a clear next question: can the project repair one narrow missing bridge well enough to run a decisive comparison? Phase 10 investigated that question. Its most important result was not a better snowflake. It was a precise account of why the proposed bridge could not yet be built.
