Chapter 32

The Cheapest Honest Test

Somebody publishes a new idea about why ice grows the way it does. Building it into the simulator would take weeks. Is there a smaller calculation that could already show it does not help?

~19 min read6 interactives3 equations0 three-dimensional solver runs

By the end you will be able to

  • Define a discriminator and say why a cheap test is only useful if failing it really contradicts the idea.
  • Explain mean squared error in one sentence and read three of them side by side.
  • Say why a humble baseline that fits one number per experiment can beat a published theory, and what that victory does and does not mean.
  • Separate a diagnostic that depends on a modelling choice from a physical score.
  • Explain why “all no-pass” completed the phase, and what it cost.

Where this chapter sits — Phase 9, complete 2026-08-13

Phase 9 screened proposed mechanisms against the Phase 8 library. It ran the full crystal simulator zero times. Two proposals reached a quantitative test: one failed, and the other stalled on a mapping the source did not supply. Nothing was promoted. The charter had said in advance that “An all-no-pass result completes the phase.” It did.

Fifteen ideas in an afternoon

Before testing anything, Phase 9 wrote down what there was to test. In roughly an hour and a quarter between its plan commit and its completion commit (the record keeps no stopwatch, so every duration in this chapter comes from commit timestamps), using three queries to an open scholarly index and the papers the project already held, it registered 18 sources and 15 named hypotheses about ice growth. The search itself cost three tenths of a cent in index fees, which is worth noticing: the expensive part of science is rarely the reading list.

The fifteen are worth seeing in one place, because Part One of this course has already met most of them as physics. Here they are as claims a model could be built around.

The maker had directed that the papers Phase 8 had found and saved be included, and the list of arms grew by two to meet it; the fifteen hypotheses stayed fifteen.

A small door with a strong lock

A proposal can be very complicated. Building it into the crystal simulator might take weeks, and once built it would generate impressive pictures whether or not it was right. A discriminator is the smallest calculation the proposal must pass before that work is justified. The key word is must. A toy calculation is cheap, but it only discriminates if failing it genuinely contradicts the proposal’s reason for existing.

The charter fixed the order of operations in one sentence: “Protocol compatibility precedes a score, one-factor probes precede combinations, and the permanent GGThreshold and LibbrechtKinetics operators remain unchanged controls.” (project charter, Phase 9). Translate it: first check that the experiment and the model are even talking about the same quantity (Chapter 31’s operands); then test one idea at a time, never two together; and never touch the two reference solvers while doing so.

The difference between a cheap test and a cheap discriminator is easiest to feel by trying to tell them apart. Five candidates for the rule Chapter 32 is about are below. Only one of them is the test the project registered.

The crystals that grew at half speed

The idea that got the fullest test was the Lamb bulk-transfer rule. Its authors had trained a neural network on 290 levitated crystals and distilled from it a symbolic formula: the rate at which vapour is delivered to a crystal, they proposed, depends on the crystal’s mass in a way that plain diffusion theory does not capture. It is a bold, specific, cheap-to-evaluate claim, which makes it a perfect candidate for a discriminator.

The test needed no lattice, no facets, no three-dimensional field. Take six of the Penn State levitation histories from Chapter 31: two crystals at −33.8 °C in air 17 % supersaturated over ice, four at −35.7 °C at 28.6 %, all starting between about 8 and 10 micrometres in radius, each scored second by second from time zero to at most 499 seconds — the project’s strict reading of the 0 to 500 second domain the rule’s authors claimed. Four of the records end naturally between 311 and 464 seconds; the two labelled 725 run on past 900 seconds, and those later seconds were simply not scored. Treat each crystal as a sphere. Step its predicted growth forward one second at a time (that is what integrating means here: the program takes four trial slopes inside each second and blends them — a Runge–Kutta step — on a one-second grid), and compare each predicted mass curve with the measured one.

Here is the rule this chapter keeps calling plain continuum theory, written out. It is one line, and almost everything else in this section is an argument about it.

dm/dt = 4π r σ K,     K = 1 ÷ (Rvapour + Rheat)

where
dm/dt
how much mass the crystal gains this second, in kilograms per second
r
the radius of the sphere with the same volume as the crystal, in metres — this is the step where a real snow crystal is replaced by a ball
σ
supersaturation: the surplus vapour the air carries over what ice can hold, from Chapter 4. Here 0.17 for the two crystals at −33.8 °C and 0.286 for the four at −35.7 °C
K
the transfer coefficient: how easily vapour is delivered, in kilograms per metre per second. It is one divided by the sum of two costs, exactly as two resistances add in series
Rvapour
the cost of diffusing vapour through the air to the surface
Rheat
the cost of carrying the heat released by freezing away again

Transcribed from runner/src/phase9-dbt-model.ts, the committed constants and transfer functions of the Phase 9 D-BT test. Chapter 4 wrote the same two doors in different letters (X0/R and alphaHK) and set heat aside; this rule keeps heat and has no surface term at all, which is another way of saying it assumes every molecule that arrives sticks.

Put a real crystal into it. At −33.8 °C and 97,055 pascals the transfer coefficient works out at 3.72 × 10−9 kilograms per metre per second, and the heat cost is only about 5 % of the total: this is a diffusion-limited calculation, and the heat door is nearly wide open. Crystal 725c starts at a radius of 8.9 micrometres, which for a ball of ice weighs 2.69 nanograms. The rule says it starts collecting at 0.0708 nanograms a second — 2.6 % of itself per second. Add that on (the careful step adds 0.0711 ng over the whole second, because the rate is already rising as the crystal grows), work out the new radius, ask again. That is the whole calculation, repeated 499 times.

Because K hardly changes while the crystal grows, this particular rule also has an exact answer, which is handy for checking the arithmetic: the square of the radius grows in a straight line.

r2 = r02 + 2σKt ÷ ρ    so    m/m0 = (1 + 2σKt ÷ ρr02)3/2

where
r0
the radius at time zero
ρ
the density of ice: 910 kilograms per cubic metre in this model
m/m0
the mass ratio, the crystal’s mass divided by its starting mass. This is what the test actually compares, so a value of 3 means the crystal has tripled.

The same committed constants, integrated. At the −33.8 °C conditions the square of the radius grows by 1.391 square micrometres every second; at −35.7 °C, by 1.909. Both are this course’s arithmetic on committed constants, not a Phase 9 result. Chapter 31’s “mass like time to the power 1.5” is this same formula at long times.

The four rules were plain continuum theory (diffusion of vapour plus conduction of heat, as in Chapter 4); a project hybrid that adds a surface-attachment term and a near-surface vapour jump to it; the Lamb rule; and a deliberately humble baseline: continuum theory multiplied by one constant per crystal, with that constant fitted using only the other five crystals, so no history is graded on a number tuned to itself. This is called leave-one-out, and its job is to be the stupidest thing that could possibly work. The hybrid scored almost the same as plain theory and does not enter the verdict; the record keeps it anyway, because a reported diagnostic and a verdict are different things.

Careful

Leaving one crystal out and fitting on the other five is a real safeguard, and it is not the held-out validation of Chapter 17. Every one of these six histories is Phase 8 development evidence: the project had already seen them all when it designed this test. Statisticians call what happened here cross-fitting — rotating which part of your own data you grade against. The Phase 9 guide says so in as many words, and adds a warning about its own file names: identifiers beginning with “heldout” mean only an omitted fold and “grant no held-out evidence status”. Notice too that the recorded fit does not close the arrow, and could not: it was chosen to fit five other crystals over their whole curves, not this one at its last second.

Look at the bars before reading the scores, because they carry the most physical information in the whole phase. Every multiplier is close to one half. The six recorded crystals grew at about half the rate the project’s sphere-plus-continuum calculation predicts, and they did so consistently. The multiplier is a number the baseline was allowed to fit, and the record lists what it may be absorbing: the apparatus, the levitation electric field, the crystal’s shape (a frozen droplet need not be a solid single-crystal sphere), or other missing effects. The baseline “won” by absorbing that unexplained factor into a fitted constant. It did not explain it, and the test cannot say whether the factor belongs to the crystals or to the calculation. The project recorded it and moved on.

The baseline gets a seat at the table

The score was mean squared error. The quantity compared is the mass ratio, the crystal’s mass divided by its starting mass, so a value of 3 means the crystal has tripled. At each sampled second, take the difference between predicted and measured mass ratio, square it so misses in either direction count alike, average within each crystal first, then average the six crystals with equal weight, so that a 500-sample history does not outvote a 312-sample one. Smaller is better; a perfect prediction scores zero. The square root of a score is the typical miss: about 9 starting masses for plain theory, about 21 for the Lamb rule, under 1 for the baseline. Here is that sentence as arithmetic.

MSEcrystal = (1/N) Σ (pi − oi)2     MSEfamily = (1/6) Σ MSEcrystal

where
pi
the mass ratio the rule predicts at the i-th sampled second
oi
the mass ratio the laboratory measured at that same second
pi − oi
the residual: predicted minus measured. Positive means the rule overshot. (This is not Chapter 22’s residual, which measures how far a solver’s inner loop is from settling.)
N
how many seconds that crystal was scored over: 500, 500, 450, 464, 324 and 312 for the six, 2,550 in all
Σ
“add up all of them”

Squaring does two jobs at once: it makes a miss in either direction count the same, and it makes one big miss cost more than several small ones. Averaging inside each crystal first is the step that stops the longest history from owning the answer — the same choice Chapter 31 showed you can make four different ways.

A worked miniature, with invented numbers: suppose a rule’s residuals over five seconds are 0, +0.5, −0.5, +0.5 and +1. Square them (0, 0.25, 0.25, 0.25, 1), average (0.35), and the typical miss is the square root, 0.59. Now replace that last +1 with +3 and the score jumps to 1.95, nearly six times worse, for one bad second out of five. Here is what came back for the real family of six histories.

The registered rule for promotion was fixed before the run, by the record’s own hashes and a review before launch: the Lamb rule had to beat the baseline outright on at least four of the six histories, ties counting as losses, and its family error had to be lower too. It won zero. Twenty-four pre-declared sensitivity cases, each nudging one input on the crystal being graded (its starting radius to the top or bottom of its stated range, or its measured mass by five percent either way) without refitting the baseline, flipped none of the six verdicts. The label the project recorded was central no-effect or failure. One caveat the plan records against itself: the protocol and the result first entered Git in the same commit, so an outside reader cannot prove from Git alone that the rule came before the answer. Pre-registration only binds when someone else can check the order.

Careful

“The Lamb rule is wrong.” That is more than the test showed. It showed that, on six histories from one chamber, at two temperatures, with the project’s constants, the rule as transcribed predicted worse than plain theory. The record lists what it could not rule out: the electric field that holds the crystal up, the possibility that these very crystals were in the rule’s training set, and the fact that the rule’s own code hard-wired an air pressure the chamber did not quite have. A discriminator rejects a proposal for this project’s purposes. It does not referee the literature.

Some numbers are maps, not measurements

The second test was about the two-branch prism idea, and it teaches a different lesson. Sei and Gonda measured, at 40 pascals of air, how fast the basal and prism faces advance as the supersaturation in their chamber rises. The project’s attachment law predicts exactly that: face speed against supersaturation. A direct comparison, at last.

Except for one word. The law wants the supersaturation at the crystal’s surface. The paper reports the supersaturation in the chamber. Between the two sits a layer of depleted air whose thickness depends on the crystal’s size, the substrate’s heat flow and the gas, and the paper does not supply it. So Phase 9 declared five candidate mappings (a mapping is a rule for turning the number the paper reports into the number the law needs): surface value equal to 12.5 %, 25 %, 50 %, 75 % or 100 % of the chamber value. It asked the question under each.

At 12.5 % and 25 % the two-branch law does slightly better than the inherited one. At 50 % it does about five and a half times worse. At 75 % and 100 % both laws are worse than predicting no growth at all, which says the mapping, not the physics, dominates there. A second check makes the same point from the other side: replayed over all six Sei and Gonda series, the inherited law on its own also loses to the zero-growth control at 75 % and 100 %, so the trouble belongs to the mapping and not to one law. The verdict reverses between two neighbouring choices that nobody could justify from the source. Phase 9 therefore recorded the result as diagnostic and mapping-dependent, 2 of 5, and refused to turn it into a physical score.

How we know

The family errors are the comparatorFamilyMse values in evidence/phase9-dbt-six-history-development-v3/report.json; the six multipliers and the residual sign counts are in that record’s scores.jsonl. The ten mapping errors are the matched-prism values in evidence/phase9-mf-mk2-planar-diagnostic-v1/mapping-decisions.jsonl; that file also holds a six-series zero-growth control of 0.40288. The 0.4508 control used above is a different number and comes from a different file: it is the equal-weight mean of the two matched prism zero-growth rows in series-scores.jsonl, 0.361853 and 0.539723. To reproduce “five and a half times worse”, divide 0.381919 by 0.069841. The two durations in this chapter come from commit timestamps in the repository history, not from any record.

Key idea

A plot pixel converted with a printed axis becomes a physical value. A quantity translated through a modelling choice the source did not make stays a diagnostic. Whenever software transforms evidence, ask which of the two just happened.

Who checked this chapter

The scores, multipliers and residuals on this page are the committed contents of evidence/phase9-dbt-six-history-development-v3/ and evidence/phase9-mf-mk2-planar-diagnostic-v1/; the growth rule is runner/src/phase9-dbt-model.ts, which the sphere demo re-implements rather than quotes. Two independent re-implementations — one during the 2026-09-14 audit, one while building this page — reproduce the committed end residuals for all six crystals to about one part in a million, which is the check that the demo is doing the project’s arithmetic. The same audit found and this chapter has corrected two real errors: the scoring domain stopped at 499 seconds rather than running to the end of every record, and the 0.4508 zero-growth control was cited to the wrong file.

Reading the ledger

Here is the whole phase in counts. The project’s archive held 70 names for source documents that resolved to 59 complete files, because several names pointed at the same paper. Against the 51 library records, Phase 9 registered 112 “purposes”, each a specific use of a record by a specific arm: 51 were eligible with a stated limit, 41 were ineligible because the record could not answer that arm’s question, and 20 were blocked by a missing source. Two arms reached a quantitative comparison. Zero proposals were promoted, and zero were combined.

When a cheap test is too cheap

“All no-pass” sounds like nothing happened. Something did: the project was saved from adding attractive complexity whose advertised improvement had not survived its first fair test, and it recorded a factor-of-two gap between six levitated crystals and a sphere calculation that nobody has yet explained. But the record is also honest about what the phase did not do, and the list is long. Only three of the fifteen ideas were ever put on trial against laboratory numbers: the inherited broad-facet law, the two-branch prism law and the Lamb rule. (A fourth, coupled vapour-and-heat transport, did meet the six measured curves — but as the rival the Lamb rule had to beat, not as a proposal under test.) The other twelve got no further than source intake, categorical constraints, manufactured arithmetic checks or an explicit refusal, and the record counts a refusal as a result, not as nothing. Ventilation, the width-dependent attachment that motivated the whole M1 story, step-source location, pressure-dependent kinetics, the forcing-path and memory ideas: every one closed as source-blocked (a full text the project could not obtain), protocol-ineligible (an apparatus the solver does not represent, or a source whose current edition had not been compared) or non-identifiable (a mapping like the one above that could not be sourced).

There is a cost on the other side too. The commits from adoption to completion span about five hours and twenty minutes of wall-clock time (adopted at 22:49 on 2026-08-12, completed at 04:09 the next morning), not counting the knowledge baseline before or the reviews and repairs after. The completion commit alone added 29,448 lines: adapters, protocol files, two small solvers, their tests, and about 5,000 lines of independent byte verifiers, all around two calculations, six integrations of a sphere and 96 evaluations of a rate law. Chapter 29 warned that rigour has a budget; Phase 9 spent most of its budget proving that two small answers were exactly what they said they were.

Still unsettled

Why did the levitated crystals grow at half the predicted rate? The continuum rule already includes heat conduction, and the project’s hybrid, which adds a surface-attachment term and a near-surface vapour jump, scored almost identically (86.88 against 87.45), so neither addition closes the gap. The record lists what the fitted factor might be absorbing: the levitation electric field, the apparatus itself, and shape, since the frozen droplets may not have been solid single-crystal spheres. It does not list a surface-attachment barrier; whether one could account for a factor of two is a question this course raises, not one the record tested.

An all-no-pass phase leaves a sharp question behind it: could the project repair just one of the missing bridges between a laboratory record and a model output well enough to run a decisive comparison? Phase 10 set out to find out, and Chapter 33 tells that story. It is not the story of a better snowflake.
