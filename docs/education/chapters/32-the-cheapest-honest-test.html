Chapter 32

The Cheapest Honest Test

Somebody publishes a new idea about why ice grows the way it does. Building it into the simulator would take weeks. Is there a smaller calculation that could already show it does not help?

~15 min read5 interactives0 three-dimensional solver runs

By the end you will be able to

  • Define a discriminator and say why a cheap test is only useful if failing it really contradicts the idea.
  • Explain mean squared error in one sentence and read three of them side by side.
  • Say why a humble baseline that fits one number per experiment can beat a published theory, and what that victory does and does not mean.
  • Separate a diagnostic that depends on a modelling choice from a physical score.
  • Explain why “all no-pass” completed the phase, and what it cost.

Where this chapter sits — Phase 9, complete 2026-08-13

Phase 9 screened proposed mechanisms against the Phase 8 library. It ran the full crystal simulator zero times. Two proposals reached a quantitative test: one failed, and the other stalled on a mapping the source did not supply. Nothing was promoted. The charter had said in advance that “An all-no-pass result completes the phase.” It did.

Fifteen ideas in an afternoon

Before testing anything, Phase 9 wrote down what there was to test. In roughly an hour and a quarter between its plan commit and its completion commit (the record keeps no stopwatch, so every duration in this chapter comes from commit timestamps), using three queries to an open scholarly index and the papers the project already held, it registered 18 sources and 15 named hypotheses about ice growth. The search itself cost three tenths of a cent in index fees, which is worth noticing: the expensive part of science is rarely the reading list.

The fifteen are worth seeing in one place, because Part One of this course has already met most of them as physics. Here they are as claims a model could be built around.

Two more arms were added once the project’s own archive was reconciled: an aggregate gas-and-pressure transport arm, and an observed surface-state arm about roughness, terraces and pockets seen in published micrographs and growth-and-sublimation sequences. The maker had directed that the papers Phase 8 had found and saved be included, and the list of arms grew by two to meet it; the fifteen hypotheses stayed fifteen.

A small door with a strong lock

A proposal can be very complicated. Building it into the crystal simulator might take weeks, and once built it would generate impressive pictures whether or not it was right. A discriminator is the smallest calculation the proposal must pass before that work is justified. The key word is must. A toy calculation is cheap, but it only discriminates if failing it genuinely contradicts the proposal’s reason for existing.

The charter fixed the order of operations in one sentence: “Protocol compatibility precedes a score, one-factor probes precede combinations, and the permanent GGThreshold and LibbrechtKinetics operators remain unchanged controls.” (project charter, Phase 9). Translate it: first check that the experiment and the model are even talking about the same quantity (Chapter 31’s operands); then test one idea at a time, never two together; and never touch the two reference solvers while doing so.

The crystals that grew at half speed

The idea that got the fullest test was the Lamb bulk-transfer rule. Its authors had trained a neural network on 290 levitated crystals and distilled from it a symbolic formula: the rate at which vapour is delivered to a crystal, they proposed, depends on the crystal’s mass in a way that plain diffusion theory does not capture. It is a bold, specific, cheap-to-evaluate claim, which makes it a perfect candidate for a discriminator.

The test needed no lattice, no facets, no three-dimensional field. Take six of the Penn State levitation histories from Chapter 31: two crystals at −33.8 °C in air 17 % supersaturated over ice, four at −35.7 °C at 28.6 %, all starting between about 8 and 10 micrometres in radius, each followed second by second from time zero until its record ends, between 311 and 499 seconds later. Treat each crystal as a sphere. Add up its predicted growth second by second (integrate it) under four different rules, and compare each predicted mass curve with the measured one.

The four rules were plain continuum theory (diffusion of vapour plus conduction of heat, as in Chapter 4); a project hybrid that adds a surface-attachment term and a near-surface vapour jump to it; the Lamb rule; and a deliberately humble baseline: continuum theory multiplied by one constant per crystal, with that constant fitted using only the other five crystals, so no history is graded on a number tuned to itself. This is called leave-one-out, and its job is to be the stupidest thing that could possibly work. The hybrid scored almost the same as plain theory and does not enter the verdict; the record keeps it anyway, because a reported diagnostic and a verdict are different things.

Look at the bars before reading the scores, because they carry the most physical information in the whole phase. Every multiplier is close to one half. The six recorded crystals grew at about half the rate the project’s sphere-plus-continuum calculation predicts, and they did so consistently. The multiplier is a number the baseline was allowed to fit, and the record lists what it may be absorbing: the apparatus, the levitation electric field, the crystal’s shape (a frozen droplet need not be a solid single-crystal sphere), or other missing effects. The baseline “won” by absorbing that unexplained factor into a fitted constant. It did not explain it, and the test cannot say whether the factor belongs to the crystals or to the calculation. The project recorded it and moved on.

The baseline gets a seat at the table

The score was mean squared error. The quantity compared is the mass ratio, the crystal’s mass divided by its starting mass, so a value of 3 means the crystal has tripled. At each sampled second, take the difference between predicted and measured mass ratio, square it so misses in either direction count alike, average within each crystal first, then average the six crystals with equal weight, so that a 500-sample history does not outvote a 312-sample one. Smaller is better; a perfect prediction scores zero. The square root of a score is the typical miss: about 9 starting masses for plain theory, about 21 for the Lamb rule, under 1 for the baseline. The registered rule for promotion was fixed before the run, by the record’s own hashes and a review before launch: the Lamb rule had to beat the baseline outright on at least four of the six histories, ties counting as losses, and its family error had to be lower too. It won zero. Twenty-four pre-declared sensitivity cases, each nudging one input on the crystal being graded (its starting radius to the top or bottom of its stated range, or its measured mass by five percent either way) without refitting the baseline, flipped none of the six verdicts. The label the project recorded was central no-effect or failure. One caveat the plan records against itself: the protocol and the result first entered Git in the same commit, so an outside reader cannot prove from Git alone that the rule came before the answer. Pre-registration only binds when someone else can check the order.

Careful

“The Lamb rule is wrong.” That is more than the test showed. It showed that, on six histories from one chamber, at two temperatures, with the project’s constants, the rule as transcribed predicted worse than plain theory. The record lists what it could not rule out: the electric field that holds the crystal up, the possibility that these very crystals were in the rule’s training set, and the fact that the rule’s own code hard-wired an air pressure the chamber did not quite have. A discriminator rejects a proposal for this project’s purposes. It does not referee the literature.

Some numbers are maps, not measurements

The second test was about the two-branch prism idea, and it teaches a different lesson. Sei and Gonda measured, at 40 pascals of air, how fast the basal and prism faces advance as the supersaturation in their chamber rises. The project’s attachment law predicts exactly that: face speed against supersaturation. A direct comparison, at last.

Except for one word. The law wants the supersaturation at the crystal’s surface. The paper reports the supersaturation in the chamber. Between the two sits a layer of depleted air whose thickness depends on the crystal’s size, the substrate’s heat flow and the gas, and the paper does not supply it. So Phase 9 declared five candidate mappings (a mapping is a rule for turning the number the paper reports into the number the law needs): surface value equal to 12.5 %, 25 %, 50 %, 75 % or 100 % of the chamber value. It asked the question under each.

At 12.5 % and 25 % the two-branch law does slightly better than the inherited one. At 50 % it does about five and a half times worse. At 75 % and 100 % both laws are worse than predicting no growth at all, which says the mapping, not the physics, dominates there. A second check makes the same point from the other side: replayed over all six Sei and Gonda series, the inherited law on its own also loses to the zero-growth control at 75 % and 100 %, so the trouble belongs to the mapping and not to one law. The verdict reverses between two neighbouring choices that nobody could justify from the source. Phase 9 therefore recorded the result as diagnostic and mapping-dependent, 2 of 5, and refused to turn it into a physical score.

How we know

The family errors are the comparatorFamilyMse values in evidence/phase9-dbt-six-history-development-v3/report.json; the six multipliers and the residual sign counts are in that record’s scores.jsonl. The ten mapping errors and the zero-growth control are the matched-prism and six-series values in evidence/phase9-mf-mk2-planar-diagnostic-v1/mapping-decisions.jsonl. To reproduce “five and a half times worse”, divide 0.381919 by 0.069841. The two durations in this chapter come from commit timestamps in the repository history, not from any record.

Key idea

A plot pixel converted with a printed axis becomes a physical value. A quantity translated through a modelling choice the source did not make stays a diagnostic. Whenever software transforms evidence, ask which of the two just happened.

Reading the ledger

Here is the whole phase in counts. The project’s archive held 70 names for source documents that resolved to 59 complete files, because several names pointed at the same paper. Against the 51 library records, Phase 9 registered 112 “purposes”, each a specific use of a record by a specific arm: 51 were eligible with a stated limit, 41 were ineligible because the record could not answer that arm’s question, and 20 were blocked by a missing source. Two arms reached a quantitative comparison. Zero proposals were promoted, and zero were combined.

When a cheap test is too cheap

“All no-pass” sounds like nothing happened. Something did: the project was saved from adding attractive complexity whose advertised improvement had not survived its first fair test, and it recorded a factor-of-two gap between six levitated crystals and a sphere calculation that nobody has yet explained. But the record is also honest about what the phase did not do, and the list is long. Only three of the fifteen ideas were ever set against laboratory numbers: the inherited broad-facet law, the two-branch prism law and the Lamb rule. The other twelve got no further than source intake, categorical constraints, manufactured arithmetic checks or an explicit refusal, and the record counts a refusal as a result, not as nothing. Ventilation, the width-dependent attachment that motivated the whole M1 story, step-source location, pressure-dependent kinetics, the forcing-path and memory ideas: every one closed as source-blocked (a full text the project could not obtain), protocol-ineligible (an apparatus the solver does not represent, or a source whose current edition had not been compared) or non-identifiable (a mapping like the one above that could not be sourced).

There is a cost on the other side too. The commits from adoption to completion span about five hours and twenty minutes of wall-clock time (adopted at 22:49 on 2026-08-12, completed at 04:09 the next morning), not counting the knowledge baseline before or the reviews and repairs after. The completion commit alone added 29,448 lines: adapters, protocol files, two small solvers, their tests, and about 5,000 lines of independent byte verifiers, all around two calculations, six integrations of a sphere and 96 evaluations of a rate law. Chapter 29 warned that rigour has a budget; Phase 9 spent most of its budget proving that two small answers were exactly what they said they were.

Still unsettled

Why did the levitated crystals grow at half the predicted rate? The continuum rule already includes heat conduction, and the project’s hybrid, which adds a surface-attachment term and a near-surface vapour jump, scored almost identically (86.88 against 87.45), so neither addition closes the gap. The record lists what the fitted factor might be absorbing: the levitation electric field, the apparatus itself, and shape, since the frozen droplets may not have been solid single-crystal spheres. It does not list a surface-attachment barrier; whether one could account for a factor of two is a question this course raises, not one the record tested.

An all-no-pass phase leaves a sharp question behind it: could the project repair just one of the missing bridges between a laboratory record and a model output well enough to run a decisive comparison? Phase 10 set out to find out, and Chapter 33 tells that story. It is not the story of a better snowflake.
