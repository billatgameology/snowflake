Four Hundred Years of Looking

Who first asked why snowflakes have six sides — and how did anyone ever find out?

Seventeen centuries of noticing

People had been writing down that snow has six points for more than two thousand years before anyone thought to ask why. Here is the list of everyone who noticed, and did not ask.

The earliest such account we know of is Chinese, from 135 BC, by the philosopher Han Yin, who wrote: “Flowers of plants and trees are generally five-pointed, but those of snow, which are call ying, are always six-pointed.” Later Chinese writers repeated the observation. The sixth-century poet Hsiao Tung put it as “The ruddy clouds float in the four quarters of the cerulean sky. And the white snowflakes show forth their six-petaled flowers.” (Both quotations: printed p. 24.)

Europe was slow to catch up. The reference usually quoted as the European first is a woodcut printed in 1555 by the clergyman Olaus Magnus. Look at it before you accept the claim.

The better candidate is the English astronomer Thomas Harriot, who appears to have been the first in Europe to clearly identify and document the snowflake’s six-fold symmetry, in 1591 (printed p. 24). Harriot matters again in a moment, for a different reason.

Now look back over that list and check the claim. Every one of them is reporting. Han Yin says snow flowers have six points; Hsiao Tung says the same thing more beautifully; Harriot documents it carefully. None of them asks why, or suggests that there might be an answer at all. That is not a criticism — it is the ordinary state of human knowledge about almost everything. Turning a fact into a question is a separate move, and somebody has to make it.

Key idea

Noticing a pattern and demanding an explanation for it are two different acts. Seventeen centuries separate the first record of six-fold snow and the first person to treat it as a problem with a solution.

1611: Kepler asks the question

In 1611 the German astronomer and mathematician Johannes Kepler presented a small treatise called The Six-Cornered Snowflake to his patron, the Holy Roman Emperor Rudolf II, as a New Year’s Day gift. It is a peculiar present, and it is also the first time anyone speculated about an actual theory for why snow crystals are six-sided.

Kepler starts by ruling something out. Flowers show similar symmetries, so he asks whether the same explanation could cover both, and decides it cannot. Flowers are alive; a snowflake plainly is not:

“Each single plant has a single animating principle of its own, since each instance of a plant exists separately, and there is no cause to wonder that each should be equipped with its own peculiar shape. But to imagine an individual soul for each and any starlet of snow is utterly absurd, and therefore the shapes of snowflakes are by no means to be deduced from the operation of soul in the same way as with plants.”

Kepler, The Six-Cornered Snowflake, 1611, as quoted in Libbrecht, Snow Crystals, printed p. 25.

This is a better argument than it looks. Kepler is pointing out that a snowflake is a simple object — made of one substance, ice, with no organs and no plan — compared with the bewildering complexity of anything living. A simple object with a strikingly regular shape ought to have a simple organising principle behind it. So he went looking for one.

And here Thomas Harriot returns. Drawing on his correspondence with Harriot, Kepler noticed that stacking cannonballs also yields geometric structures with six-fold symmetry, and guessed that there might be a mathematical connection between the two things. He could not push the analogy far, because in 1611 the atomistic view of matter — the idea that stuff is built from enormous numbers of identical tiny units — had not been developed. But the guess pointed the right way. The geometry of stacking is exactly where the answer lives.

Nothing in that picture is about snow, or ice, or water. It is about what happens when you insist that identical round things fill space as densely as possible. Loose, they have no pattern at all; crowded to the limit, six is not chosen — six is what is left over. Kepler suspected that whatever ice is made of might be stacking in a similar way, and that the snowflake’s six corners were the stack showing through.

Kepler had the right idea and the wrong stack. Water molecules are not free to crowd like cannonballs. Each one is held at a fixed angle by its bonds to its neighbours — the two bonds inside a water molecule meet at 104.5 degrees, close enough to the tetrahedral angle of 109.5 degrees that the four bonds around each oxygen atom in ice sit in an essentially tetrahedral arrangement (printed p. 49). It is that angle, not tight packing, that forces the hexagon. Chapter 3 does the real stacking.

Kepler also spotted something else worth keeping: snow crystals and mineral crystals look like relatives. Both show symmetrical, flat-sided, faceted structures. Whatever the organising principle is, it is not special to snow. Here is the family he had in mind.

Then he did the honest thing. At the end of the treatise Kepler accepted that the science of his day was not advanced enough to explain any of it. He was right. Three centuries would pass before scientists knew enough about atoms, molecules, and their arrangement in solid materials to answer the question he had just asked.

Better eyes, better drawings

While the theory sat still, the observing got better — and it got better in step with the instruments.

The French philosopher and mathematician René Descartes recorded the first detailed account of snow-crystal structures in Les Météores in 1637, and he did it with no lens at all. He describes “little roses or wheels with six rounded semicircular teeth…which were quite transparent and quite flat.” Then comes the sentence worth stopping on:

“There followed, after this, a further quantity of such wheels joined two by two by an axle, or rather, since at the beginning these axles were quite thick, one could as well have described them as little crystal columns, decorated at each end with a six-petaled rose a little larger than their base.”

Descartes, Les Météores, 1637, as quoted in Libbrecht, Snow Crystals, printed p. 24.

Two flat plates joined by a short thick column. That object has a name now — a capped column — and it is real. Descartes saw it with unaided eyes in 1637, and you can check him against a photograph.

What Descartes did next mattered more than the drawings. He was struck by the geometrical perfection of these shapes — flat facets, exact hexagonal symmetry — and used them as part of an argument that geometry and mathematics belong at the centre of any description of the natural world. That idea is so ordinary today that it is invisible. In 1637 it was new, and snowflakes helped make the case.

The microscope arrived in the middle of the seventeenth century, and Robert Hooke pointed his at snowflakes — along with practically everything else he could find — for Micrographia in 1665.

As the quality and availability of optical magnifiers improved over the next century and a half, so did the accuracy of the drawings. The clearest example was made at sea. The English explorer William Scoresby sketched snow crystals during a winter voyage through the Arctic and recounted them in 1820, and his are the first drawings that accurately depict many features of snow-crystal structure — along with several genuinely rare forms, including triangular crystals and Descartes’s capped columns again.

Read that last sentence of the caption again, because it is the most scientific thing in this whole section. Scoresby noticed that the crystals depended on where he was — the cold Arctic gave him more highly symmetrical crystals than Britain did. That is an observation about cause, not just about appearance, and it is more than a century ahead of the person who eventually made it into a research programme.

Every observer in this section shared one crippling handicap: the subject melts. You cannot put a snow crystal on the desk and draw it at leisure. You look, it goes, and you finish the sketch from memory. The consequence is blunt. Even the best snow-crystal drawings of the era, Libbrecht writes, “lacked detail and were not completely faithful to their original subjects” (printed p. 26). Memory smooths things. It makes arms match that did not quite match, and it drops the small ugly details that were never the reason you looked in the first place.

Bentley’s five thousand plates

The memory problem was solved by a farmer. Wilson Bentley, of Jericho, Vermont, became interested in the microscopic structure of snow crystals as a teenager in the 1880s, built himself a contraption for attaching a camera to a microscope, and photographed his first snow crystal in 1885, when he was 19 years old.

He then kept going for forty-six years. Over that time he captured more than 5,000 snow-crystal images, each on a four-inch glass photographic plate, living his whole life in the same Jericho farmhouse and photographing every winter with essentially the same equipment he had built as a teenager.

Here is the technique, in Bentley’s own order of operations. A snow crystal is made of pure ice, and ice is clear, not white. Bentley lit his crystals from behind, which makes a photograph that is bright crystal on bright background — low contrast, and hard to read. To fix it he copied each glass negative — the developed plate, on which the picture appears with light and dark swapped — and painstakingly scraped away the emulsion, the thin light-sensitive coating that carries the image, from the background areas only. Making a paper print swaps light and dark back again, so a clear-scraped background prints black and the untouched crystal prints white.

Careful

If ice is clear, why is a snowbank white? Because a heap of transparent fragments scatters light in every direction, exactly as a pile of crushed glass does. Whiteness is a property of the heap, not of the ice. As for the darkroom work: some people accused Bentley of altering his photos to augment what nature had provided, but he never hid that he processed them this way, and he was always quick to point out that he never changed the snow-crystal images themselves (printed p. 27).

So the images are honest. The album is not, and this is the more interesting problem, because nothing dishonest happened anywhere in it. Photographic emulsion was expensive. Over an entire winter season Bentley photographed only about a hundred specimens on average, saving his plates for the most photogenic crystals he could find. Every choice was reasonable. The cumulative result is a body of five thousand images in which the rare is normal.

If you selected the way Bentley selected, your six plates are stars and your board is not. That gap is selection bias: a distortion produced entirely by which honest measurements get kept. It needs no fraud, no exaggeration and no bad intent, which is what makes it so hard to see from the inside.

How we know

We are not guessing about the bias, and we are not judging Bentley by taste. Modern automated cameras photograph falling snow with no human in the loop — they shoot whatever arrives — and those unbiased records confirm that well-formed stellar crystals are exceedingly rare. The check works because the selection step was removed, not because the new cameras are sharper.

One more thing came out of that album, and you have almost certainly repeated it. The familiar line that no two snowflakes are exactly alike appears to have started with Bentley’s photographs — thousands of images, all different, shown to a public that had never seen one magnified. It is a claim worth taking apart properly, and it deserves its own treatment later.

In the late 1920s Bentley worked with W. J. Humphreys, chief physicist of the United States Weather Bureau, to publish a book containing more than 2,000 of the photographs. It appeared in November 1931. The 66-year-old Vermont farmer died of pneumonia a few weeks later.

X-rays answer Kepler

Kepler’s question — why six? — was still open. It could not be settled by looking harder at snowflakes, because the thing that needed looking at was far too small to be seen with light. It took a different instrument entirely.

Two ideas have to come first, and the first one is about waves by themselves. Drop two pebbles into a still pond and watch where the ripples cross. Wherever a crest from one arrives on top of a crest from the other, the water heaves higher than either ripple could manage alone; wherever a crest arrives on top of a trough, the two cancel and the surface barely moves. Which of those you get at any particular spot depends on one thing only: how much further one ripple had to travel to reach it. Arrive having gone the same distance, or a whole number of wavelengths further, and the two are in step and reinforce; arrive half a wavelength further and crest lands on trough and they cancel. That adding and cancelling is called interference, and it is the whole mechanism behind what follows.

Now the second idea. When a wave meets a row of regularly spaced obstacles, each obstacle sends out its own wavelet, and those wavelets interfere. Look in some directions and every wavelet has travelled a whole number of wavelengths further than its neighbour, so they arrive in step and add; look in most other directions and the extra distances do not line up, so the wavelets cancel each other out. The outgoing beam therefore splits into a few sharp directions instead of spreading evenly. That splitting is called diffraction, and any regularly spaced set of obstacles that does it is a grating. Because the extra distances are set by how far apart the obstacles sit, the spacing of the obstacles sets the directions — which means the outgoing beam carries information about a spacing you never had to see.

The real breakthrough came in 1912, when the German physicist Max von Laue and co-workers shone X-rays through a crystal of copper sulphate and found that the crystal acted like a grating: the beam came out as a pattern of spots that could be recorded on photographic film. William Henry Bragg and William Lawrence Bragg, father and son, then worked out the mathematics for going backwards — from the pattern of spots to the arrangement of atoms that produced it. That is the birth of crystallography.

A general property of diffraction gratings, of which a crystal is one. The statement that a crystal “acted like a grating and produced a diffraction pattern that could be measured on photographic film” is Libbrecht, Snow Crystals, printed p. 28.

That inversion is inconvenient, but the important part is what survives it: the symmetry passes straight through. A pattern of atoms with six-fold symmetry produces a pattern of spots with six-fold symmetry — and among the lattices you can build in the panel below, only the hexagonal one puts six spots in the innermost ring.

Which brings us to a Cambridge laboratory in 1929. Working with the Braggs, William Barnes turned the method on ice and determined its structure for the first time, discovering the hexagonal lattice of ice Ih — the subscript “h” is for hexagonal — the ordinary form of ice that environmental snow crystals are made of. His discovery photograph is below.

Kepler asked in 1611; Barnes answered in 1929. Three hundred and eighteen years after the question was put, scientists had finally proven that the arrangement of molecules in the ice lattice really was the underlying source of the snowflake’s six-fold symmetry. Kepler had guessed the right kind of answer, if not the right stack.

That is a long time to hold a question open, and the word crystal carried a wrong answer for even longer. It comes from the Ancient Greek krystallos, meaning “ice” or “rock ice” — but the Greeks were not using it for ice, they were using it for quartz. The Roman naturalist Pliny the Elder explained clear quartz as a form of ice frozen so hard it could no longer melt. He was completely wrong: quartz is not ice and contains no water. Nearly two thousand years later you can still find “quartz” listed in a dictionary as a definition of crystal. Bad ideas have long lifetimes — which is worth remembering before deciding that Barnes closed the subject (printed p. 28).

Careful

It is tempting to think crystallography therefore explains snowflakes. It does not, and the distinction runs through the rest of this site. Crystallography describes the lattice: which arrangement of molecules has the lowest energy when everything has settled down. That is a statics problem, and science is good at statics. How a crystal grows is a dynamics problem — many molecules interacting, far from settled — and science is much worse at those. Barnes explained the six-fold symmetry. He did not explain a single snowflake’s shape.

Nakaya stops waiting for snow

Everyone who had ever looked at a snow crystal had the same relationship to their subject: they waited for it to fall, and took what they were given. Weather chose the sample. In the 1930s the Japanese physicist Ukichiro Nakaya, at Hokkaido University, broke that arrangement, and in doing so conducted the first true scientific investigation of snow crystals.

He began the way Bentley had, cataloguing what fell — Hokkaido gets a great deal of snow — and he was directly inspired by Bentley’s photographs. But he did not repeat Bentley’s selection. Unlike Bentley, Nakaya looked beyond stellar crystals and set out to describe the full range of snowflake types, including columns, needles, capped columns, and other less-common forms. The result was the first photographic documentation of the broader menagerie — the full zoo — of falling snow: the plain and the lumpy alongside the spectacular.

Then he changed the question from “what falls?” to “what can I make fall?” Nakaya realised that observation alone would never explain the variety, so he constructed a walk-in freezer laboratory at Hokkaido and grew snow crystals inside it, on a thin fibre suspended in a chamber whose conditions he chose. On 12 March 1936 one of them worked. It was the world’s first synthetic snow crystal.

Everything changes with that fibre. If a snowflake is something you find, all you can do is describe it and hope the pattern speaks. If a snowflake is something you can make, you can hold every condition fixed, move one of them, and watch what the crystal does about it. That is the move from natural history to experimental physics, and it is why Nakaya, rather than any of the careful observers before him, is where snow-crystal science starts.

But the whole move rests on one thing being true. A crystal you grow on a fibre in a freezer is only worth studying if it is the same object the sky makes. Nakaya put the two side by side.

He then spent years doing exactly that — varying the temperature and the amount of spare water vapour in his chamber, and recording which kind of crystal came out of each combination. What he built out of those records is a chart that turns a snowflake into a weather report. It earns a chapter of its own, later, once you have enough physics to be properly shocked by it.

Still unsettled

Ninety years on, this is not a solved subject. The crystallography of ice — Barnes’s side of the problem — has been essentially settled for decades. The other side is not. The rules governing whether a passing water molecule sticks to an ice surface or bounces off it are called the attachment kinetics — a term you will meet properly later. Building a comprehensive model of snow-crystal attachment kinetics is, in Libbrecht’s words, “very much a work in progress, with many unsolved problems still outstanding” (printed p. 30). Keep that in mind for the rest of this site. You are not being walked through a closed case.

Four centuries, one table. Read down the last column rather than the first: what moves the story forward is almost never a cleverer person, it is a better instrument, and each one changes which question can be asked next.
