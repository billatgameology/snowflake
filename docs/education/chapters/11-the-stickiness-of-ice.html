Chapter 11

The Stickiness of Ice

A water molecule slams into a snow crystal. What actually decides whether it stays?

~22 min read 10 figures 4 interactives

By the end you will be able to

  • Recognise a vicinal surface — a facet tilted by a degree or two — and say why tilting a face that little can make it a hundred times stickier.
  • Read α = A exp(−σ0/σsurf) as a threshold: σ0 is how hard it is to start a brand-new molecular layer.
  • Explain why a low σ0 means fast growth on that face.
  • Say where the published σ0 and A values came from — and where the whole scheme is openly resting on guesswork.
  • Say why the prism ceiling A sags below 1 near the melting point, and why the explanation offered for it is still a cartoon.

Does the molecule stick?

The previous chapter ended with a promise: growth measurements turned into two curves, one for each kind of face, and a single equation for reading them. Both are built out of a number you already own. Chapter 5 gave you the attachment coefficient α — the probability that a molecule striking the surface actually joins the lattice — along with the growth law v = α vkin σsurf and the nucleation barrier standing behind a facet's small α. Everything there was an idea. This is the chapter where those ideas become measured numbers, with error bars, printed in papers, and honest about where they run out.

One line of refresher, in the monograph's own words, because the exact wording carries the rest of the chapter: “One can think of α as a sticking probability, equal to the probability that a water vapor molecule striking the ice surface becomes assimilated into the crystal lattice” (printed p. 138). Being a probability it is boxed in: 0 ≤ α ≤ 1. Nothing sticks at 0; every arrival is captured at 1.

Chapter 5's growth law has a proper name, which is worth having now that we are dealing with laboratory measurements: the Hertz–Knudsen relation. Heinrich Hertz and Martin Knudsen worked out, around the turn of the twentieth century, how fast a solid grows from its own vapour given the net flux of molecules hitting it. The relation already nets the two vapour traffics against each other — molecules arriving at the surface against molecules leaving it — so what it delivers is an ideal growth speed. The attachment coefficient is the factor that separates that ideal from what the crystal actually keeps.

vn = α · vkin · σsurf

Libbrecht, Snow Crystals, Equation 4.1, printed p. 137

That is the same equation as chapter 5's, in the monograph's notation: vn is the speed of the surface measured straight out from itself, vkin the fixed conversion factor set by temperature and by how heavy a water molecule is, and σsurf the spare vapour right at the surface. Read it the same way — speed = stickiness × a fixed conversion × how much spare vapour is there. It behaves sensibly at the boring end, which is a good sign in an equation. “Note that Equation 4.1 includes the trivial case of a vapor/solid interface in equilibrium; if the supersaturation σsurf is zero, then the growth rate must also be zero” (printed p. 138). No spare vapour, no growth, no matter how sticky the ice is.

Careful

α is not a property of ice, the way density or melting point are. It is a property of this bit of surface, right now. The value “may depend on σsurf, T, surface orientation relative to the crystal axes, and perhaps other factors” (printed p. 138). On one crystal, at one instant, in one parcel of air, a flat face can be a hundred times less sticky than the ragged surface a micron away from it. Those differences are where snowflake shape comes from.

And the connection to shape is blunt. A crystal spreads sideways into a plate when its side faces capture molecules much more readily than its top and bottom do; it stretches into a column when the reverse is true. In symbols (the sign ≪ means “is very much smaller than” — not just smaller, but smaller by a big factor): “thin platelike crystals (including stellar plates and stellar dendrites) form only when αbasal ≪ αprism while slender columnar forms require αprism ≪ αbasal” (printed p. 138). Everything else in this chapter is an attempt to work out what sets those two numbers.

Three kinds of surface, one number each

Chapter 5 sorted a snow crystal's alarming amount of surface into three classes and pinned the symbols onto a real crystal: top basal terraces with an α of their own, top prism terraces with an α of their own, and everything else — shoulders, ridges, hollows, ragged branch ends — rough, with α near 1. Here is that picture again, because every measurement in this chapter is a measurement of one of those two small numbers — and because the boundary between “flat” and “rough” turns out to be far sharper than anything has a right to be.

The classification needs only a sentence of recall, because it turns on one contrast. A faceted surface — basal or prism — is molecularly flat — an unbroken plain of molecules with almost nowhere for a newcomer to grip — and shows α values “orders of magnitude lower than on non-faceted surfaces” (printed p. 139), under 0.01 not unusually. A rough surface is crowded with one-molecule steps and exposed bonds, so an arriving molecule is captured essentially on contact, becoming “immediately indistinguishable” from the ice already there (printed p. 140); that is αrough ≈ 1, the fast kinetics limit.

What chapter 5 did not need, and this chapter does, is the case in between. A vicinal surface is a facet cut at a slight angle — which puts a regular staircase of steps across it. The shallower the tilt, the further apart the steps. Whether such a surface behaves as flat or as rough comes down to a race. A molecule that lands on one of the terraces between the steps is at first only loosely held — an admolecule, sitting on the crystal but not yet part of it. It skates about for a while and then heat throws it back into the air. But if it meets a step edge first, it drops into the lattice properly and is captured for good. So “it is not uncommon to have αfacet < 0.01 while αvicinal ≈ 1 at a vicinal angle of just a degree or two” (printed p. 141). Tilt a face by one degree and its stickiness can jump a hundredfold.

The monograph states that race as a plain inequality. Call the average gap between steps ℓvicinal, and call the typical distance an admolecule covers before it evaporates the diffusion length, xdiff. Then “αvicinal ≈ 1 when ℓvicinal < xdiff, and αvicinal → αfacet when ℓvicinal → ∞” (printed p. 140). Steps closer together than a molecule can walk: everything gets caught. Steps further apart than that: almost nothing does.

That sharpness is the whole reason the classification works. α does not drift smoothly as you swing the surface around; it has deep, narrow cusps sitting exactly at the principal facet orientations, and is close to 1 everywhere else. Which licenses a drastic simplification: “Essentially all non-faceted ice/vapor surfaces can be described with α ≈ αrough ≈ 1 to a reasonable degree of accuracy. Thus, the only thing left to define the overall morphology of a snow crystal is αbasal and αprism” (printed p. 141).

Careful

Chapter 5 spent three independent checks killing the popular explanation — that flat faces appear because flatness has the lowest surface energy — so this chapter will not re-run them. The one-line version: the anisotropy is real but “just too weak to significantly affect snow-crystal growth or facet development” (printed p. 139), and facets survive instead because “the rough surfaces quickly accumulate material and fill in, while the faceted surfaces accumulate material at a much slower rate” (printed p. 141).

Why a flat face is hard to grow on

So why is a facet slow? Chapter 5 gave you the answer in outline — a facet has to build each new layer out of nothing. What it did not give you is the full inventory of what an arriving molecule can do, and one item on that list is about to become important twice.

Six things can happen on that surface, and the monograph names them all (printed p. 138). A molecule can deposit — land and stick loosely, becoming the admolecule met in the last section, not yet properly part of the crystal. It can sublimate — get thrown back into the air by thermal jostling, which lone admolecules are “especially likely” to do. It can diffuse — skate sideways across the terrace, which is easy, because sliding along is only weakly resisted. It can attach — reach a step edge and lock into the lattice properly. It can be blocked by the Ehrlich–Schwoebel barrier, an energy penalty that makes it hard for an admolecule to hop down off the terrace it is on. And finally, several admolecules can meet and nucleate a terrace: clump together into a brand-new island of ice one molecule thick.

On a large flat facet there are no step edges to run to. “In the absence of nearby terrace edges to bind to, a typical admolecule will reside on the surface for only a short time before thermal fluctuations send it back into the vapor phase” (printed p. 141). The face cannot advance at all unless a new island manages to start. That is the bottleneck.

And starting one is genuinely hard. That is chapter 5's island story, so take it compressed. A small island is nearly all edge; edge costs energy at a rate called the step energy, written β; so below a critical radius Rcrit = a2β / (σsurfkT) an island is more likely to fall apart than to survive, and at a realistic 1 per cent surface supersaturation that threshold sits at about ten molecular widths — roughly 300 water molecules in all (printed p. 142). The surface is therefore “a maelstrom of molecular activity… Small terrace islands are constantly forming and disintegrating” (printed p. 141). Only the rare oversized one wins. Hold on to β, though: it is the one quantity from that story that this chapter is going to measure.

Key idea

Growth on a flat face is not a steady trickle. It is a lottery: the face waits, does nothing, waits — and then one island happens to clear the threshold and a whole molecular layer sweeps across. Everything that follows in this chapter is an attempt to say how often that lottery pays out.

There is one kind of crystal this entire picture leaves out, and it is worth naming before going any further.

Careful

The bottleneck, the threshold and the lottery all rest on an assumption stated a page earlier in the monograph, and stated plainly: “We further assume a perfect crystalline structure, free from dislocations and other lattice defects” (printed p. 141). A crystal whose lattice has a flaw called a screw dislocation carries a permanent step that can never run out — the step winds round and round the defect like a spiral staircase, so the face keeps advancing without ever having to start a new island. Such a crystal shows “substantial growth rates even when σsurf is far below that required for normal terrace nucleation” (printed p. 168). Everything in this chapter is about the defect-free case. That is a reasonable place to stand rather than a free one: “up to 90 percent of well-formed samples are consistent with nucleation-limited growth [2013Lib, 2019Lib2], at least at temperatures above -20 C” (printed p. 169).

One number per face: the nucleation barrier

Statistical mechanics has a well-worn machinery for working out how often a rare threshold event happens. Beginning in the 1930s, I. N. Stranski, R. Kaischew, R. Becker and W. Döring, and M. Volmer built exactly this theory for the birth of one-molecule-high terraces on flat crystal faces; W. K. Burton, N. Cabrera and F. C. Frank extended it in 1951 into the modern theory of crystal growth (printed pp. 29–30). Feeding a growing ice facet through that machinery gives a remarkably compact answer.

α(σsurf) = A e−σ0/σsurf

where
α
the sticking probability on this facet
σsurf
the spare vapour just above the surface
σ0
the nucleation barrier, expressed in the same units as σsurf: roughly, how much spare vapour this face needs before new layers start with any regularity
A
the ceiling — the value α climbs towards once the barrier stops mattering. You would expect 1.

Libbrecht, Snow Crystals, Equation 4.4, printed p. 142

The theory also hands over the barrier itself, and it turns out to depend on almost nothing. The monograph's Equation 4.5 writes σ0 in terms of the step energy β, the size of a water molecule, the temperature, and one dimensionless geometrical fudge factor of about 1 dropped in to absorb several small theoretical corrections — and β enters squared, so the barrier is exquisitely sensitive to it (printed p. 142). You will not need to compute with it. What you need is the conclusion, and the conclusion is what makes the whole scheme worth trusting: “over a broad range of surface characteristics, the terrace step energy β is the only parameter that has a substantial effect on σ0” (printed p. 143). Expensive edges mean a stiff barrier. Every messy detail of how admolecules hop, how long they linger, how many islands are alive at once — all of it washes out.

Now go back to Equation 4.4 and read it as a threshold, because that is what an exponential of −σ0/σsurf is. If the spare vapour is well below the barrier, the ratio in the exponent is large and negative, α is minuscule, and the face is effectively dead. Push σsurf up towards σ0 and α climbs steeply. Push it well past σ0 and the exponential factor goes to 1, so α flattens out at A. The face has stopped caring about the barrier altogether: nucleation has become so easy that the surface is dense with steps and behaves as if it were rough.

There is a trap in the sign here, so it is worth saying out loud. Because σ0 sits in a negative exponent, a bigger σ0 means a smaller α. Or, as the monograph puts it: “A lower nucleation barrier (i.e., a lower σ0) means faster attachment kinetics on the corresponding facet surface” (printed p. 153). Low barrier, fast face.

Notice how brutal the curve is. Over most of its range α is not merely small, it is small by factors of thousands, and it changes by factors of thousands for changes in σsurf that look modest on a linear axis. This is the single most important non-linearity in the subject. A patch of surface that gets slightly less vapour than its neighbour does not grow slightly slower — it can grow hundreds of times slower, and stay behind forever.

Be careful what that steepness explains, though. It is an amplifier, not a cause. Two faces with identical barriers would be amplified identically, and a crystal made of them would keep the same proportions at every temperature. What turns a snowflake's shape over in a few degrees is that the two faces do not have identical barriers, and that the gap between them changes with temperature. Which two barriers, and how they move, is the next chapter.

The shape of the α curve is also a fingerprint you can go and look for in a laboratory. If growth really is limited by the birth of new terraces, then measured growth speeds must bend upwards in exactly this way. They do.

Key idea

A whole face of a snow crystal — a maelstrom of billions of molecules landing, skating and leaving — is summarised by two numbers: a barrier σ0 and a ceiling A. Of the two, σ0 does nearly all the work, and it traces back to a single equilibrium property of the surface, the step energy β.

Where the numbers actually come from

A formula with two free parameters per face is only worth having if somebody has gone and measured them. This is the part it would be easy to skate over, so let us be explicit about the chain of steps.

You grow a single small ice prism under controlled conditions and measure how fast one of its faces advances, to a precision of nanometres per second, over a range of supersaturations and temperatures. That gives you vn. You divide out vkin and σsurf using the Hertz–Knudsen relation, which converts each speed into a value of α. You then fit α = A exp(−σ0/σsurf) through those points to extract A and σ0 at that temperature. Repeat at many temperatures and you have curves. The monograph describes precisely this sequence (printed p. 145), and is candid about what it produces: those curves “are little more than parameterized fits to experimental data.”

The captions on the next few figures call the result the CAK model, short for Comprehensive Attachment Kinetics. It is worth knowing what that name covers: it is a label for one agreed set of fitted σ0(T) and A(T) curves, not a rival theory sitting behind them. The theory is the terrace nucleation of the previous two sections; the CAK model is the bookkeeping.

Every value below was measured on a broad facet — a face wide enough for the terrace-nucleation picture above to apply to it cleanly, in an experiment designed to keep it that way. Figure 4.3 said that a finished snow crystal is steered by the topmost facet terraces at its branch tips, and on a developed crystal those terraces are not broad at all — some are mere slivers. Nothing guarantees that a sliver obeys the numbers measured on a wide plain of the same crystal plane. Two things jump out of that table. The first is how strongly the barrier moves with temperature: the prism barrier at −15 °C is roughly an order of magnitude higher than at −5 °C. Treat that factor loosely, because the two ends of the comparison come from different experiments — one prism facet on one crystal, fitted in the monograph, against a value from a separate later paper — and the smooth σ0,prism curve in Figure 4.5 climbs more gently than a straight ratio of the two printed numbers suggests. The direction is not in doubt, though, and since σ0 sits in an exponent even a modest climb is an enormous change in stickiness for ten degrees. The second thing is the prism A value at the warm end — 0.25 and 0.2, nowhere near 1. Hold that thought.

How we know

The strongest argument that these numbers mean something is that separate experiments, built differently, land on the same answer. At −2 °C four independent data sets were collected: two different laboratory instruments, crystals grown on the ends of ice needles, and a free-fall growth chamber. That last one matters, because whatever a crystal is resting on is exactly the sort of thing that could quietly seed new terraces and fake a low barrier — the monograph flags “substrate-induced basal nucleation” as a real practical limit elsewhere in the same chapter (printed p. 162).

There is one more check available, and it is the elegant one. Because σ0 is tied to the step energy β, you can run the whole chain backwards: take σ0(T) extracted from growth speeds, invert Equation 4.5, and you have measured β — an equilibrium property of the ice surface, obtained entirely from watching crystals grow. β is otherwise very hard to get at, and in fact “growth-rate experiments have yielded the best measurements of β on the ice facets” (printed p. 143). What comes out is at least the right size: in the cold limit it heads for the value expected of a rigid lattice, and at the warm end the basal value heads for the one measured for ice growing in liquid water. Both targets come from completely different experiments, and the monograph reports that “these statements appear to fit the data” (printed p. 145).

Still unsettled

Smooth curves are a presentation choice, and the monograph says so plainly: the smooth presentation “glosses over experimental uncertainties and the inevitable inconsistencies between published data sets” (printed p. 144). Published values for the same barrier at the same temperature do not always agree. To pick one concrete case: the basal barrier at −5 °C is printed as 0.7 % in one paper and 0.73 % in another (arXiv:1912.03230v1 p. 3; arXiv:1912.09440v1 p. 11) — a small disagreement, but a real one, and it is the sort of thing a smooth curve hides. Treat a barrier value as good to a few tens of percent, not to three decimal places.

When the surface is not rigid: why A is less than one

Everything so far has quietly assumed a hard, rigid ice surface: a lattice of fixed sites with molecules hopping between them. Close to the melting point that assumption starts to fail, and Figure 4.11 already told you one consequence — the step structure “will become less rigid…, which should substantially reduce the Ehrlich-Schwoebel barrier.” A soft step no longer traps admolecules on the terrace they landed on, so the neat separation between one terrace and the next begins to blur. That is the promised thread, and it is real. But it is not the strangest thing a softened surface does. The strangest thing is what it does to the ceiling.

That ceiling is A: the value α settles at once the barrier has been overwhelmed. At that point nucleation is happening constantly, the surface is thick with steps, and — on the argument from earlier in this chapter — it should behave like a rough surface. Which means A should be 1.

For the basal faces it is: “we see Abasal ≈ 1 at all temperatures in Figure 4.5…, along with Aprism ≈ 1 at temperatures below -10 C. Consequently, it appears that the high-temperature region with Aprism < 1 is something of an anomaly” (printed p. 146). Above about −10 °C the prism faces are stubbornly slower than they have any right to be, even when there is plenty of vapour and no barrier left to speak of.

You can see the consequence directly. A crystal grown at −0.5 °C, where the spare vapour was far above the barrier, still came out sharply prism-faceted — not because starting layers was hard, but because the prism ceiling itself is low.

So what is throttling the prism faces? The honest answer is given first: “the short answer is that this is an empirical observation with no obvious physical explanation” (printed p. 146). But there is a suggestive coincidence. The temperature where Aprism starts dropping below 1 is about the temperature where the ice surface starts wearing chapter 5's quasi-liquid layer. That film reaches about one molecular layer thick around −10 °C and keeps thickening without limit as the temperature climbs towards 0 °C (printed p. 55). The two temperatures line up.

The proposed picture runs like this. When a thick quasi-liquid layer is present, a molecule arriving from the air does not join the lattice directly; it has to work its way down through that film to the ice underneath. In the Wilson–Frenkel description of a solid growing from a liquid, the sticking probability is inversely proportional to the liquid's viscosity. Ice growing in ordinary liquid water puts on prism faces extremely fast, which is exactly why you would expect the film to be no obstacle at all. The measurements say otherwise, so the film must not behave like bulk water. And there is independent support: a molecular-dynamics study found the near-surface layer to be “up to 1000 times higher” in viscosity than bulk water, “and is especially high on the prism facet” (printed p. 146). A molasses skin, not a water skin.

The idea is not idle: it makes a testable prediction. If mobility rises as the film thickens, then Aprism should climb back towards 1 as the temperature approaches 0 °C (printed p. 146). There is a hint of that upturn in the existing data and not much more. It is exactly the sort of prediction that a targeted experiment could confirm or kill.

Still unsettled

Blaming the quasi-liquid layer is a popular move in this field and has been for decades, but the connection has never been nailed down. As of the monograph: “there seems to be no concrete, well-established physical connection between surface melting and snow crystal growth, although this situation may change at any time” (printed p. 55). And even within the proposed picture, it remains unexplained why the prism faces should be throttled when the basal faces are not.

It is worth being clear about what has and has not been established. That growth on a large flat facet is limited by the birth of new terraces, and that α therefore takes the form A exp(−σ0/σsurf), is on firm ground: the functional form comes from theory that predates ice research by decades, it is “especially robust” within that theory (printed p. 143), and repeated independent measurements land on it. The values of σ0 are honest fits to real data with real error bars. The story about why Aprism dips below 1 is a cartoon, and is labelled as one by the person who drew it.

Two faces, two barriers, and — so long as neither ceiling is dragging — one rule, in chapter 5's words rather than new ones: the face with the lower σ0 grows faster, and a face that grows fast grows itself off the crystal, which is why the slow one is the face you end up looking at. So plot both barriers against temperature, put them on the same axes, and watch what they do to each other — because they do not stay in the same order.
