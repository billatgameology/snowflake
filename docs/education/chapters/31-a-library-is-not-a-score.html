Chapter 31

A Library Is Not a Score

A century of people have grown ice in laboratories and written down what they saw. Why can you not just put all their numbers in one spreadsheet and grade the model against the column?

~16 min read5 interactives0 solver runs in this phase

By the end you will be able to

  • Name five different laboratory ways of measuring a growing ice crystal, and say what each one actually records.
  • Explain what an operand is and why two numbers need matching apparatus, observable and history before they can be compared.
  • State the two-witness rule and say why only one of the 16 targets in the first Phase 8 book met it.
  • Read the labels P0, P1 and P2 as roles rather than grades.
  • Say what a source-blocked operand is, and why writing “not recoverable” is more useful than guessing.

Where this chapter sits — Phase 8, complete 2026-08-12

Phase 8 ran no solver. It produced two things, and this chapter keeps them apart by name. The target book (Phase 8A) has 18 entries: 16 targets and two calibration inputs, each with a robustness class. The benchmark library (Phase 8B), built after a rescoping decision, has 51 records that point at 252,134 native data rows and 431 digitized plot points, each with a role P0, P1 or P2. Every one of the 51 is labelled development evidence: Phase 9 may use it to build and reject ideas, but agreement with it never counts as confirmation, “even when a source replay agrees” (project charter, Phase 9). None is held out. The library scored nothing and validated nothing. That was the point.

Fifty-one records, no two alike

Imagine collecting reports from many kitchens. One cook records the oven temperature every second. Another photographs the crust after an hour. A third changes the recipe halfway through and only writes down the final weight. You have a rich library. You do not have fifty-one measurements of the same thing, and if you subtracted every number from one model prediction and averaged the result, the average would mean nothing.

Snow-crystal experiments differ in exactly this way. Some measure how fast a face advances. Some sort the finished crystal into plate or column. Some follow one crystal’s mass second by second. The crystals hang in an electric field, or sit on a sapphire window, or fall through a cloud tunnel, or drop through thin helium, or grow in air at a mere 40 pascals. The air pressure, the gas, the temperature control, the crystal size and even the definition of “growth” change from paper to paper.

Phase 8 was the project’s attempt to gather that literature honestly. Chapter 17 taught you why the model’s own inputs cannot validate it: the M1 dips were placed to agree with the Nakaya diagram, so agreement with Nakaya is in-sample. If the model is ever to face evidence it was not built from, somebody first has to find that evidence, record exactly what each measurement is, and refuse to blur the differences. The chapter title is the rule the project wrote for itself: what came out is a library, not a score.

Read down the “how it is held” column and the problem is already visible. A crystal resting on a window sheds the heat of freezing into the window; a floating one cannot. A crystal falling through a tunnel meets fresh air; a resting one sits in air it has already emptied. At 40 Pa vapour reaches a face so easily that the surface itself sets the growth rate; at sea-level pressure the slow diffusion of vapour through air sets it. None of these is a worse experiment than the others. They are answers to different questions.

Careful

“More data means more certainty.” Only if the data measure the same thing. The levitation chamber alone contributes 252,040 rows and the Takahashi habit boundaries contribute two numbers, but the two numbers answer a question the 252,040 rows cannot: at what temperature the shape flips. A pile that is mostly one instrument does not become an independent second opinion by being large.

Match the question before the answer

A comparison is a subtraction: a measured value minus a predicted one. Each side of the subtraction is an operand, the plain arithmetic word for “one of the things you operate on”. Phase 8’s central rule is that the two operands have to be the same kind of thing before anyone is allowed to subtract them.

“The same kind of thing” means more than the same units. If a paper reports a crystal’s width after twenty minutes at −15 °C on a sapphire window in air at one atmosphere, then the model operand has to be a width, defined the same way, at twenty minutes, at −15 °C, with the window’s effect on the vapour and heat represented or explicitly declared missing, in air at one atmosphere. Drop any one of those and the subtraction silently changes the question. That is why each Phase 8 record carries the apparatus, the observable, the environment, the time or size window, how the numbers were recovered from the paper, and the limits that travel with them.

Key idea

A number is not evidence for or against a model until you can say what the model would have to output to be compared with it. Phase 8 wrote that down for every record before any model was allowed near the shelf.

Two witnesses

The project’s charter gave every target a robustness class, and the rule is short enough to quote in full:

“Class A requires at least two independent witnesses from different laboratories and methods; Class B is single-laboratory or protocol-bound; Class C records a standing disagreement.”
snowflake repository, project charter.md — Phase 8

This is the same instinct a court has. One witness can be honest and still be wrong about what they saw, because their apparatus, their calibration, their way of counting is a single point of view. Two laboratories using two methods who nevertheless agree are much harder to explain away.

Now the sobering count. Of the 16 targets in the first Phase 8 book (18 entries, two of them calibration inputs that could never be targets), exactly one reached Class A, and it did so conditionally: Takahashi’s free-falling crystals gained mass like time to the power 1.5, and Bacon’s levitated particles grew in radius like the square root of time. Those are the same law if the crystal keeps roughly the same shape and density, because mass goes as radius cubed, and the cube of a square root is the power 1.5. The other fifteen targets, and both inputs, were Class B. None was Class C. One candidate disagreement, about the cold end below −20 °C, could not be filed because the papers that govern it were never acquired and extracted; the other, two laboratories’ fitted attachment coefficients, turned out to compare unlike quantities, and a disagreement between unlike quantities is not a disagreement at all.

P0, P1, P2 are roles, not grades

When the library was reshaped into its 51-record form, each record was given one of three letters. It is tempting to read them as marks out of three. They are not. They say what kind of evidence the record is. They are also unrelated to the provenance classes P1 to P4 of Chapter 16, which grade where a model input came from; the clash of letters is unlucky, and the record uses both.

  • P0 is native longitudinal data: a measurement followed through time, in the laboratory’s own numbers, not read off a plot. The 16 levitation mass histories and the two −50 °C dimension histories are the 18 P0 records. The record writes down their limits too: the levitation runs never observed the crystal’s habit, and twelve of the sixteen mass traces contain at least one measured decrease that a later phase must not smooth away or force to be monotonic.
  • P1 is a direct measurement that discriminates between candidate model mechanisms (development only: it can reject a mechanism, never certify one): the 26 digitized plot series from Libbrecht, Sei and Gonda, and Gonda and Komabayasi, plus Bacon’s two reported aggregates. 28 records.
  • P2 is an interpretation safeguard: a rule needed to use P0 and P1 correctly, such as “the 48 % and 20 % labels in this archive have no stated reference, so do not convert them into a model forcing”. Five records, zero measurement coordinates.

How the plot points were made

Digitizing a plot means placing a cursor on every printed marker and reading off its coordinates. Phase 8 did this twice, with two readers, and kept the disagreements. The first operator registered 33 expected markers for each of three Libbrecht 2011 series; both readers independently counted 37 in each, and the registered count had to be corrected before any coordinate was used. The first round of adjudication (the step where the two readers’ clicks are reconciled) was thrown out entirely because 45 of the 431 marker positions were still empty and two temperature assignments were wrong. The corrected map, 431 points, is what the library holds.

Why not one score

A single score feels decisive, and that is the danger. Suppose you did average everything. The 252,040 levitation rows from one instrument would drown the 50 digitized points from another. A unit conversion that is legitimate for one family (pixels to micrometres, given the figure’s axis) is impossible for another (a percentage whose reference nobody stated). Missing uncertainty cannot be repaired by averaging harder. And the moment a single number exists, people start optimizing the model against it; the charter allows that kind of development, but it is explicit that agreement reached that way is never validation.

“Not recoverable” is a data value

Sometimes the honest entry in a record is that a number cannot be had. The paper whose methods section would say what an archive’s forcing labels are measured against sits behind a paywall, and no public copy was found; two markers at the low end of a Sei and Gonda curve merge with the curve itself, so the readers refused to place them rather than guess; an archive labels its forcing “48 %” without saying 48 % of what. The missing quantity is what Phases 9 and 10 would later call a source-blocked operand (Phase 8’s own records say “source gap” or “not numerically recoverable”): the comparison is fully specified, and one required input cannot be reconstructed from evidence the project actually holds.

Writing that down is useful in two directions. It stops a later model from quietly guessing a number, and it tells a human exactly which paper or which measurement would unblock the comparison. Phase 8 recorded several such gaps: the full text of Keller and Hallett’s 1982 ventilation experiments was never acquired, so the library holds only an abstract-level lead; Bailey and Hallett’s later habit diagrams below −20 °C were never acquired, so the cold end has no quantitative target; no laboratory experiment matched the curvature (Gibbs–Thomson) question, so that stayed a numerical diagnostic.

What the library cost

Chapter 29 told you that carefulness has a budget. Phase 8 overspent it and then corrected course, and the record is candid about both halves. The first plan called for an exhaustive census: inventory every measurement-bearing document, search the outside literature under a registered protocol, and give every unit a final label. Before a single search ran, the search protocol had grown to 21,695 lines of code with 29 saved output files and 113 mutation controls (deliberately corrupted inputs the checker had to catch), and it was rejected five times in review. The census then inspected 1,242 pages of 49 papers and extracted zero numeric rows, because it was a discovery pass: it found and sorted documents, and by its own rules did not count as reading them.

The next day a decision record rescoped the phase. Its own words: the maker accepted a correction to “close a Phase-9-ready priority benchmark corpus rather than an exhaustive measurement corpus” (decision 0048). Under that narrower goal, the actual scientific payload (parsing two native archives, digitizing 26 plot series, recording two Bacon aggregates) landed the same morning: by the commit record, about four and a half hours separate the rescope from the closure. The lesson is the one the working rules now state outright: process that is “demonstrably redundant, self-defeating, disproportionate to its risk, or displacing the real work” should be named and stopped, however much has already been spent on it (AGENTS.md, Rule 14).

One more check is worth knowing about precisely because it failed. A registered audit of nine sample records found one container of Bacon data that the census had missed. The record keeps the failed audit as it stood, promotes the two Bacon aggregates that container held, reviews a registered replacement, and only then reports a corrected sample with zero misses. A check that can fail, and whose failure is preserved, is worth more than one that always passes.

Where the rows actually live

The 252,134 native rows and the 431 digitized points are not in the public repository. The project never established that it had the right to redistribute them, so the tracked records hold each file’s identity, its cryptographic hash, its schema and a locator, while the bytes sit on the project’s private storage. Anyone with the repository can verify that a file is the right file; only the project can read its numbers. Chapter 33 will show what that arrangement costs when the storage is unavailable at the wrong moment.

The set that was supposed to be sealed

Chapter 17 introduced the difference between in-sample and held-out evidence, and the first Phase 8 book took it seriously: seven of its targets were pre-registered as held out, sealed away so that a future test could face data nobody had consulted while building the model. In the successor library that number is zero: every one of its 51 records is labelled development evidence.

What happened is instructive rather than scandalous. The exploratory census exposed the outcomes of those experiments while the next phase was being designed, and an earlier audit (decision 0043, before Phase 8 began) had already found that none of the four validation families assigned to Phase 7 (growth rates, size-dependent habit, pressure dependence, growth history) was yet apples-to-apples with the solver as built. Decisions 0047 and 0048 ruled that any measurement whose outcome had shaped the Phase 9 design counted as development evidence, and decision 0050 wrote the consequence into the charter. The seven sealed entries still stand in the frozen first book as history; the successor library that Phase 9 actually uses has none. The charter says it plainly: the successor corpus “contains zero held-out records”.

Still unsettled

With no held-out records in the library, nothing on these shelves can upgrade the model to “quantitatively validated”. The charter still names two routes that could: Phase 6’s Nakaya comparison, which the model has already failed once, and Phase 7’s separately gated held-out families, which need new measurements or a genuinely unseen dataset frozen before its values are inspected. Whether such a dataset exists for a lattice model of crystals tens of micrometres across, growing in still air at one atmosphere, is an open question, and it is the question Phase 7 inherited.

Phase 8 did not decide whether the simulator was good. It made some questions answerable and made the unanswerable ones visibly unanswerable. Phase 9 then asked the natural next thing: of all the mechanisms people have proposed for why ice grows the way it does, which ones can be rejected cheaply, before anyone spends months building them into the solver?
