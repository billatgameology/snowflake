Chapter 30

Finished, Failed, and Not Yet Built

How can an experiment fail and still finish a phase — while the next phase, described in great detail, has not started at all?

~9 min read4 interactives1 table

By the end you will be able to

  • Separate a scientific outcome from the question of whether a planned procedure finished.
  • Explain why Phase 6 could close after the model failed its named comparison.
  • Tell a product specification from a product that has actually been built.
  • Describe the three large jobs still owned by Phase 7 without pretending they happened.
  • Read the status of Phases 6–10 without turning one phase’s credit into another’s.

Current boundary — 2026-08-25 evidence state

Phase 6 is complete on an accepted negative finding. Phase 7 may begin without waiting on Phases 8–10, but it has not started. Phases 8 and 9 are complete as development work. Phase 10 is complete-negative. None of those later phases earned a quantitative-validation claim. This chapter teaches the final recorded state; Chapters 28 and 29 preserve the earlier cliffhanger as history.

The ending changed

Chapter 29 ended while Phase 6 was still open. The project had two large historical sweeps: the broad-facet CAK run matched 3 of 90 scored conditions, and the Nakaya-informed M1 run matched 54 of 90 on their common scope. Those numbers were real measurements of those exact programs. They were not the final gate, because the registered numerical checks and reporting rules were still unfinished.

The final Phase 6 work did not rescue the model. A third arm (an arm is one complete sweep of the same conditions under one set of physics choices) ran the M1 configuration with its two Nakaya-informed dip factors switched off, identical to M1 in every other registered respect, and agreed at 5 of 90 on the same common scope. That “common scope” needs one clause of explanation: it is CAK’s frozen 90-point scoring rule re-applied to the other arms’ measured classes, a re-scoring rather than a second measurement; M1’s own registered scope was 78 points, on which it scored 54. An 80-row numerical ladder then returned NO-PASS at both grid spacings (the two sizes of grid cell that were tested): 28 of 64 registered comparisons failed the rule that attached-cell counts (the number of grid cells the crystal has claimed, the model’s stand-in for mass) must agree within half a percent, so every tally above carries the caveat that its grid resolutions are published as not converged. The ladder’s verdict covers only the smallest registered crystal sizes at four check points; the larger size stratum was never verified. The project reported all of it, ran its closure gate with no flags (no command-line switches that could soften a check) on 2026-08-20 (node runner/src/main.ts gate6, exit 0 at commit 44488ab), and accepted the failure to reproduce the Nakaya diagram as the finding. The gate passed 13 of 13 closure checks. No Evidence label was upgraded: the phase closed with zero “quantitatively validated” claims.

Two results from one gate

The word pass is dangerous without an object. “The model passed” asks whether nature and simulation agreed under a named test. “The closure gate passed” asks whether the project actually ran what it promised, preserved the failures, stated the limits, and stopped without rewriting the marking scheme. Phase 6 answered the first question no and the second yes.

Imagine a chemistry practical. Your prediction is wrong, but your notebook contains every measurement, the scale was checked, the uncertainty is shown, and the conclusion says the prediction was wrong. The experiment failed to support the prediction. The practical was still completed honestly. Calling the notebook incomplete until the prediction becomes right would reward cheating.

Careful

“Complete” is not a synonym for “succeeded”. In this project the word marks that a phase reached the terminal rule written for it, either in advance or, as with Phase 6, amended in the open by a numbered decision record before the end. Phase 6 is complete and its model failed the comparison; both halves are true at once, and the rest of Part Two depends on keeping them apart. When you read a status table, always ask which of the two the word is doing.

The rule that made a negative result possible

The charter said in advance, in the sentence Chapter 17 quotes in full, that “A negative result is a result”: a negative comparison completes Phase 6 when agreements and disagreements are both reported. It also forbade quiet tuning until the diagram matched. That is why “the model lost” could be an ending instead of a demand for another knob.

How failure finished a phase

A phase is a contract for work, not a promise of good news. Phase 6 promised a comparison, numerical controls, preserved artifacts, a readable report, and a gate that rebuilt the verdict from those artifacts. Once those existed, the scientific answer was allowed to be negative. Completion says the question was asked under the amended contract. It does not say the hoped-for answer arrived.

The word amended is doing real work there. Two weeks before the close, decision 0045 bounded what remained to one week of computing and closed three registered items as “not computed”: the conservative scoring headline that would have combined measured and extrapolated classes, the production replacement run that Chapter 29 called R15, and the full three-arm campaign of 5,508 rows. The maker’s recorded reason was that the phase’s answer was already known and more compute would not change it. Amending a contract in the open, with a numbered record, is the honest alternative to either quietly abandoning the obligations or pretending they were met.

The phase that has not happened

Phase 7 is written in the charter in enough detail to sound real. The charter gives it four view profiles, a polished timeline, smooth ice rendering, save/load experiments, comparisons, a local gallery, and 3-D-print export. It also owns two serious scientific-engineering jobs deferred from Phase 6: a GPU-parity campaign of at least 200 automated runs, intended to cover all 204 sweep points for each of the three arms, and four named held-out comparison families (growth rates, size-dependent habit, pressure dependence and growth history) that the decision records say cannot be averaged together or discharged by the Nakaya comparison.

But a specification is not an implementation. No Phase 7 plan has been adopted, no working copy of the code has been opened for it, and no Phase 7 gate has run. The old development instrument from Phases 3–5 exists; the polished Phase 7 product does not. This is not a failure. It is a status statement.

Four views, one engine

The clever part of the Phase 7 design is that its profiles are not four different simulators. They are four windows onto one engine and one saved environment schedule. A designer might say “make it wider, then sprout branches”; the compiler would turn that intent into a temperature-and-vapour history. A scientist could inspect the same history as numbers. A realistic view could render the resulting ice. A developer could replay the exact scene for an explainer video. Changing the window must not change the crystal underneath it.

The work that travels forward

Moving work to another phase does not make it disappear. Phase 7 still owns three different kinds of work. The product work asks whether people can actually use the instrument. GPU parity asks whether the fast graphics-card version, which stores numbers to about seven digits, agrees with the slow processor version that keeps sixteen and acts as the answer key, once the latest symmetry rule has been carried across to it. Held-out validation asks whether the model can face data that did not help design it, with apparatus and observable made genuinely comparable first.

The status map below is the shortest honest summary. Notice that complete appears beside several different scientific outcomes. Phase 8 completed a data-building job. Phase 9 completed an all-no-pass model-development campaign. Phase 10 completed a negative evidence package. The shared word tells you the planned work reached its terminal rule; the words after it tell you what was learned.

Still unsettled

The no-dip arm’s 5 of 90 against M1’s 54 of 90 says the two dip factors carried almost all of M1’s agreement in this solver, at this crystal size, under this frozen configuration. It does not say the dips are physically right, because they were placed using the very diagram they were scored against (Chapter 17), and the report itself calls the contrast an implementation-level statement, not a claim about nature. Whether any untuned mechanism can produce the diagram’s reversals at this crystal size is exactly the question the project has not answered. The three chapters that follow are about what the project did instead of answering it: building a measurement library, running development-only mechanism probes that all came back no-pass, and checking its own numbers and sources. None of that earned validation.

Phase 7 is the striking thing that has not happened. The next thing that did happen looked much less glamorous: people gathered measurements, tracked which laboratories shared the same apparatus and data, and refused to flatten them into one giant score. That became Phase 8 — a library designed to stop the next experiment from comparing the wrong things.
