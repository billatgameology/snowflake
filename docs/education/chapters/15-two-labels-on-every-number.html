Chapter 15

Two Labels on Every Number

How can a program honestly print −15 °C on screen and still refuse to say that this is what snowflakes do at −15 °C?

~15 min read 7 original interactives 0 borrowed figures

By the end you will be able to

  • Take any number a program shows you and say what it is, separately from what it has earned.
  • Explain why a single confidence score cannot answer three questions at once — and show that it cannot, rather than assert it.
  • Find the verb doing the smuggling in a sentence like “our physics-based simulator shows that…” — and say when a sentence has none.
  • Argue that a blank space with a stated reason is a stronger interface than a plausible number.

The project’s solver takes a temperature in real degrees. Ask it for −15 °C and a crystal grows. Nothing about that is dishonest: the temperature enters the equations as a physical quantity — saturation pressure over ice, kinetic velocity and supersaturation are all functions of it (core/src/libbrecht.ts: pSatIce, vKin, sigmaWater), and the growth command takes it on the command line (runner/src/main.ts:570–572, grow-lk --temp-c). But a second sentence lurks right behind the first, and it is a completely different claim — that the crystal which comes out at −15 °C is what nature actually grows at −15 °C.

Every honest thing this project can say, and every dishonest thing it could have said, lives in the gap between those two sentences. This chapter is about the vocabulary built to keep them apart, and the code that enforces it when a person forgets. (How temperature became a real input rather than a caption is chapter 21’s story, in its section “Where temperature stops being a label.”)

The ladder that broke

The project’s charter — the design document the rest of Part Two keeps quoting — originally had a simpler idea. Every quantity on screen would carry a confidence level: a number from 1 to 4. Low meant “don’t trust this much”; high meant “solid.” It is the design almost everybody reaches for first, and it lasted until somebody tried it on real readouts.

On 2026-07-14 it was thrown out. The reason is one sentence in the decision record that removed it, and in the charter besides: the four levels “conflated provenance, validation, and units (temperature input is not computed state; units do not imply validation)” (docs/decisions/0005-validation-scope-surface-operator-numerics.md:157–158). Three unrelated questions were wearing one hat:

  • Where did this number come from? Did you type it, did the solver compute it, or did somebody make it up?
  • Has anyone checked it against nature?
  • Does it carry a physical unit?

A single rung cannot answer all three, and you can prove it to yourself in about a minute. The demo below has five readouts from the real application, named as the application names them. One mentions d: a made-up bookkeeping quantity tracked by the project’s older, simpler surface rule — the code that decides, cell by cell, how fast vapour sticks to the ice — instead of real supersaturation. Assign each readout a level from 1 to 4. Three lamps score you, one per question.

The best any assignment can do is one lamp out of three, and one lamp can never be lit at all. That failure is the interesting one: five genuinely different origins, four rungs, so two readouts must share a level — at which point the level has stopped saying where anything came from.

The fifth origin is ticks/s, the solver’s speed: a real measurement in real units, and not a statement about snowflakes at all. The shipped application prints the live rate followed by ticks/s (instrument performance, not a model quantity) (app/src/main.ts:599). A ladder has no rung for “this is not the kind of thing you are asking about.” Two independent labels do.

Key idea

A confidence score is a compression. If the things you compressed are independent, the score is not a summary — it is a place for one of them to hide.

Two axes, not one score

What replaced the ladder is deliberately less convenient. The charter’s rule is that “every quantity shown to the user carries two independent labels, and the UI never silently upgrades either” (project charter.md:80). The two labels are:

Type — “what the quantity is: input (the temperature slider), computed state (a vapor field value), derived metric (a hollowness index), or phenomenological parameter (a G–G threshold, a noise amplitude)” (project charter.md:83). “Phenomenological” is the honest word for a knob that changes the picture and corresponds to no quantity in nature.

Evidence — “what it has earned: unvalidated; qualitatively supported; or quantitatively validated over a named domain. Only Phase 6 can grant the third, and only where the comparison actually holds” (project charter.md:84). Phase 6 is the validation phase — the one that runs the model against measurements it could disagree with. It remains active and incomplete. After an initial CAK_A1 sweep was invalidated by decision 0031, the corrected historical CAK arm completed 204 rows and measured 3 of 90. The separate historical M1 arm completed 204 rows and measured 54 of 90 on the common scope (54 of 78 on its own). Both artifacts can now be independently re-derived, but both remain measured-only: they did not execute R15, which is planned but unfrozen, unimplemented and unexecuted. The same status applies to the matched M1_NO_DIP_ABLATION; numerical adequacy, the preview-resolution GPU cohort and all four held-out families remain open, so neither result promotes the model’s Evidence label. Chapter 28 keeps the historical records and current obligations separate.

The whole force of the design is in the word independent. Knowing what a number is tells you nothing about whether anyone checked it. A temperature you typed has never been checked and is still perfectly honest. A supersaturation computed from a physical relation has also never been checked, and is also perfectly honest — it just must not be described as a result about the world.

Six readouts follow. Five are the application’s own wording; the sixth, the temperature line, is what the charter specifies for that input (project charter.md:89). Give each a Type and an Evidence. There is a trap, worth naming in advance so you can watch yourself fall into it anyway: carrying a real physical unit is not evidence of anything.

Notice where everything landed. All four rows filled; two of the three columns stayed empty. Nothing this project displays sits in quantitatively validated, and the reason is not modesty — the column has a precondition, and the precondition has not been met. The application’s status panel ends, on all three of its code paths, with the same line: all model quantities: Evidence = unvalidated (§1.5) (app/src/main.ts:573, :633, :678). The control pane beside it is titled GGThreshold dev instrument (model, unvalidated) (same file, line 1284).

The two sentences that must never merge

Here are the two sentences, in the charter’s own words, as a standing rule that binds every part of the project:

How we know

“No UI label ever claims more physical confidence than Phase 6 has earned. ‘The model was given real physics’ and ‘the model was shown to reproduce reality’ are different claims; only Phase 6 can promote the first to the second.” — project charter.md:342, one of the ten standing guardrails at lines 337–346 of the same file.

The first sentence is about what went in. The second is about what came out: a comparison the model could have lost. Only a test turns the first into the second, and only for the comparison that was tested.

In practice the weld happens on a single word. Four sentences follow. Three are the kind this guardrail forbids, and each hides the whole move in one word — the word that spends a fact about the inputs on a claim about the output. Find it. The fourth is already honest, and that one is the point of the exercise.

In the first sentence the word is shows, with physics-based holding the door open for it. The honest rewrite splits it in two. The solver is given measured attachment kinetics and a real temperature. Run it at −15 °C and it produces some habit. Whether that habit is the one nature grows at −15 °C is a separate question, this sentence does not answer it, and only Phase 6 can.

Notice what the rewrite refuses to do: it does not say which habit came out. That is deliberate. Naming the habit in the same breath as “measured attachment kinetics” is the weld again — it invites you to read the outcome as confirmed because the inputs were real. The outcome is a written-down result of this project, and chapter 28 is where it belongs, with its comparison attached.

So what would it take to promote a label? Not an opinion. The demo below is the upgrade button, and it refuses.

Two things in that refusal are worth pulling out. First, an upgrade needs a named domain, not a general endorsement: a report about growth rates says nothing about pressure dependence, so five of the six rows stay grey even after it loads. Second, P3 inputs chosen to impose agreement with the Nakaya diagram can earn only in-sample reproduction. The Arm 1 report’s sentence — “Every run is no-SDAK; SDAK appears in no source file” (research/phase6-sweep-report.md:225) — is true of the source tree and runs it records, not the current tree. M1 is now implemented as an everywhere-narrow starter approximation. Its historical arm completed at 54/90 common scope (54/78 arm scope), while CAK completed at 3/90. Both are measured-only; neither is the registered replacement gate. Their difference is confounded because broad curves and A prefactors change with the dips; a matched M1 no-dip arm remains open. Keeping P3 out of Arm 1 preserves its historical measurement outside direct Nakaya tuning, but does not upgrade it into the absent gate — the charter’s own version of one: “Set −5 °C — does a column grow? Set −15 °C — does the habit flip back to plates? A model that cannot be wrong is not attempting this problem” (project charter.md:187). Chapter 17 is about that distinction.

The most convincing fake available

Now the part that is genuinely counter-intuitive. If your model has not been checked, which readout should it print when you hover over a patch of the crystal’s surface?

The charter’s own worked example. The two readouts are verbatim from project charter.md:89; the status and the right-hand column are ours.
ReadoutStatusWhat a reader concludes
attachment rate: 91% banned …that somebody measured this.
high relative attachment tendency allowed …that the model has an opinion.

The instinct is that the first is more scientific, because it is more specific. The charter says the opposite, in a clause worth memorising: “a fake physics readout is worse than none — and a real-looking number resting on an untested model is the most convincing fake of all” (project charter.md:89). Precision is not a property of a number but a claim about how well the quantity is known, and every digit you print is another unit of that claim.

uclaimed = ½ × 10−n

where
uclaimed
the uncertainty a reader is entitled to assume when you print a number — half a unit in the last place you printed
n
the number of decimal places actually printed

Standard reading convention, not a project result. It applies to a bare numeral; a number printed with its own ± band states its uncertainty outright instead. The band it is compared against below is the real one: docs/libbrecht-parameters.md:246.

The demo below uses a genuine number from the frozen parameter table: σ0 for the basal facet at −2 °C, recorded as 0.0035 (docs/libbrecht-parameters.md:255). It was read off a plot, because neither source document prints a formula or a table, and its stated uncertainty is “±25% relative on σ0 (log-scale reading)” (same file, line 246). Where it came from is chapter 16’s subject; here, only what happens when you print it.

Careful

“Vague wording means the authors are hedging.” Sometimes. But a vague phrase over an untested model is a correctly sized claim, and a four-decimal number over the same model is an oversized one. The register the writing is in should match the evidence, not the confidence of the writer.

What the interface refuses to say

Same argument, one step further. Disclaimers are cheap; the convincing evidence that a labelling rule is real is what the program declines to print, because a blank space is the hardest thing to ship and the easiest thing to fill with something plausible.

The application can run its solver on the CPU or on the graphics card. Two readouts, the aspect ratio and the symmetry error, are full-field metrics: computing them means visiting every cell. In GPU mode the field stays on the card, so the panel would have to drag millions of cells back across the link every frame. It does not estimate them. It prints, instead:

aspect ratio: not computed in GPU mode (full-field metric) · symmetry error: not computed in GPU mode (full-field metric) (app/src/main.ts:605–606).

The same refusal appears in the depletion readout, its reason spelled out in a code comment: “the depletion HUD needs the full vapor field, and GPU snapshots carry compact counters/probes only — the panel says so instead of faking values” (app/src/main.ts:829–831). Three more refusals sit alongside it:

  • Speed is tagged as belonging to the machine, not the model: (instrument performance, not a model quantity) (app/src/main.ts:599, :650).
  • A missing measurement is written out loud: the depletion panel’s number formatter renders any non-finite value as the literal string undefined (NaN) (app/src/main.ts:807) — not a dash, not zero, not the last good value.
  • Even the renderer disowns itself: rendering backend: … (this page; not the evidence backend) (app/src/main.ts:566), so nobody mistakes what drew the picture for what produced the result.

None of that is decoration. Zero is a number a reader will believe. “Not computed” is not.

Why d is not sigma

Here is where the discipline stops being a writing style and becomes code that throws. That is a programmer’s verb and the rest of Part Two leans on it, so here is what it means: the program reaches a check it cannot satisfy and stops instead of answering. No picture, no number, no best guess — just a message naming what was wrong. It is the loudest thing a program can do, which is precisely why the awkward checks are wired to it rather than to a warning nobody reads.

The project carries two surface rules — the repository calls them surface operators — through the same machinery, permanently: “GGThreshold — the working floor is never deleted” (project charter.md:338), so that a problem in the physics can be told apart from a problem in the machinery. The Gravner–Griffeath threshold rule tracks an invented quantity called vapor mass, written d, in units that exist only inside the model. The Libbrecht kinetics rule tracks real dimensionless supersaturation relative to ice, written sigma — the quantity Part One spent a chapter on. On screen they are indistinguishable: one number per cell, coloured blue.

The comment at the top of the display module states the hazard flatly:

How we know

“The two permanent surface operators expose UNRELATED state that must never be relabeled as each other… f is NOT a reuse of G-G boundary mass b, and sigma is NOT d in different clothes.” — app/src/display.ts:3–9. The enforcement is one function down the file: “assertFieldSemanticsHonest rejects any operator/field-name combination that would show one operator’s state under the other’s label” (same file, lines 11–13).

Think about what a mislabel would buy you. A made-up quantity in made-up units would appear wearing a physical name, and nothing would look wrong: the picture is identical. That is the failure mode the project exists to refuse, so it is not left to vigilance. Try it.

Look at what an honest label contains. Neither is just a name. The G–G field reads model units (computed state, unvalidated); the Libbrecht field reads dimensionless, relative to ice (computed state, unvalidated) (app/src/display.ts:33, :43). Type and Evidence are baked into the string, and the function checks the wording too: change the text at all and you get display labels drifted from the honest fixed wording. You cannot soften a label without failing the check.

A badge you cannot take off

One more, because it is the case where a real integrity failure was caught and the fix was not “be careful.”

The application can load a saved crystal and inspect it. Those files come in two kinds: real published evidence produced by a gate run, and synthetic fixtures — made-up files the tests chew on. A review round found the harness cheerfully treating the second as the first: “the repository’s explicit synthetic NOT-gate-evidence fixtures passed the visual verifier, which then displayed and recorded them as published Pass A/B evidence” (docs/plans/phase-4-morphology-gauntlet.md:899–904). Pass A and Pass B are the two runs of Phase 4’s shape gauntlet — the same set of test crystals grown once on the borrowed threshold rule and once on the mixed-provenance Libbrecht parameterization; chapter 24 is about why there have to be two. Everything about those fixtures was internally consistent. That was the problem.

Two words are about to do a lot of work. A hash is a short fingerprint computed from the exact bytes of a file — change one byte and the fingerprint changes completely, so a fingerprint written down earlier names one specific file and no other. The ones below are 64 hexadecimal characters, usually shown truncated.

A manifest is the second word, and there is an everyday thing it is almost exactly: an exam timetable, written and signed before anybody sits down. Which candidates sit which paper, at which desk, with which calculator. Notice what a timetable does not contain: answers. It could not contain them — it was written before the exam. A manifest is the same object for a set of solver runs. It records which runs, at what settings, and with which version of the program, and nothing any run produced is inside it.

So take the fingerprint of the timetable, and what you have pinned is what was promised — never what came out. That is all “the manifest hash” means in this project, and it has teeth. Before any run starts, the program rebuilds the timetable from its own source and refuses to go on unless the rebuilt copy fingerprints to the one a reviewer signed off. If it does not match, it throws: A-EXEC-CONFIG: rebuilt Pass-A manifest differs from the reviewed freeze (runner/src/gate4a.ts:2539–2540). You cannot quietly rewrite the paper once the timetable is signed. Chapter 27 is about why it is signed first.

How we know

Pass A’s timetable is a real file — out/phase4/pass-a/manifest.json — and the exact list of what it is allowed to contain is written into the source as the Gate4AManifest type (runner/src/gate4a.ts:133–146). Per run it fixes the grid, the parameters, the random seed and the stopping rule. Once for the whole set it fixes which surface rule was used, whether the arithmetic ran on the processor or the graphics card, how many digits that arithmetic carried, a fingerprint of the solver’s own source code, and four version markers for the frozen pass criteria — the marking scheme, which is chapter 27’s subject. Every entry is something that could otherwise have been changed after the fact without leaving a trace.

The fix was to stop inferring the class and make every saved file declare it. Each one already travels with a small block of labels about itself — which run made it, which pass, which timetable it was promised under. Two more labels were added to that block, and the app now refuses to open any file whose two labels disagree with each other:

The evidence-class fields, from app/src/inspect.ts:54–60.
FieldAllowed values
evidenceClass "published-gate-evidence" | "synthetic-fixture-not-gate-evidence"
syntheticNotice "SYNTHETIC FIXTURE - NOT GATE EVIDENCE" (optional)

The obvious attack is to delete the notice. Try it, and then try the less obvious one.

The two halves are not the same kind of safeguard, and it is worth being exact about what each buys. Removing the badge is closed outright. The class is a separate field, so a saved file that calls itself synthetic and then does not carry the exact string SYNTHETIC FIXTURE - NOT GATE EVIDENCE is thrown out (app/src/inspect.ts:292–295). There is no way through by leaving something off.

Lying about the class is closed differently, and more weakly. A saved file that calls itself published-gate-evidence has to quote the frozen Pass A manifest hash — the fingerprint of the signed timetable — and carry no synthetic notice at all (same file, lines 283–287). On its own, that quotation buys nothing, because the answer is public. The frozen hash sits in the source as a plain constant (app/src/inspect.ts:214–215) and is published besides in out/phase4/gate4-report.json under passA.manifestSha256; and the value being checked is simply whatever the file being loaded says. So a forger copies it, and the comparison then matches by construction — for the forgery exactly as for the real thing. The run identifier is no better: the checking function never compares it against the frozen list of runs, and the real identifiers are published to be read anyway.

The check that bites is an earlier one, and it works by making the file say something about itself that it cannot control. Every saved file has to carry its own fingerprint in a field of its own — the app calls the field checkpointSha256. When the app opens the file it fingerprints it independently: to a program a saved file is just a long run of bytes, and hashing them costs nothing. Those two values have to agree. If they do not, the load throws — artifact hash mismatch (same file, lines 243–247), and nothing is displayed.

A forger can pass that check without difficulty: fingerprint your own forgery, write the true answer in the field. And that is the trap closing, not opening. out/phase4/pass-a/gate4a-report.json publishes the fingerprint of every real Pass A run — thirteen of them, at payload.runs[].final.checkpointSha256. A forged crystal is a different file, so its honest fingerprint appears nowhere on that list of thirteen; and it cannot write down a flattering one instead, because the app recomputes the value from the bytes in front of it. The manifest hash matches happily. The file has still been forced to state a fact about itself that anyone can look up and find missing. That is the whole asymmetry, and it is the point of the section: a vague badge cannot be contradicted by evidence, and a fingerprint a file is compelled to compute about itself can be.

Key idea

Every safeguard in this chapter has the same shape: the honest thing is the default, and the dishonest thing requires an explicit lie in a field somebody else checks. That is the difference between a policy and a mechanism.

Who has actually checked any of this

A chapter about honest labelling owes you its own labels, so here they are.

This project has been reviewed constantly — the evidence-class failure above was found by a review round, not by a test — and every recorded non-maker reviewer-agent round is an LLM review. So is the recorded two-reviewer process that checked this page. Maker audits are recorded separately, and the maker is not identified as a domain scientist. The project’s own sharing rules treat the absence of a recorded human domain-expert review as a limitation to declare rather than a box ticked (docs/stretch-sharing-and-investigation.md:239–241). The same passage requires at least one human domain scientist to read any manuscript before submission. No such review is recorded.

That matters here more than almost anywhere, because the failure mode of a machine reviewer is the failure mode this chapter is about: fluent, plausible, internally consistent output nobody has compared against the world. This vocabulary does not fix that. It does something narrower — it makes the gap sayable, so it can be stated alongside the result rather than discovered afterwards.

Still unsettled

Nothing here can force a label to be true. It can force a label to be consistent, and refuse an upgrade with no artifact behind it. Whether the four Types are the right four, and whether three Evidence tiers are enough for a project that will eventually have partial agreements, are both open. The set was fixed in one decision record on one day, and two of the three tiers have never been used: the strings qualitatively supported and quantitatively validated appear nowhere in the application’s source.

One more thing the labels cannot do alone. “Evidence: unvalidated” tells you nobody has checked a number against nature. It does not say whether it was measured in a laboratory, fitted through a curve, chosen to make a picture come out right, or invented because the grid needed a spacing. Wildly different situations, one label — so the project needed a second vocabulary.

Which is chapter 16’s question: where did each of these numbers actually come from, and what changes when one of them turns out to have been chosen so that the answer would come out right?
