# Hook bank — chapters 1–13

Hooks written as **spoken opening lines** (the maker does voiceover). Each carries its payoff
and its chapter, so a script can be built without re-deriving the science. Written
2026-08-04 after reading all thirteen chapters.

Two items are flagged **UNSOURCED** and must be checked before they are spoken — see
[open-questions.md](open-questions.md).

---

## The pattern that emerged

Sorting ~50 candidate hooks produced one dominant shape and three supporting ones.

**Pattern 1 — "it's backwards."** Not one hook type among several: it is the *native grammar
of this whole subject*. Nearly every load-bearing fact in these thirteen chapters is the
inverse of the intuitive answer. That gives one repeatable format — *"You'd think X. It's
the opposite. Here's why"* — which is the most native short-form structure there is, and
here it isn't a gimmick, it's what the science says.

**Pattern 2 — "seeing the unseeable."** The proof beat. Somebody found a way to look at a
thing that cannot be looked at.

**Pattern 3 — "somebody did something absurd."** The person beat. Needs no new animation.

**Pattern 4 — "the honest gap."** Where the data stops and the guess starts. This is the
Trojan-horse payload and the thing almost no other science channel does.

**Suggested rhythm, which falls out for free:** a *backwards* hook opens, a *seeing the
unseeable* beat proves it, and every fourth or fifth piece is an *honest gap* one that
quietly teaches people how to read a graph.

## Pattern 1 — It's backwards

| # | Spoken hook | Payoff | Ch. |
|---|---|---|---|
| 1 | "A snowflake is not frozen water. It was never liquid." | Vapour goes straight to ice — deposition. A frozen raindrop is sleet, and it's a lump. Only the seed droplet was ever liquid, and it's ~1 part in 100,000 of the finished crystal. | 1 |
| 2 | "Water in a cloud doesn't freeze at zero degrees." | Freezing needs somewhere to *start*. Some droplets hold out past −20 °C; pure water reaches nearly −40. What decides is the speck of dust inside. | 1 |
| 3 | "'It's too cold to snow' is real — and it's got nothing to do with cold." | By −20 °C the cloud has spent its liquid droplets: some froze, most evaporated. No liquid water left to feed the crystals. It's running out of fuel. | 1 |
| 4 | **"You see a snowflake's flat faces because they're the *worst* at growing."** | Rough patches catch molecules and race outward until they collide and vanish from the outline. The slow, smooth facets are what's left over. Nothing chose the hexagon — it's the survivor. | 5 |
| 5 | "That perfectly flat face is actually slightly concave." | The corners get more vapour, the centre gets more terrace steps to stick to; the product is constant so the face advances as one sheet. Reaching that balance means dishing inward — by a few molecular layers. You will never see it. | 5 |
| 6 | "The colder it gets, the more spare water there is. And the *less* water there is." | The percentage climbs all the way to 47% at −40 °C. The actual grams peak near −12.3 and collapse. Forty-seven percent of almost nothing is almost nothing. | 4 |
| 7 | "The corners of a snowflake get the most food and are the worst at eating it." | High supersaturation + low attachment at the corners; low supersaturation + high attachment at the centre. Growth rate is the product. That's what holds a facet flat. | 5 |
| 8 | "The middle of a giant snowflake is a fossil." | Once the outer edge starts intercepting the vapour, the centre stops being fed and stops changing. The small hexagon in there is very nearly the whole crystal as it was early on — preserved by starvation. | 4 |
| 9 | "Triangular snowflakes have six sides." | Six edges at ordinary hexagonal angles, alternating long and short. And it's backwards twice: the three you read as the *long* edges are the **slow** ones. The three that won the race shrank into the little truncating corners. | 9 |
| 10 | "Lab-grown snowflakes aren't fake. They're better." | Real snow spends minutes in transit and arrives travel-worn, rounded, partly sublimated. A growth chamber catches the crystal at its peak: razor-sharp facets, a degree of perfection rarely found in nature. | 10 |
| 11 | "A floor that looked 99% empty ruined decades of measurements." | Tiny stray crystals covering 1% of a chamber floor pull the supersaturation down by a **factor of twenty**. Most earlier growth measurements were judged unreliable because of it. | 10 |
| 12 | "A *lower* barrier means a *faster* face." | The barrier to starting a new molecular layer sits in a negative exponent, so a bigger barrier means a smaller sticking probability. Get that sign backwards and you get the whole subject backwards. | 11 |
| 13 | "Colder weather does not make fancier snowflakes." | Temperature decides plate-or-column. *Humidity* decides simple-or-elaborate. Dry air gives you plain blocky crystals however far below freezing you go. | 7 |
| 14 | "The wetter the cloud, the branchier the crystal — and it's still starving." | **CORRECTED 2026-08-05.** An earlier version read "not because they're growing fast" — ch 6's Fig. 3.5 caption says branching is more likely when growth *is* fast, and "the most elaborate crystals fall from the wettest clouds." Both are true: the shortage is **local and self-inflicted**. The crystal eats faster than the air can resupply, so a bump that pokes out reaches richer air and runs away with it. | 6, 7 |

## Pattern 2 — Seeing the unseeable

| # | Spoken hook | Payoff | Ch. |
|---|---|---|---|
| 15 | "You can't photograph humidity. So he photographed where it *wasn't*." | A fog of droplets condenses on the substrate — except in a clean moat around the crystal, where the ice already ate the vapour. The moat's edge is literally a contour line of the humidity field. | 4 |
| 16 | "The humidity sensor turns into a snowflake and eats its own reading." | So you never measure it. You *construct* it out of two thermometers: a frost ceiling sets the supply, the floor sets what the crystal calls "enough," and the gap between them is the answer. | 10 |
| 17 | "He measured a crystal growing a few nanometres per second by counting flickers of light." | Two reflections, one from the top of the ice and one from the bottom, going in and out of step. Every full flicker cycle is one fixed step in thickness. | 10 |
| 18 | "Kepler asked why snowflakes have six sides in 1611. The answer arrived in 1929, on a piece of film." | X-ray diffraction. The lattice is far too small for any microscope, but a crystal acts as a grating and the six-fold symmetry lands on the film in plain sight. **318 years.** | 2, 3 |
| 19 | "This ice column has a groove around its waist. That groove is one wrong layer of bonds." | A twin plane — a single layer of cubic bonds in an otherwise hexagonal stack. It's weaker, so it sublimates faster, and the groove is the only visible tell. | 3, 9 |
| 20 | "Two numbers, measured a century ago, predict the angle of a snowflake nobody had found yet." | The lattice says an arrowhead twin's edges must meet at 88.7°. Somebody put a protractor on a photo of one from the South Pole: **88.5 ± 0.5**. | 9 |
| 21 | "You can test for facets with a lamp." | Flat faces throw light straight back like little mirrors. Rounded, rimed, battered crystals don't. Sparkle is a free optical test — the same physics as the interferometer, done with your eyes. | 10 |
| 22 | "You can *write* on a snowflake." | Drop the humidity for thirty seconds, put it back, and you've planted a permanent hexagonal rib. Do it on a timer and you get a spider web. Being able to write the feature is far stronger evidence than explaining it afterwards. | 8 |

## Pattern 3 — Somebody did something absurd

| # | Spoken hook | Payoff | Ch. |
|---|---|---|---|
| 23 | "A Vermont farmer photographed snowflakes for 46 winters." | Wilson Bentley, first photograph 1885 at nineteen, same home-built camera-on-a-microscope, 5,000+ glass plates. He scraped the emulsion off the backgrounds by hand to make them print black. | 2 |
| 24 | "His book came out in November 1931. He was dead a few weeks later." | Pneumonia, aged 66. | 2 |
| 25 | "Bentley's 5,000 photographs are all real, all honest — and the album is still a lie about winter." | Plates were expensive. He shot ~100 specimens a season and saved them for the photogenic ones. Result: a body of work in which the rare is normal. **Selection bias with no fraud, no exaggeration, no bad intent** — which is what makes it so hard to see from inside. | 2 |
| 26 | "He got tired of waiting for snow, so he built a walk-in freezer and grew his own." | Nakaya, Hokkaido, first synthetic snow crystal 12 March 1936, on a thin fibre. He sat inside the freezer with the experiment, for hours, in a coat and gloves. That's the move from natural history to experimental physics. | 2, 10 |
| 27 | "Put two thousand volts on a frosted wire and ice needles shoot out of it." | Electric ice needles, near −6 °C: 100–150 µm per second, a 3 mm needle in under a minute. They're used as clean single-crystal perches to grow the crystal you actually want to watch. | 10 |
| 28 | "To find out what shape a snowflake really *wants* to be, you'd have to leave one alone for eight days." | That's the settling time for a crystal a tenth of a millimetre across. Nobody has the patience or an apparatus that holds still that long — so the equilibrium shape of ice has never been definitively measured. | 5, 13 |
| 29 | "Most of what we know about snowflakes was paid for on one man's credit card." | He calls it "'forbidden' research, because there is no way any of the usual funding agencies will touch it." No application, no product, no funding. Equipment part-funded by sales of his own popular books. | 13 |

## Pattern 4 — The honest gap

| # | Spoken hook | Payoff | Ch. |
|---|---|---|---|
| 30 | "The most memorable line on this graph isn't a result. Nine words in the caption tell you so." | "*The dotted line was drawn to guide the eye.*" Four kinds of line exist on a science chart — a measurement, a fit, an eye guide, and a formula picked because it was convenient — and papers usually tell you which, in one skippable clause. | 13 |
| 31 | "The numbers that make the theory work were chosen to make the theory work — and he says so in print." | The SDAK dip positions and widths were "judiciously chosen to provide a reasonable explanation for the temperature-dependent behaviors seen in the Nakaya diagram." That is an honest placeholder, and it is labelled as one. | 12, 13 |
| 32 | "He worked out his own mechanism from first principles and it came up five times too small." | The corner-to-facet diffusion picture predicts the barrier dropping to about *half*; the growth experiments imply about a *tenth*. His own verdict: "too simplistic to explain the observed SDAK phenomenon." | 12 |
| 33 | "'I now believe that my earlier analysis was largely incorrect.' — the same guy who published the earlier analysis." | That's what a live field looks like from the inside. Results get withdrawn by the people who published them. | 12 |
| 34 | "There are 7 kinds of snowflake. Or 35. Or 121." | All three numbers are real; none is a fact about snow. They're the sizes of three filing systems. "There is no absolute classification system for snow crystals, and there never will be" — the shape depends on the whole growth history, the way a dog's breed depends on its whole ancestry. | 9 |
| 35 | **"These questions aren't open because they beat ten thousand physicists. They're open because barely anyone has looked."** | "Typically, there are a handful of interested souls around the globe at any given time thinking about this problem." **Difficulty and neglect look identical from the outside, and they are not the same thing.** | 13 |

## The rest — hooks that are simply strong

| # | Spoken hook | Payoff | Ch. |
|---|---|---|---|
| 36 | "Five degrees. That's the whole reason snowflakes have six sides." | A water molecule's bond angle is 104.5°; the perfect tetrahedral angle is 109.5°. That near-miss forces four bonds per oxygen into an essentially tetrahedral arrangement, and everything follows. | 3 |
| 37 | "A ring of five *almost* works." | Flat rings have fixed corner angles: three gives 60°, four gives 90°, five gives 108°, six gives 120°. Folding can only sharpen a ring, never widen it — so it must overshoot 109.5° to close. Five misses by one and a half degrees. Six is the first that makes it. | 3 |
| 38 | "Ice could have been cubic. Same molecules, same bonds, one twist." | And nobody has ever definitively observed pure cubic ice — in the lab or in nature. Samples come out as jumbles of both stackings. | 3, 13 |
| 39 | "The six arms match. Nothing connects them." | "The growth of the six branches is not synchronized by any communication between them, but rather by their common history." Six independent responders, one shared trip through the cloud. | 1, 8 |
| 40 | "You can grow identical-twin snowflakes. The trick is to be violent about it." | Two seeds, one shared schedule. Big abrupt changes drown out the small private differences; gentle gradual ones don't. That's the proof that growth is largely deterministic. | 8, 10 |
| 41 | "'No two alike' depends entirely on what you mean by alike." | Two simple hexagons? Easy — there's a photo of a matching pair. Every molecule? Never: ~1 water molecule in 5,000 carries deuterium, scattered at random, ~10¹⁵ of them per crystal. A *complex* crystal has ~100 features, and 100 things can be arranged ~10¹⁵⁸ ways — about 10⁷⁰ times more than there are atoms in the known universe. | 8 |
| 42 | "The entire shape of a snowflake gets decided on a strip of ice 200 molecules wide." | Real edge radii are 1–2 µm, so the last flat terrace at the rim comes out around 50 nanometres. The broad-facet measurements say thin plates at −15 °C shouldn't exist. "Either something is very wrong with the large-facet measurements, or small facets have a different story to tell." | 12 |
| 43 | "Turn off ice's sense of direction and you don't get a blob. You get seaweed." | With no anisotropy the tip splits, and splits again, forever — just as branched, no shape at all. That's the control experiment for the whole subject: diffusion supplies the branching, the lattice supplies the directions, and a snow crystal needs both. | 6 |
| 44 | "This chemical freezing on a lab bench at room temperature makes the same shape." | Succinonitrile. No water, no cold, no cloud — same parabolic tip, same 60° sidebranches, same stunted neighbours. The branching instability doesn't care what you're made of; it only cares that growth is diffusion-limited. | 6 |
| 45 | "Every snowflake wears an invisible halo of air it has already eaten." | And it re-forms about a thousand times faster than the ice grows — ~50 ms against minutes — so it never lags. It's painted onto the outline, however complicated the outline gets. | 4 |
| 46 | "There are negative snowflakes. Crystal-shaped holes growing *inside* a block of ice." | Pull vapour out of an internal cavity and its walls sublimate outward, faceted. A subtractive sibling of ordinary growth: empty space is what grows. | 4 |
| 47 | "This shape has never fallen on anybody." | Fishbone dendrites need supersaturations that do not occur in the atmosphere. Entirely a laboratory creation. And the reverse is true too: the prettiest crystal in the book, the fernlike stellar dendrite, cannot be grown in the apparatus that takes the prettiest pictures. | 7, 10 |
| 48 | "A snowflake is not a reading of where it was. It's a recording of where it *went*." | The map only works if the weather holds still, and a cloud doesn't. So the crystal never sits on a point — it drags a line. Oldest ice in the middle, newest at the tips, nothing overwritten. | 8 |
| 49 | "A hollow column has two pits in it that match wiggle for wiggle." | Both ends lived through the same conditions at the same times, so they're two independent recordings of one history. And the very centre can never be hollow — there was never a moment when it could have been. | 7 |
| 50 | "Snowflakes remember." | Two crystals in identical air can grow into different shapes because of what happened to them *earlier*: a burst of fast early growth sharpens a facet, moves the crystal onto a different branch of the kinetics, and leaves it there. Hysteresis, not noise. | 12 |
| 51 | "It takes about a hundred thousand cloud droplets to build one snowflake." | And none of that water ever reaches the crystal as liquid. Droplet → vapour → ice, every molecule of it. The crystal is quietly taking its own cloud apart. | 1, 4 |
| 52 | "If ice is clear, why is a snowbank white?" | A heap of transparent fragments scatters light in every direction, exactly like a pile of crushed glass. Whiteness is a property of the heap, not of the ice. | 2 |

## Notes for scripting

- **Hooks 4, 5, 7, 9, 12 are the same idea at four scales.** Don't spend them in one week.
- **Hook 35 is the channel's thesis if it ever needs one.**
- Hooks 15, 16, 17, 22 are the ones that make the science feel like *craft* rather than
  received fact — they pair naturally with any pattern-1 opener as the proof beat.
- Hooks 23–26 need no new animation at all. Cheapest good pieces on the list.
- Hook 41 needs care: the slogan is only true with the word **complex** in it. Dropping that
  word makes it false, and the corpus is explicit about it.
