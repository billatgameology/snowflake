# Question bank — graded by how good the answer is

The maker's proposed format: **a question, then an answer.** "We have a lot of answers, just
not all of it."

Chapters 1–13 support a third beat that almost nobody else in science content does:
**question → answer → *how solid that answer actually is*.** The corpus already carries the
vocabulary for it — chapter 13's learning objective is literally "tell a measurement from a
fitted curve, a fitted curve from a line drawn to guide the eye, and any of those from a
formula chosen because it was convenient," and chapter 15 (Part Two) asks you to "take any
number a program shows you and say what it *is*, separately from what it has *earned*."

**Signature graphic:** the four kinds of line, colour-coded. Recurring, instantly
recognisable, and it teaches scientific literacy without ever announcing that it is teaching.

Tiers below are assigned from what the chapters actually say, read in full 2026-08-04.

## Tier 1 — Settled. The answer is solid and the payoff is complete.

Highest volume, cheapest to make, safest to say. This is the bread.

| Question | The answer | Ch. |
|---|---|---|
| Why do snowflakes have six sides? | The shortest closed ring of oxygen atoms in ice is six. A water molecule's 104.5° bond angle sits close enough to the 109.5° tetrahedral angle that four bonds per oxygen go tetrahedral; a ring must overshoot 109.5° when flat to be able to buckle down to it, and six is the first that does. Answered by X-ray in 1929. | 3 |
| Is a snowflake a frozen raindrop? | No — that's sleet. A snow crystal is built from water vapour, molecule by molecule, with no liquid stage. Only the seed droplet was ever liquid. | 1 |
| Why doesn't a cloud freeze at 0 °C? | Freezing needs somewhere to start. A clean droplet has nothing to offer one, so it stays liquid — supercooled — until a speck inside nucleates it, typically near −10 °C. | 1 |
| Why is it sometimes too cold to snow? | By around −20 °C the cloud's supercooled droplets are gone — some frozen, most evaporated away feeding crystals. No liquid left, no snowfall. It's fuel, not cold. | 1 |
| Where does a snowflake start? | A cloud droplet, 10–20 microns across, that froze. Everything you can see was deposited on it out of the air afterwards; the seed is roughly one part in a hundred thousand of the finished crystal. | 1 |
| Why do snowflakes have arms? | A bump that pokes out of the surface reaches air the crystal hasn't eaten yet, grows faster, pokes out further. Mullins–Sekerka. A perfectly flat growing surface is a valid answer to the equations — just not a stable one. | 6 |
| Why do the six arms match? | Nothing coordinates them. They travelled through the same couple of millimetres of air, so they felt every change at the same instant and each responded on its own. Proven by growing identical twins on demand. | 1, 8 |
| Why *don't* the sidebranches match? | Those are seeded by chance bumps, and chance isn't shared. Two sides of a *single* arm don't match each other. Shared history builds the star; private noise decorates it. | 6, 8 |
| Is it true no two are alike? | Only with the word **complex** in it. Simple hexagons match easily — there's a photograph of a matching pair. The uniqueness comes from counting: ~100 independent features, ~10¹⁵⁸ arrangements. | 8 |
| Why is a snowbank white if ice is clear? | A heap of transparent fragments scatters light in every direction, like crushed glass. Whiteness belongs to the heap, not the ice. | 2 |
| Why are the faces flat? | The flat faces are the *slowest* growers. Fast rough surfaces run outward until they collide with their neighbours and vanish from the outline. Facets are what's left. | 5 |
| What's the difference between snow, sleet, rime and graupel? | Sleet: a raindrop that froze. Rime: cloud droplets that froze *onto* a finished crystal — weight without pattern. Graupel: rime that took over completely. Snow crystal: built from vapour, one molecule at a time. | 1, 9 |
| Is ski-resort snow real snow? | No. It's water blasted through nozzles and frozen — lumps, no facets, no branches. *Laboratory* snow is real snow: same vapour growth, same object, made indoors. | 10 |
| How do you see the humidity around a growing crystal? | You let a fog of droplets condense. They can't form where the crystal already ate the vapour, so the bare moat around it is a contour line of the humidity field. | 4 |
| Why do the arms grow faster than the middle? | The parts that stick out sit closest to untouched air, so the vapour gradient there is steepest, so delivery is fastest. Sheltered interiors get the weakest flow and stop changing. | 4 |

## Tier 2 — Mostly. The mechanism is understood; the numbers are honest fits with real error bars.

| Question | The answer, and where it thins out | Ch. |
|---|---|---|
| Why does a growing tip keep exactly the same shape? | It matches a 1947 mathematical solution — a parabolic needle advancing at constant speed, unchanging. Ride along with it and nothing moves. But diffusion alone doesn't pick *which* parabola; the extra physics that settles it is the attachment kinetics, which "are not well known from independent measurements." A line through the data isn't the same as understanding why. | 6 |
| How sticky is ice? | One number per face: alphaHK = A·exp(−σ₀/σsurf). The functional form is on firm ground — it predates ice research by decades and independent experiments land on it. The σ₀ values are honest fits. Treat them as good to a few tens of percent, not three decimal places; published values for the same barrier disagree slightly. | 11 |
| Why does a warm prism face grow more slowly than it should? | Even with no barrier left, its ceiling A sits well below 1 above about −10 °C. "The short answer is that this is an empirical observation with no obvious physical explanation." The quasi-liquid-layer viscosity story is a cartoon, and is labelled as one by the person who drew it. | 11 |
| What actually falls out of the sky? | Mostly irregular clumps — small, malformed, stuck together. Well-formed stars are rare and heavily over-represented in photographs. There is no absolute classification system, and there never will be, because the underlying variation is continuous. | 9 |
| How thick is the slushy layer on ice? | About one molecular layer near −10 °C, thickening logarithmically toward 0 °C. Real, measured, predicted by Faraday in 1859 — and different experiments probe different properties of it and are hard to compare. | 5 |

## Tier 3 — A hypothesis wearing a lab coat.

| Question | Where it stands | Ch. |
|---|---|---|
| Why is a very narrow facet so much easier to grow on than a wide one? | SDAK — structure-dependent attachment kinetics. Its author calls it "a working hypothesis" and "the only viable option currently available." The dip positions and widths were "judiciously chosen" to match the Nakaya diagram; the formulas' shapes are called "completely ad hoc." Working the mechanism forward from first principles comes up about **fivefold short**. | 12, 13 |
| Why do triangular snowflakes exist? | Three of the six prism facets race ahead, and "the molecular structures of all three of these top prism terraces are identical." A first-principles account of why they diverge "will likely remain an unsolved problem for many decades." | 9, 13 |
| Is the quasi-liquid layer the hidden hand behind everything? | Long suspected, possibly right. "For the present, there seems to be no concrete, well-established physical connection between surface melting and snow crystal growth." A strong lead, not an explanation. | 5, 13 |

## Tier 4 — Nobody knows.

The rarest and most valuable. Don't spend these fast.

| Question | Why it's open | Ch. |
|---|---|---|
| **Why does the shape flip — plate, column, plate, column — as it gets colder?** | Open since the 1930s. The alternation is reproduced across three laboratories and thirty years; nothing about the lattice predicts it, diffusion is ruled out, surface energy is ruled out. The candidate answer is SDAK, and its dips were placed to make the answer come out right. | 7, 12 |
| What does the ice surface look like, molecule by molecule, while it grows? | Nothing has ever imaged it. "Several rather basic aspects of the surface structure and dynamics of ice at the molecular level remain quite mysterious." Everything under that skin is in hand; the skin is the frontier. | 5, 13 |
| Do the two kinds of face really premelt differently from each other? | This is the load-bearing assumption of the leading explanation — and molecular-dynamics simulations "generally see similar premelting behaviors on the two facets." Not confirmed by the best available calculation, not refuted either. | 12 |
| What shape would a snowflake settle into if you just left it alone? | Never definitively measured. One experiment tried, in 1985, and almost certainly never got there: a crystal a tenth of a millimetre across takes about **eight days** to equilibrate. | 5, 13 |
| Does pure cubic ice exist? | Never definitively observed, in the laboratory or in nature — samples always come out as jumbles of both stackings. No faceted "cubic" snow crystal has ever been made, "although this would certainly be an interesting sight." | 3, 13 |
| How hard is it to grow on the third kind of face? | **There is a blank in the data.** Only the basal and prism step energies have ever been measured. The pyramidal one hasn't — so the explanation for why pyramidal facets only appear below −20 °C has to be guessed backwards from the sightings, and is labelled speculative. | 3, 9, 13 |
| Why does a whiff of the wrong vapour rewrite the shape? | Ten parts per million of butyl alcohol turns plates into columns. Observations run from the 1940s to the 2010s. The verdict on all of it: "There is much experimental evidence supporting this general conclusion, and little understanding of any of it." | 5, 13 |

## Why this format is worth the effort

A young viewer who watches fifteen of these picks up, without ever being told, **the
difference between knowing something and guessing well.** That is the real education, and
the snowflakes are the Trojan horse carrying it.

It also solves the honesty problem structurally rather than by disclaimer: a piece that ends
on "nobody knows" is not a weaker video than one that ends on an answer. In this subject it
is usually the stronger one.
